Chapter Fourteen
UNESCO
"I never was politically very active, but when Dwight Eisenhower was running for
president, I became involved in his campaign. He was not on the ballot in Minnesota. I
didn't do very much-I agreed to pay the telephone bill for the write-in campaign.
Although it wasn't much, it identified me with helping them. Future Supreme Court
Justice, Warren Burger, was one of the organizers of the write-in campaign.
"People came to me after Eisenhower was elected President and said, 'Would you like to
be Assistant Secretary of the Army?' and offers like that. I said, 'I really enjoy being at
the University
-no thank you.'
"Then a great lady in Minnesota, Elizabeth Heffelfinger, who was very active in the
United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization, proposed my name as
the nominee for the first official United States representative to the UNESCO executive
board. She was the wife of Peavy Heffelfinger, the son of 'Pudge' Heffelfinger, the
famous Yale football player of the 1880s. I thought that appointment would be fun,
because it was not a full-time commitment. It tied in with academic work, I thought, and
so I took it on and went down to the World Conference at Uruguay in 1954.
ạWhen I arrived in Uruguay, I was quite embarrassed that our State Department's forty-
man delegation, had hired all the big cars they could. The Russians had tried to do the
same thing, of course. The smaller countries had a hard time finding adequate transporta-
tion for their delegates.
"I thought it would be nice if I would drive some of the smaller nations' representatives
up to the Palacio Legislativo, where the meeting was held, in one of the U.S. cars. So
when this great big car drove up with the United States and UNESCO flags on it, I looked
around to see if anybody in front of the Majestic had on peculiar clothing. In those days,
the Burmese Ambassador to UNESCO wore a skirt, while some of the Africans were in
leopard skins. Indians wore the Nehru jackets; and so I'd pick up anybody who was in
strange dress. If they wore ordinary clothes, to hell with them-they were probably
civilians or bureaucrats. One day, I saw two fellows out there, one with a red beanie on
his head, and the other with a black beanie. They also wore what looked like night
dresses, so I beckoned to them and assuming they must be UNESCO, invited them into
the car. My total Spanish at that time, and it hasn't improved much since then, was 'Yo
ambajador de Estados Unidos.' I introduced myself, and this man came back in perfect
English, 'My name is Martini.'
