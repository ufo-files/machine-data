The Experimental City

"I began the study of the Experimental City abow 1962-1963. I decided the only way
to do it was to build an Experimental City and label it by that name, not to call it 'Uto-
pia' or some fancy title. I also wanted to avoid the idea being confused with what the
government was then doing with its model city' concept."
"As I wrote about the Experimental City, I allowed my engineering imagination to rein
free. It was natural for me to think about how to improve the function of city services
through fundamental city planning. The experimental city had to be built, in my opinion,
at least 100 miles from any other great city, because otherwise it would become a mere
bedroom satellite. I was in Minnesota, and I thought Minnesota was a great place.
There's only one great city there, the complex of St. Paul and Minneapolis. Miles from
the Twin Cities was lots of absolutely virgin land.
"The idea was to build the city with plenty of undeveloped land around it, but part of the
concept was also not to allow the city to grow. In other words, to plan it for a specific
population and hold it to that. A major problem of cities is cancerous unplanned growth.
Very few cities are fully planned, only the hearts of cities are planned. L'enfant planned
the streets of Washington, D.C., so a cannon could command all the roads coming in.
Today a single disabled automobile can affect all the roads that come into Washington.
ạThe experimental city, on the other hand, was to be built completely upside down. What
happens when cities grow unplanned is that people move out from the edges, and they
move into more or less undeveloped ground, a few suburban houses and dirt roads. A
certain number of houses are needed before local government puts in sewers, runs in
telephone lines, and provides various other services. The crime rate has to rise before
goverment worries about a police force. Enough children must live out there before
goverment worries about the schools. Therefore, all the essential human services, the
way we do it in the United States and most other places in the world, must lag behind the
given needs. Cities have to wait for human needs to become acute before they can even
make a pitch for these things.
"So I said, 'Let's design a city for a quarter of a million people'-a modest proposal, I
thought. Critics of the Experimental City idea responded, 'A quarter of a million people?
What have you been smoking?'
