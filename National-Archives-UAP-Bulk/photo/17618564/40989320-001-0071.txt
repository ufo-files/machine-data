I. INTRODUCTION
The propagation of sound through the atmosphere is a phenomenon
which has been exploited for a number of military and scientific
purposes. from the military point of vien, development of sound
ranging techniques during Morld War I led to the "spottings of one-
my field artillery positions. The accuracies of gun locations and
the military value of this intelligence proved to be such that sound
ranging continued as an instrument of military observation during
the interim period between Morld Mars I and II. During this period
and well into World War II the Signal Corps at the request of the
Field Artillery, engaged in intensive investigations of the propaga-
tion of sound through the atmosphere, particularly with the and view
of evaluating meteorological effects on sound propagation so as to
increase the accuracy of sound position finding. The salient re-
sults of these investigations have been reported in Signal Corps
publications listed in the attached bibliography. Perhaps the most
characteristic feature of this Simal Corps work 18 that the atno-
sphere was treated in its dynamic state rather than a statistical
state; that is to say, the actual variations of temperature, humid-
ity, wind direction and wind speed were considered in evaluating
the sound paths through the atmosphere. Since the meteorological
state of the atmosphere, as expressed by these parameters, is sub-
ject to radical change with time and location, a technique for the
analysis of the propagation fiald of an atmosphere of variable tom-
perature and wind had to be devised. It is essentially this tech-
nique, extended to exceptionally long ranges, that is reported in
this Appendix.
From the scientific point of view the analysis of sound propa-
gation to long distances can be exploited to determine the meteoro-
logical characteristics oi the upper atmosphere, provided the source
position, detection positions, instant of detonation, and certain
other factors are know to sufficient accuracy. Investigations of
this type have been conducted by Maipple, Gutenberg, and others.
The Signal Corps conducted scientific studies of this type during
the Arco, Idaho explosions of October 1946 (explosions conducted by
the Army-Navy Munitions Board). These studies have led to conclu-
sions which are considered to be of great import to the problem of
long range detection of large explosions. A section of this Appendix
