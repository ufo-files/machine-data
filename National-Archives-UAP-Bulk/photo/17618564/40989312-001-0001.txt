Chapter Four
The University Years
After finishing high school, Athelstan entered the University of Capetown at the remark-
ably tender age of barely sixteen. He enrolled in the engineering curriculum, and studied
mathematics, physics, chemistry and various engineering subjects.
Like most college students Athel had a car, a 1914 vintage Model-T Ford. The Univer-
sity was situated high on the side of the mountain overlooking Capetown, and his car was
equipped with a simple gravity-feed tank that sat above the engine. When Athel traveled
up a steep hill, the gravity feed wouldn't work. With his infinitely logical and practical
mind, he promptly solved the problem the simplest way he could.
Being a 'clever' inventor, I decided that I wasn't going to do anything about it at all no
fuel pumps or any of that nonsense. I simply drove to the bottom of the hill, turned the
car around and backed it all the way up. The gravity feed worked beautifully in reverse.

Athelstan enjoyed Capetown University, where he says he became acutely aware of the
importance of excellent teachers. Although the school was not well funded by modern
standards, academically, Athel found it excellent. The University's fiscal condition kept
it from providing extensive laboratory and vocational facilities to students. Instead,
Capetown made sure its students received an intensive grounding in fundamentals.
ạI went through four normal years at Capetown University. Realizing student engineers
needed practical experience, they insisted we find approved engineering jobs during our
Many civil engineering graduates in Athel's day went into the South Africa Highway
Department. Often, mechanical engineers were hired by railroads as apprentices.
Electrical engineers went to work in the power stations. That was the conventional career
plan for Capetown engineers, but as usual, Athelstan was an exception. He went to sea
alone, because he felt the land was becoming overcrowded. These three themes are
repeated throughout Athel's life - being apart from society, seeking new challenges, and
the sea.
"I began at sea as an officer on the British ship Luxme of the Andrew Weir lines, based
in Scotland. She was a vessel of opportunity that took coal from Natal and traded it for
gunny sacks from Calcutta, as well as teak from Burma, and other cargoes as they came
along. It was a great experience, although I doubt that it broadened my engineering
knowledge. I did write a paper about maintaining diesels at sea, and was very proud of it.
