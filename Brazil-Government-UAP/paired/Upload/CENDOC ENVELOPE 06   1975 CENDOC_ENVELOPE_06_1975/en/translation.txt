Unidentified flying object (UFO) sightings have been reported in various parts of the world throughout history. These reports often describe strange lights, unusual aircraft shapes, or inexplicable phenomena in the sky. While some UFO observations may have natural explanations, many remain unexplained and have fueled speculation about extraterrestrial life and visits.

The term "UFO" is used to refer to any aerial phenomenon that cannot be immediately identified or explained. It does not imply that the object is of extraterrestrial origin. Reports of UFOs range from ancient texts to modern-day sightings, with varying degrees of detail and credibility.

Many countries have established official reporting mechanisms for UFO sightings, encouraging witnesses to come forward and provide information. These reports are then investigated by specialized teams or government agencies to determine their origin and significance.

The study of UFOs, or astrobiology, explores the possibility of life beyond Earth and the potential for extraterrestrial intelligence. While the existence of extraterrestrial life remains a mystery, the investigation of UFO reports contributes to our understanding of the universe and the search for answers to profound questions about our place in the cosmos.

While Jacobs is concerned with the general question of the existence of UFOs, there are other more limited parts of the problem. One area that has figured prominently in flying-saucer lore is the Bermuda Triangle. This area of the Atlantic Ocean, in the triangle between Puerto Rico, Bermuda and Florida, has been the subject of sailors' stories and fears for hundreds of years. About 40 ships and 20 aircraft have been lost there during the past 150 years; some disappeared without a trace. In other cases, crews vanished without an obvious cause from apparently sound ships. A theory developed that this is the operating area not of visitors from outer space but of inhabitants of inner space: a people living underground who venture out in their submarine and aerospace vehicles. Ivan Terence Sanderson advanced this theory in Invisible Residents². There is also another theory that in past ages an advanced people visited Earth and left a homing-type device on the Atlantic seabed, beyond the coastal shelf, to guide future space visitors to Earth.

These two ideas are very frequently considered seriously and accepted without criticism, as in Charles Berlitz's The Bermuda Triangle and John Wallace Spender's Limbo of the Lost³.

Lawrence David Kusche, in The Bermuda Triangle Mystery—SOLVED,† undertakes a critical evaluation of this problem, which has become one part, albeit the smaller one, of the overall question of beings not from Earth versus terrestrial beings. Mr. Kusche is a librarian, not a historian, and his work does not have the same scholarly character as Dr. Jacobs's. Whatever the source of activity, the myth concerning the Bermuda Triangle has "grown" like "Topsy." Those wishing to believe have adopted an eclectic approach, shaping — or, more precisely, reshaping — the stories of disappearances to fit preconceptions. This misrepresentation is what interests Kusche. First he presents the overall myth. Then, in individual chapters, he presents the myth of each disappearance. In each case the myth is followed by the facts as known. Here Kusche supplies the sources of his information. In many examples the adaptation of facts to fit the legend is immediately apparent.

In other cases the revisions have been subtler — a quotation out of context here, a small incorrect chronological statement there. For example, in August 1963 two Air Force KC-135 tankers collided in midair southwest of Bermuda. Debris from these aircraft was found the following day. On the next day more debris was found 160 miles away, but it was quickly identified as having no connection with the two aircraft. Those inclined to make the collision a mystery would conveniently ignore the fact that the wreckage in the two areas was not related in any way. Instead, they would ask how, if the two tankers collided, the debris could be so far apart. The Air Force knew from the first day of the search that a midair collision had occurred. Yet the myth quotes unidentified "authorities" scratching their heads and declaring that "something very strange is happening there." A logical examination of the actual facts leads one to believe that these "authorities" must have been the local dogcatchers in Yeehaw Junction, Florida.

Another incident cited in support of the legend involved an Air Force C-124 that "disappeared" on a flight to "Ireland." The legend says this occurred in March 1950, but the Air Force did not lose any C-124 in the Atlantic that month. In fact, the reference seems to concern an aircraft that crashed in March 1951 on its way to England — far north of the Triangle. More importantly, the aircraft did not disappear. Within 24 hours of its crash — or midair explosion — debris was sighted by an Air Force B-29. The aircraft carrier Coral Sea found the ocean full of debris, some of which its crew recovered and which was identified as coming from the C-124. The waves were rough and storm winds were blowing in the area, making any chance of recovering bodies impossible.

Perhaps the best point about the Bermuda Triangle "mystery" was made by a US Navy officer quoted in Time magazine in January 1975. He pointed out that the heavily traveled triangle between the Sabre Islands, the Azores and Iceland had far more unexplained disappearances than the Bermuda Triangle⁴. Nevertheless, as Kusche emphasizes, the Bermuda Triangle is a manufactured mystery. It arose "because of careless research and was elaborated and perpetuated by writers who deliberately or ignorantly used misconceptions, faulty reasoning and sensationalism." Constant repetition then lent the legend an aura of authenticity. Through his careful research, Kusche has exposed the elements of error and falsehood surrounding the "Bermuda Triangle" myth.
