4.3.0. PHOTOS WITH FLASH
  4.3.1. GUIDE NUMBER AND POWER
      When purchasing a flash, there are six things to consider:
      - The illumination power of the flash is given in units of BCPS.
      - Consult the manuals for flash photography, as there is a fundamental concept: the guide number. This is a value that depends on the flash and the film sensitivity.
      - Manuals typically provide a table of guide numbers versus film sensitivity.
      - If the table only lists guide numbers for black-and-white films, there is no need for concern.
      - For color films, simply divide this number by the film's speed.
      - For example, if the guide number for a 100 ASA black-and-white film is 44, for a 200 ASA color film, the guide number would be 22.
      - The function of a guide number is to determine the aperture of a diaphragm, based on the distance. Divide the guide number by the distance to the object and you get, approximately, the appropriate aperture.

5.    1. 1. INTRODUCTION

      Those who have silver jets at home know perfectly well that they tend to oxidize. Silver forms two oxides: (AgO), which is black or brown, and silver oxide II (Ag2O), which is light or dark gray. In nature, the latter is less frequent and the former is the one with lower oxidation state. If the conditions of our atmosphere were more oxidizing, silver oxide II would form more easily. However, our atmosphere contains many reducing elements that inhibit or even reverse oxidation processes. Among them, we can cite nitrogen, carbon monoxide, hydrogen, etc. These, along with pollutants such as carbon particles, mineral and vegetable dust, and various gases, are strong reducers.

In general, they slow down or even annul any oxidizing chemical attack.

      Monovalent silver compounds have a long time to transform into bivalent compounds if the environment is oxidizing; if the environment is reducing, they tend to transform into monovalent compounds.

      Reactions are extremely dependent on light action. If we expose different silver objects to light and darkness, we will see that the first darkens much more quickly than the second.

      However, this property is not exclusive to oxides.

      The birth of photography was based on the following idea: it was possible to produce graphic images of reality from the least amount of chemical photolysis, especially of silver compounds.

         The first process to achieve success only produced an image on copper plates, known as the daguerreotype (named after Louis Daguerre, the French inventor who created it). This process allowed the documentation of reality for the first time. Thanks to it, we now know how famous figures' anatomies looked, and even distant ones like Chopin, Wagner, Pedro I, etc.

       The daguerreotype essentially consisted of the following: light passed through a lens system that "exposed" a photosensitive metal plate. A revealing bath made the clear parts of the image appear light, and the dark parts dark. It was a positive reproduction.

      The need for a photographic process that would allow the production of multiple copies of the same scene or image led to the development of photography as we know it today: a positive reproduction from negatives.

       Theoretically, the process is simple: light passes through a lens system and exposes a transparent screen covered on both sides by a gelatine emulsion of silver salts. An image is then formed. What was clear is recorded as dark; what was clear is recorded as clear. The screen, in the form of plates or rolls, becomes the negative of the photograph. After a developing and fixing bath, a paper print can now be produced. A photographic paper (also covered by a gelatine emulsion of silver salts) is placed under the negative. The image is then illuminated quickly. The light, upon reaching the dark areas, is absorbed and blocked; upon reaching the transparent areas, it passes through them and exposes the paper. After a developing process, we see that the parts covered by the dark areas of the negative become clear, and the parts covered by the transparent areas become dark.
