**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions. Through new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named CHARLEVILLE-MEZIERES (08) 1982 is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases.

On January 17, 1982, at 1:30 AM, two witnesses observed from their homes a bright orange glowing ball on the ground in a field, illuminating everything around it. The phenomenon was described as rising from the ground, leaving a colored gaseous layer. The next day, the witnesses visited the presumed location of the PAN, but found no traces on the frozen and snowy ground. The details reported in the incident report (PV) are vague, and it is impossible 35 years later to contact the witnesses to enrich or clarify these details.

Despite this, based on the experience gained by the GEIPAN, characteristic elements allow for a very probable hypothesis for this observation—the observation of the Moon. The PAN was stationary for a relatively long duration (around 15 minutes in this case). The described shape was "a huge orange ball with intense light." The Moon was indeed in the direction observed by the witnesses, and they did not notice it. While we do not have the exact address of the witnesses, they stated their windows faced east, and the Moon was indeed present at 1:30 AM at an azimuth of 102° (nearly due east) and a low altitude of 2.3°—which typically gives a reddish or orange hue. The low altitude might have led the witness to believe the ball was about 300 meters away, on a hill. The light was so intense that they could not see what was around it. The light illuminated the entire neighborhood. The witness stated, "For me, this ball was on the ground."

The other witness, T2, described a scenario more consistent with an PAN on the horizon rather than against a landscape background (doubtful due to T1's account): "I saw an object rising from the ground. I didn't see its shape, but I noticed a gaseous layer of gray-white color with an orange center that gradually diminished. The gray layer took over. After this phenomenon, the object left. We went to bed."

The source of confusion is the failure to recognize the Moon. This often occurs due to clouds or fog partially obscuring the Moon. T2's description ("like a gaseous layer of gray-white color with an orange center that gradually diminished") strongly suggests this was the case. The cloud layer can sometimes reveal the Moon in various shapes, as T1 noted, "When it was moving away, I noticed it looked like what is called a flying saucer," or a blurred or shapeless outline when the cloud covers but does not block the moonlight.

Many witnesses reported the PAN finally moving, sometimes very quickly. This is the illusion of distance escape. The dimming of the Moon due to increased cloud cover is logically interpreted by the witness as the PAN escaping into distance (which would have a constant constant light intensity) and is even more plausible when the cloud gap in the Moon narrows, causing the angular size of the PAN to decrease. Here, T1 did not explicitly mention an angular movement in the sky, saying only, "It left in the direction of SEDAN." Around 1:45 AM (the observation duration is not specified, but it can be assumed that by the time T2 gets up and this strange phenomenon is observed, at least 15 minutes have passed), the Moon is at 105° azimuth, and the direction of SEDAN is centered around 128°. It is quite plausible that the witness described the distance escape (in the Moon's direction) by approximating it to the direction of SEDAN, especially if the witness does not know the exact direction of SEDAN (off by 20°, which is not a significant error) or lacks a more precise way to indicate the Moon's direction to the authorities (conveniently, the only geographic landmark in that direction seems to be the village of Fleigneux, as far from the incident site as SEDAN). Additionally, T2 did not mention a final direction of movement.

We also note that over 15 minutes, the Moon's altitude increased by 3°, which aligns with T2's observation if he observed for a sufficient duration ("I saw an object rising from the ground") and 3° to the right, potentially leading T1 to believe the PAN was already moving towards SEDAN, assuming a sufficient observation duration and that T1 distinguished between the PAN's direction and that of SEDAN (the most likely scenario is that T1 associated the PAN's direction with that of SEDAN due to misidentification or convenience).

Considering all these elements, the most likely hypothesis is the observation of the Moon. It cannot be definitively confirmed because we do not have the exact addresses of the witnesses, and we cannot confirm the absence of physical obstacles between the witnesses' position and the Moon's (although generally, the relief does not present any significant obstacles beyond 100 meters, which cannot be verified). Similarly, while the PV mentions a clear sky, it is not possible to assure the absence of a total cloud cover blocking the Moon (as no meteorological data are available).

Therefore, the GEIPAN classifies this case as B: probable observation of the red Moon.
