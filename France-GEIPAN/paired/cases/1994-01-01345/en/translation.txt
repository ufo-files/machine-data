Summary: Aircraft encountering a phenomenon confirmed by radar control. Instantaneous disappearance: unexplained case consistent with a strong sense of strangeness.
--- seg-ef90ded42731465119df80b7 [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On January 28, 1994, at 1:14 PM, the crew of an Airbus A320-11 operated by Air France flying between Nice and London witnessed an unexplained phenomenon. Overflying the Coulommiers region in Seine-et-Marne, the chief steward, present in the cockpit, reported a phenomenon to the captain that resembled a weather balloon. The captain and co-pilot both observed a dark brown or dark red disk that changed shape before suddenly disappearing. The captain reported the incident to Reims air traffic control, who confirmed there was no air traffic in the area. Simultaneously, an unidentified radar track was recorded by the radar at Cinq-Mars-la-Pile. This track, recorded for 50 seconds, intersected the flight path of AF3532 but did not match any filed flight plan and disappeared at the same time from both the crew's view and the radar screens.

Three years later, after the case was publicized, witnesses came forward and provided official statements to the gendarmerie and the SEPRA (Service d'Expertise des Phénomènes rares aérospatiaux au CNES). Investigators remained cautious about the correlation between the radar signature and the phenomenon. While the radar signature did not match the observation, the phenomenon could not be an airplane because there would have been a corresponding radar trace. In 2012, a new verification was requested from Meteo-France to explore the possibility of a weather balloon (see attached). A weather balloon did indeed cross the plane's trajectory, but an hour before the observation. At the time of the observation, the balloon, having burst, should have been descending far to the southeast of the plane. Thus, the case remains unexplained, with consistent testimonies for a most unusual observation (see the investigation report).

Two hypotheses can still be considered but are impossible to confirm: an unknown atmospheric optical phenomenon or a flexible object (e.g., a balloon envelope) drifting with the winds. This case is classified as D1: a moderately consistent, highly strange unexplained case. Late testimonies, the absence of the steward's testimony, and the inconsistent radar track weaken the case's consistency.
