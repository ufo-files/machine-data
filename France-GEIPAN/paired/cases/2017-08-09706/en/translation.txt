Description: On August 4, 2017, between 1:55 PM and 2:10 PM, a witness was intrigued by an object at the altitude of commercial airline cruising flights. The witness, using binoculars, observes a vertical cylinder in the sky. The upper third of the cylinder is red-orange, while the lower two-thirds are white, until a large cloud coming from the southwest obscures it. Two planes pass practically vertically below the observation, one coming from the West going towards the East, the other coming from the Southeast going towards the Northwest. Once the cloud dissipates, the observed object is no longer there. The witness thinks it's a drone, although the object's altitude is high. No other testimony is collected. The strangeness perceived by the witness seems to reside in the stationary nature of the object, its shape, and its disappearance. Stationarity is difficult to assess. The witness states that the cloud came to cover the PAN, the relative movement between the cloud and the plane could also be due (at least in part) to the movement of the PAN towards the cloud. It is classic for a commercial jet to appear as a cylinder or cigar without wings. Sunlight illumination quite consistently finds reflective conditions on a cylindrical or rounded surface (cockpit, airplane nose) while reflections on wings are indeed much stronger (larger mirror surface) but extremely fleeting (all or nothing). The perception of verticality of the cylinder is entirely subjective, if the aircraft moves within the plane containing the witness's line of sight, the fuselage appears vertical. If this craft is transverse to the line of sight, the fuselage appears horizontal. A high-altitude aircraft, without a trail behind it, can be distinguished for a moment due to the reflection of sunlight on the aircraft, and become invisible a little later due to the evolution of that same reflection. And as soon as it disappears from the naked eye, it becomes impossible to re-fix binoculars on it. It should be noted that the witness is located in an area with high aeronautical traffic at all altitudes (at least during this time of day) and that observing this traffic in the sky has already led to feelings of extreme strangeness, resulting in a GEIPAN report (EMERAINVILLE (77) 06/05/2017). Once it is verified (here through aeronautical context analysis) that the observation itself does not report anything strange, the GEIPAN does not dedicate more resources to identifying a more precise aircraft hypothesis (exactly which aircraft) as its probability is significant. As a result, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: Aircraft.
