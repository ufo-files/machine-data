--- seg-6fca931de634755eb19bab52 [needs-review] ---
Description: On December 26, 2018, at 06:45, a witness, having breakfast in their living room, was intrigued by the sight of an object moving in the sky. He thinks it's a satellite, but its size surprises him. The witness steps outside and observes an object with four bright points suggesting a square shape. The object moves from South/Southwest to East without any particular noise being heard. It moves on a straight trajectory but starts to "waver" then makes a long turn before being lost from view. The witness very likely observed a pass of the International Space Station (ISS). He thought he saw a satellite "but it was bigger." Indeed, the ISS does appear much larger and brighter than a typical satellite. We verify that the ISS indeed has a trajectory consistent with the description of Southwest to East and within the exact temporal range of the observation on that morning. See the attached trajectory log obtained from www.calsky.com. The described aspect corresponds to many testimonies received at GEIPAN. (See the site GEIPAN www.geipan.fr with case search by keyword ISS, also see the explanatory video A satellite or the International Space Station (ISS)). Some of these testimonies GEIPAN mention, as in this case, a curvature of the trajectory. The fact is that the trajectory, as seen by the witness, of a satellite that does not pass vertically is indeed curved. Additionally, the ascent to the highest point (here 40°, but the witness perceives it as "almost overhead") lends itself to poor perceptions of the trajectory, as the higher the object, the less sensitive the witness is to its positioning and its evolution in their basic reference points of North, East, South, West. The perceptions of oscillation on the trajectory are also classic and correspond to the known perception phenomenon called autokinetic (see the following article: The Autokinetic Effect, which occurs when observing a bright point in the darkness. A peculiarity here is that the witness distinguishes several distinct bright points, exactly 5. The angular separation capability (distinction between two points) of the human eye is of the order of 1/60th of a degree, or 1/3600th of a radian. For an object at 400 km distance, the points could only be distinguished if their separation is greater than 100m. Outside the station has a wingspan of approximately 100 meters, which theoretically does not allow for the distinction of points inside. But the separation capability is a complex notion that depends on the witness (who may have a capability beyond the average) and the radiometric contrast, which is very strong here as these lights are seen against a black background. A factor of two or three better than this standard capability explains the observation of several points (more reflective on the station). The fact that the station was at that time accompanied by four other resupply satellites (see the satellite log attached) does not allow for an explanation of these 5 points, as the satellites were then docked to the station (except perhaps one at a given moment) and this does not increase the overall size of the object. The above peculiarity is not totally explained, but it does not contradict the hypothesis. Finally, the space station is then almost as bright as VENUS. It is hardly possible that the witness observed a PAN other than the ISS, without also distinguishing the ISS, which would then be in close proximity to the PAN and evolving in a similar manner as the latter at the same time. As a result, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: very probable observation of the International Space Station (ISS).
