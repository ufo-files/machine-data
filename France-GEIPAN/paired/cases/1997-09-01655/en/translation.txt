**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions. Through new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. The observation case previously classified as D, also known as BAGNOLS SUR CEZE 1997, is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases.

On September 9, 1997, at 9:30 PM, the witness, located on the Chemin des Malins in SAINT-GERVAIS (30), spotted a glowing ball rising from the hill of Fontesquière, pausing, varying in brightness, before disappearing. On September 11, 1997, at 9:30 PM, the same witness, on the Chemin des Abels in SAINT-GERVAIS (30), observed a ball rising above the Cazelle rock, pausing, taking a horizontal trajectory towards the West, and disappearing. Approximately thirty minutes later, at 10:00 PM, the witness (who did not specify their position) saw another ball in the hills of Cristolle. This ball rose from the ground and disappeared. The witness described each observation as an orange glowing ball with an apparent diameter of 8 to 10 centimeters. No other testimonies were collected. No ground traces were found. The atmospheric conditions, as reported by the gendarmerie, were very good (a very clear, starry night with no wind).

The gendarmerie investigation indicates that the night of September 9 was illuminated by a fireworks display in BAGNOLS-SUR-CEZE, marking the end of a votive festival. The witness made no distinction in the appearance of the PAN between the three observations (September 9 at 10:00 PM, September 11 at 9:30 PM, and 10:00 PM). Four hypotheses are considered: a runaway child's balloon, a captive balloon that escaped, a Thai lantern, or an aircraft (as detailed in the investigation report). The retro-reflection of urban ground lights is believed to explain the luminous nature of these balloons. The festive nature of these balloons, especially a captive balloon several meters in diameter, is only compatible with the observation on September 9 (the festival's closing night).

The balloon hypothesis cannot apply to September 11, especially as the areas overflown are less conducive to ground retro-lighting. Therefore, the viable hypotheses are: an aircraft or lantern on September 11, and an aircraft, lantern, child's balloon, or captive balloon on September 9. Considering that the witness did not mention any differences in appearance between the observations, it is assumed that the probability of them not noting any differences between these four hypotheses of such distinct nature is zero. Thus, we seek a common hypothesis for both the lantern and aircraft observations.

If we were to limit ourselves to the observation on September 9, the child's balloon hypothesis would also be eliminated. Retro-reflection of ground lights does not explain the perception of a ball for a child's balloon seen from such a distance on September 9. This difficulty in explanation is lessened for a captive balloon that is larger, but its probability of presence is lower on the festival day and even less so outside the festival. The aircraft hypothesis could only correspond to a light aircraft or military plane, or a helicopter, flying so low and close, but the single (orange) light is not compatible with (does not match) either observation, let alone all three.

The release of Thai lanterns seems, in terms of appearance and trajectory, compatible with the first two observations (September 9 and 11) and the limited information from the last observation on September 11. The main hesitation regarding the lantern hypothesis is that Thai lanterns were rare in France in 1997 and only became popular starting in 2005 or 2007. However, tourists who bought them during a summer trip to Asia might have wanted to make a spectacle with them upon returning from their vacation. The pioneering and sensational aspect of this festive practice on September 9 could have encouraged them to repeat the experience the following favorable night (September 11) for the pleasure of the initiator and a few spectators already impressed or intrigued by the discovery on September 9.

**Consistency:** The witness is unique, providing a fairly precise description of the locations and trajectories, except for the third observation. Given three successive observations, it is regrettable that the gendarmerie did not have the witness explicitly describe each observation to facilitate the expression of any differences in perception. While the lantern hypothesis can account for the descriptions, its probability is hindered by its rarity in 1997. The repetition two days later, outside the festival night, leads to additional hypotheses, though they are still plausible (the temptation to repeat such an innovative experiment), they further reduce the probability. In the end, the consistency is too marginal to support such an hypothesis, even if it remains plausible. These two factors also contradict maintaining the case in the PAN unexplained category.

Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as C and concludes: lack of reliable information.
