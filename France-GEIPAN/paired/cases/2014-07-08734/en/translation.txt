Summary: Observation of erratic movements of bright points in a night sky: probable observation of satellites and visual illusion (micro-nystagmus).
--- seg-d04448180bc8db52524ea48f [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On July 19, 2014, at 11 PM, a group of people were stargazing in the mountains. The only witness reports seeing a bright point with a wavering trajectory, then other points moving in a strange manner. Some meet to merge. Several indications from the testimony point to an explanation: - Punctual appearance of all PAN - Predominantly white color. - Linear trajectory. - Disappearance and reappearance a little further along during movement. - Erratic movements according to "small-radius curves". - Acceleration over short distances. - Sudden stops. The first four points are characteristic of satellites observed at night. The observation conditions were then ideal for observing a maximum number: no Moon, high altitude, no or little light pollution, summer sky... In addition, the observation time is also an important factor in this regard, as most satellites are visible at the beginning of the night or in the early morning, when they are still illuminated by the sun and therefore not in the Earth's shadow cone. The following three points are related to a physiological natural phenomenon of micro-eye mobility called "micro-nystagmus," which is little known by the general public. This occurs when one fixes objects, particularly at night. It translates to:

It gives the false impression that discrete, glowing objects exhibit small, erratic movements, such as zigzags or brief stops, when they are in motion. Detailed physiological explanations can be found in the middle of the page on the forum-ovni-ufologie . This phenomenon occurs particularly when the PAN crosses other intermittent glowing objects during its movement. Thus, the eye, in the presence of a new light source, tries to adapt its night vision in the best way possible by "focusing" more on these new lights. The extraocular muscles alternately and rapidly place the center of the retina (fovea) on and beside light sources. The brain subsequently misinterprets the information sent by the optic nerve from the retina as "zigzags" or "stops." Note: The testimony of other observers could not be obtained. As a result, the GEIPAN classifies the case as B: probable observation of satellites.
