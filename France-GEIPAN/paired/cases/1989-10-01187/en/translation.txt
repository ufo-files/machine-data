Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or explicit changes in classification in an investigation note. This observation case previously classified as D and named PRIVAS (07) 1989 is part of a recent batch of cases that have been reassessed. On October 14th, between 5:40 and 7:30 a couple was intrigued by a peculiar light phenomenon in the sky: the witness did not recognize it as a star, airplane, or weather balloon. The object was described as oval, very bright, and about the size of a dessert plate. Initially stationary, the object appeared to rotate at one point. A second small object, accompanied by a glow and detaching from PAN, descended vertically and then ascended towards the first object. The entire phenomenon disappeared towards the South. A gendarmerie investigation and a press article attached to the report indicate that two other witnesses observed the same phenomenon, which lasted uncommonly long. These other witnesses did not give a statement to the gendarmerie. A revisit of the case 30 years after the events highlights its low consistency. It is a single-witness case with imprecise or contradictory data, without any on-site investigation or meeting with the witness (see the investigation report). Under these conditions, it is difficult to conclude with certainty about the nature of PAN observed by the witness that day. Although most elements of the testimony and deductions from it suggest a simple astronomical confusion (particularly with Jupiter), too many data points are missing (e.g., no elevation indication), or are imprecise or contradictory (east or west azimuth, clear or cloudy sky). An on-site investigation at the time could have clarified some points, especially regarding the presence or absence of a natural obstacle that could have explained why the witness did not notice the full moon and Sirius. Indeed, the sky was very likely clear, and these two stars are at ~15° and ~30° elevation. The strangest element, from an astronomical explanation perspective, is the witness's mention of a "small object accompanied by a white glow, detaching from the larger one, then ascending back towards it." However, in the absence of precise data and an on-site investigation, various explanatory hypotheses are possible, but they cannot be confirmed or refuted. For example:

- The probable presence of an obstacle hiding the moon (tree, cloud) could have caused several moon slits in this obstacle while the rest of the observation showed only one dominant slit.
- We do not know the witness's visual acuity or if they had any visual problems (which ones?), possibly related to fatigue or prolonged sustained observation.
- We do not know if the observation was continuous or discontinuous. When did the witness observe this phase? Right after a break in their observation? In that case, this "small object and white glow" could refer to an entirely different phenomenon, such as an aeronautical one, observed spatially and temporally concomitantly with Jupiter.

The consistency of the testimony is too low to validate the explanation or the inexplicable nature of the only strange element of the observation (which, otherwise, would be classified as an observation of stars). Consequently, GEIPAN classifies the case as C: the lack of reliable data does not allow for the exploitation and processing of this observation case.
