Description: The witness, while traveling on Highway A62 between Bordeaux (33) and Toulouse (31), observed an unidentified aerial phenomenon (PAN) at the height of Saint-Médard-d'Eyrans (33) between 07:10 and 07:20. The PAN caught the witness's attention due to its intense brightness. In 5 to 10 seconds, this brightness diminished, revealing a gray metallic sphere, apparently stationary. The witness lost sight of the object after a total observation estimated between 20 and 30 seconds. Only one testimony was collected. The strangeness and consistency* of this case are considered average: only one testimony collected, no photo of the PAN. The initial description of the PAN is perfectly consistent with the hypothesis of a reflection, whether it be a weather balloon (as suggested by the witness), an airplane, or even a simple fancy balloon. (*According to the criteria of GEIPAN, consistency is the amount of reliable information collected for a testimony). The investigation ruled out the hypotheses of a weather balloon and an airplane as it was confirmed that no weather balloon was in flight in the region at the time of observation. Furthermore, no other airplane than the one observed by the witness was present in the area at that time (see the investigation report). The description of the PAN is, however, very consistent with that of a fancy balloon, due to its spherical shape and its very bright light that fades to reveal a gray metallic color. This balloon could be made of Mylar, a highly reflective aluminumized material commonly used for various types of fancy balloons. Since the sun was rising in the northeast at the time of observation, while the PAN was located to the southeast, it is quite plausible that a strong reflection of light temporarily occurred on the balloon's surface. This reflection could have faded as the geometric alignment between the sun, the witness, and the balloon became less favorable. Indeed, the witness being in rapid motion on the highway not only alters the visibility of the balloon relative to the sun but also prevents a correct assessment of its possible motion due to the parallax effect. The PAN was quickly lost from view, preventing the witness from assessing its actual motion. Therefore, we cannot confirm whether it was moving with or against the wind. Helium-filled balloons can remain airborne for several days, even at night, after being released or lost. They can thus travel long distances, carried by the wind. However, the investigation did not allow for determining the location from where this balloon could have been lost. The case is classified as "B", probable observation of a fancy balloon.
