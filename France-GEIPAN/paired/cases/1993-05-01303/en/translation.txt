Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. The case of observation previously classified as D and named PANTIN (93) 30.05.1993 is part of a recent batch of cases that have been reassessed (see the investigation report). On May 30, 1993, at 4:15 a.m., a witness, returning home after a night out, observed from her balcony an orange rectangular shape in the sky of PANTIN. The shape is estimated to be about 1 meter in size and appears stationary for 15 minutes before slowly moving, changing direction, and moving away to disappear on the horizon. The witness observes a flickering red light but no sound is heard. On the same balcony, at 4:30 a.m., the witness observes a second shape, this time circular and white, not blinding, that is stationary. The size of this disk is also estimated to be approximately 1 meter. The witness continues to observe this second phenomenon until 6 a.m., when the PAN disappears very slowly. In fact, there are two observations of two distinct PANs. Initially, it is likely the landing lights of an aircraft in a VFR night approach on one of the two Paris airports located in the observer's line of sight. Aircraft landing lights can indeed be seen from very far away, as demonstrated in an example on the GEIPAN website in the case "EZE (06) 16.05.2014," where such an aircraft was observed at a distance of approximately 36 kilometers from the witness, and in another example, "FONTRAILLES (65) 31.10.2017," where the aircraft's lights are visible and nearly stationary during the day from 100 kilometers away for over 10 minutes. The distance prevents the witness from distinguishing anything other than these powerful lights, whose color and brightness blend with the red anti-collision light located under the aircraft's fuselage, giving the "orange rectangle" shape noted by the witness. The eastward direction of movement, the low angular elevation above the horizon, the observation of a flickering red light after the aircraft pivots on its axis, positioning it face to the runway, the slow movement, and the absence of sound support the hypothesis. In a second instance (see case PANTIN (93) 30.05.1993 T2), the duration of the observation, the slow movement of the PAN in the sky, its circular shape and white color, as well as its disappearance due to the clouds present in large numbers that night, all support the hypothesis of a confusion with Venus, rising in the east and moving slowly towards east-southeast while gaining altitude. The witness is likely not accustomed to observing the sky at this late hour of the night and is unfamiliar with these astronomical phenomena. She scanned the sky probably due to the attention or concern sparked by the first observation, which is the main observation to remember. The consistency is average and benefits mainly from the identification with buildings, partially compensating for the absence of angular measurements taken by the gendarmes during their on-site investigation. This case PANTIN (93) 30.05.1993 T1 is classified as B: probable observation of an aircraft.
