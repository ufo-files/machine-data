Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over many years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named SAINT-RAPHAEL (83) 1979 is part of a recent set of cases that have been reassessed (see the investigation report). On July 13, 1979, at 1:10 PM, two witnesses (T1 and T2) working at the lookout post on Mont Vinaigre in the Esterel massif in the commune of Fréjus (83) were intrigued by the movements of a very bright spherical object in the landscape. During their depositions at the gendarmerie, a sketch was made showing the successive movements of this object (see the gendarmerie protocol). T2 used binoculars and described an object descending vertically from a cloud, moving down the hill in jerks, jumping from one hill to another, making stops, and finally rising and disappearing into a cloud. No other testimony was collected. The radar services contacted by the gendarmerie detected nothing. The weather conditions on the day and at the time of the observation are a key factor in understanding and explaining the complex movements of the PAN, which formed the main strangeness of the case. These conditions, namely a maximum wind speed of 34 knots with turbulent air flow in the area, occurred in a rugged terrain area. However, a minimal wind speed of 10 to 15 knots is enough for a lighter-than-air balloon, such as a nylon metallized model (often called a "Mylar balloon"), to follow an irregular horizontal and vertical movement. The impossibility (for any witness) of accurately perceiving the distance of an unknown phenomenon can lead to overestimating distances and, consequently, movement speeds based on angular movements (the only ones actually perceived by the eyes), thus increasing the strangeness of the perceived phenomenon (a rapid jump from one hill to another, a smart stop above the national road). These Mylar balloons can be inflated with air but are most often filled with helium and can then fly for between two weeks and two months, carried by local winds. Such balloons have existed since the late 1970s and are often circular or oval in shape, sometimes with a fanciful design. The visibility of this type of balloon is enhanced by its aluminum envelope, which strongly reflects sunlight; its shape is only really discernable at a shorter distance. Furthermore, this strong reflection of sunlight, coupled with the observation distance, does not allow for distinguishing either the possible fanciful design or the appendage that enables inflation. Consequently, GEIPAN classifies the case as B: probable observation of a Mylar balloon.
