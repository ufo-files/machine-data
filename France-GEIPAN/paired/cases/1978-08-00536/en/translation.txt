**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and the investigative experience gained over the past years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases. On August 20, 1978, starting at 10:50 PM, the attention of a military personnel is drawn to the presence in the sky of three lights, roughly the size of a ping-pong ball. These lights move from East to West, changing their positions relative to each other, and their color turns orange. The witness, on the first floor of a building, calls another military personnel who only observes a single point. Both witnesses go outside to the parking lot where they are joined by three other military personnel. All observe silent movements (transits, stops, zigzags, retreat) of a bright point in the sky at irregular speeds. The PAN disappears towards the North, losing brightness. One hypothesis being explored is the observation of a beam of light, festive or similar, creating a bright spot on the cloud screen (as described in the investigation report). The description of the PAN indeed resembles this type of misperception: three colored lights that later merge into one, erratic zigzag movements, a phase of immobility, a non-zero apparent diameter, etc... The observation took place on a Sunday evening in the middle of August, which could suggest a festive origin for this potential light beam, its source possibly located within a 10-20 km radius. The probable presence of clouds in the sky supports this hypothesis. The first skytracers were introduced in the 1970s, but their use was still rare, which inherently limits the probability of the hypothesis. The fact that no trace of a disco that might have used such equipment is found 40 years later is neutral (neither confirms nor denies it). Another parameter clearly contradicts it: the witnesses describe the brightness as greater than that of a star, while the reflection process on clouds creates a fainter halo. The PAN is certainly not a point source, as described as "big as a ping-pong ball," which would be consistent with a sky tracer. The hypothesis of a sky tracer would require another assumption, that of a confusion and aggregation of light and size factors by the witnesses to express that it was a brighter light mass than a star. This is a possible assimilation for an isolated witness, but more challenging for all witnesses. However, it is noted that there is an evident component of exchanges and influence (voluntary or involuntary) between the witnesses. Indeed, the expression "big as a ping-pong ball" is used identically by all. An isolated witness might use this comparison, associating it with their own distance to represent the ball (without specifying the distance, making the comparison unusable). Another witness (or more, 4 in total) observing the same event cannot make the same mental choices and pathways independently. Therefore, it can be assumed that the influence in the comparisons extended to other areas, such as brightness. But, nothing confirms this. This additional hypothesis (confusion and aggregation of size and brightness factors to express a bright light mass) is possible but, in the absence of a proven precedent in the GEIPAN records, remains speculative and reduces the overall probability of the sky tracer hypothesis. There is no sufficiently probable explanation. The consistency is poor: In terms of information level: - T1 is the only one to have witnessed the strange phase with multiple PAN eliminating an aircraft hypothesis; - the phase of evolution at PAN described coherently by the witnesses, but the complexity of the movement would have required clarifications (especially regarding speed and width of the zigzag); - we do not have the altitude of PAN at the beginning and end of the observation, nor the angular size (the answer "ping-pong ball" is unusable without witness precision). In terms of reliability: - it is evident that there was exchange and influence (at least in the choice of comparisons like "ping-pong") between the witnesses. This raises doubts about the reliability. Additionally, as this is a military base, the main witness (a sergeant) has authority (formal or not) over the others (conscripts) he calls to observe the strangeness or shows to them. One might question if the other witnesses have room to express a possible difference in perception and if it is more "practical" to stick to the sergeant's narrative during depositions, especially since the prior exchange allows them to do so with some assurance. This conduct could be individual or concerted. It is noted that T2 and T5 feel the need to specify (outside of the question) that they would not have noticed PAN if T1 had not reported it, this spontaneous attitude is unique in the GEIPAN records while in the presence of multiple witnesses, it is very common for one witness to alert the others. Furthermore, T4 explains that he delayed his testimony because he was "afraid of being taken for a joker," while at this stage, he should have known that three witnesses, including a superior, had already testified. In this particular context, these attitudes can be interpreted as manifestations of discomfort in testifying and also contribute to an alteration in the perception of the overall reliability. No hypothesis can be retained. On the other hand, the level of consistency is not sufficient to support the resulting strangeness level and conclude in an unexplained character. The case is classified as C, non-identifiable case due to lack of reliable data.
