Summary: Nighttime observation of a pale white-yellow light point moving slowly, and then 4 other identical shapes on the photos taken: observation of planet Mars for the main PAN and of stars for the others.
--- seg-89909336b50c21fb77a33e14 [needs-review] ---
Description: The main witness (T1) is in his home located in Le Conquet (29) on May 22, 2016, when his attention is drawn by his wife (T2) around 23:00 regarding the presence in the sky of a "star that seems to move slightly." Equipped with a camera with a telephoto lens, he takes two photographs before the PAN is obscured by the neighbor's house roof. To the naked eye, the PAN appears as a brief, pale white and yellow object moving slowly; in photographs, it appears as a "cylinder" and moves erratically. T1, upon later viewing the photographs on his computer, notices the presence of 4 additional identical forms, however less "lit up". The case consistency is good, despite only one witness reporting their observation to GEIPAN, thanks to the presence of the two photographs that could be analyzed. *According to GEIPAN criteria, consistency is the amount of reliable and objective information gathered for a testimony. The hypothesis under study is the observation of Mars for the main PAN and stars for the other four. This hypothesis could be validated by the following elements: - The shape of the PAN, appearing point-like to the naked eye, is consistent with the appearance of Mars. The erratic and cylindrical shapes later noticed by T1 on the images are only photographic artifacts caused by the combination of the shutter speed (0.625s) and T1's movement. Its color, perceived as "very pale yellow," distinguishes it from a star. The trajectory is only a deduction from T1 based on the "zigzag" shape of the main PAN in one of the photographs, caused by the camera's movement during exposure. - The changing color ("red-green" according to T1) of another of the PAN corresponds to the characteristic twinkle of a star, located very low on the horizon. The planetarium tool in the GEIPAN image analysis software - IPACO - allowed one of the T1 photographs to be overlaid with the sky map, showing very good agreement between the positions of Mars for the main PAN and stars in the Scorpion constellation for the other PAN (see annexes 1 to 3):

- the star Dschubba (Delta Scorpii) for the lowest and most sparkling "red-green" PAN
- the star Acrab (Beta Scorpii) for another PAN located in the top left
- the two optical double stars Jabhat Acrabi I and II (Omega Scorpii) for the two least bright PAN, below Acrab. Upon examination of the photographs, it will be noted that the azimuths were poorly estimated, with a difference of over 45° compared to Mars, likely due to disorientation of witness T1 during this nighttime observation. The elevation was also overestimated, 45° according to T1 for a real elevation of less than 8° for Mars. Taking photographs with a telephoto lens using high zoom is misleading and has led the observer to overestimate elevations. - Mars was well visible during the observation and imaging. - The very slow speed is indicative of the actual drift of this planet, which is enhanced by Earth's rotation. The GEIPAN classifies this case as "A": observation of the planet Mars for the PAN primary object and stars for the others.
