Summary: Visual and instrumental observations of multiple glowing spheres with varying colors and intensities in the night sky: observations of stars in the constellations of Scorpio and Eagle.
--- seg-91f413b02e3617acd66c4832 [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On June 10, 2017, starting at 10:30 PM, a couple (T1 and T2) observed from their terrace two bright, twinkling points in the sky. Observation with binoculars revealed a spherical shape and a gyroscopic effect. The two PAN were also visible through a telescope. The witnesses then observed two other spheres in a different direction, as well as two or three points near the first two. As the situation did not change, the witnesses stopped observing around 11:15 PM. At 11:30 PM, the witnesses decided to make a new observation, but nothing was visible. The observation was repeated the next day at the same time and under similar conditions. On June 12, the witnesses renewed their observation, but nothing was observable. This case, of low strangeness and good consistency (two witnesses), turned out to be a misidentification with stars from the Scorpion and Eagle constellations, perfectly visible in a clear sky (see the investigation report). The position of these stars relative to the landscape elements and their configuration among each other perfectly correspond to those of the PAN. The strangeness for the witnesses mainly resulted from the "abnormally twinkling with colors (green, red, and white) towards the South. These points seemed fixed but animated on themselves." The twinkling effect is perfectly explainable, as these stars were then close to the horizon. The description of the elements visible through binoculars and the telescope perfectly matches that of stars observed with defocused devices. The fact that the PAN were seen over two consecutive nights, at the same times and under similar conditions, is perfectly characteristic of an astronomical misidentification, and the non-visibility on the third day can be explained by a temperature change that significantly reduced atmospheric turbulence and, thus, twinkling. The witnesses did not find this star configuration in their subsequent astronomy tool search because they had memorized PAN elevations in the sky (45 to 50°) that were greatly overestimated compared to what they themselves had noticed near the elevations of the peaks (10 to 15°). The perception of star movement in the sky varies depending on their position in the vault; the witnesses very likely compared the (East-West) movement of the stars that were sources of the PAN with that of much higher stars in the sky, giving the first an impression of immobility. All the noted peculiarities have an explanation. The case is classified A, misidentification with stars from the Scorpion and Eagle constellations.
