**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to ensure more accurate conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named SAINT-PIERRE (974) 1981 is part of a recent batch of cases re-examined (as detailed in the investigation report). On October 22, 1981, at 8:30 a.m., a driver and his passenger were intrigued by a stationary, low-altitude white light phenomenon they could not explain. The PAN appeared as a streak with a round shape beneath it (T1) or as a rectangle (T2), positioned in the direction of the rising sun. The driver stopped his vehicle. He considered taking out his theodolite located at the back of the vehicle to observe the PAN more closely, but both witnesses felt "paralyzed" inside the car. The observation of this "neon tube," according to both witnesses, lasted about five minutes before the PAN gradually disappeared, either according to T1 or almost instantaneously according to T2. Despite a local press article, no other testimonies were collected. The witnesses were able to observe a condensation trail from an airplane under favorable atmospheric conditions, with the sun low behind the plane illuminating the trail. This trail was of a particular type with shreds, here with only one shred:

- The process of forming an airplane condensation trail and the persistence of this trail involve several atmospheric factors (including pressure and humidity) that can vary along the trajectory of an airplane, even without a change in altitude.
- If the process allows for the formation and significant persistence throughout the movement, the trail will be long and continuous behind the airplane, subject only to the effect of the wind, which can widen and disperse or twist it.
- If the process allows for formation but with low persistence throughout the movement, the trail will be short and will give the impression of following the airplane.
- If the formation and persistence processes vary along the trajectory, the trail can be in shreds or limited to a single end, fixed and persistent, as is likely the case here. The Contrail Science website provides examples with photographs.

The round and less bright shape observed beneath the streak by T1 lacks angular indication compatible with local dispersion and expansion of the trail. It is also unclear whether the round shape is attached to the streak or separated from it. It could also be a distant nebula whose particularly round shape led T1 to associate it with the oddity of the streak, when it would have gone unnoticed without the initial oddity. This shape was perceived by T1 as more difficult to distinguish than the streak; it's possible that T2 (who did not see it) did not have the same visual or attention capabilities as T1, or did not associate a nebula with the initial oddity. The disappearance of the trail as perceived by T2 seems too rapid. However, the hypothesis of reduced visual or attention capabilities for T2 could also explain that he only saw the beginning of the decrease in light intensity, while T1 was able to follow the phenomenon as it "faded away little by little."

The consistency is poor in terms of both information and reliability:

- The description lacks precision, particularly in terms of angular position in the sky, angular size, and the relative positions of the two shapes observed by T1.
- There are two witnesses (not independent) whose accounts align on some points and contradict each other on others. Discrepancies in the accounts did not lead to requests for clarification to better quantify the degree of discordance beyond words.
- Lack of information and some discrepancies lead to additional hypotheses to explain the observation by a trail. In fact, the "airplane trail" hypothesis loses probability but remains plausible. In the end, the consistency proves too marginal to validate such an explanation.

These two factors—the existence of a plausible hypothesis but a lack of consistency to support it—oppose maintaining the PAN in the unexplained category. Therefore, the GEIPAN concludes: lack of reliable information.
