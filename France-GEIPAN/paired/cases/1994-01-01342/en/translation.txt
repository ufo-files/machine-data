**Case Description:**

The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to ensure more accurate conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification.

This observation case, previously classified as D and named FLEURY-SUR-ANDELLE (27) 04.01.1994, is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases (see investigation report). On January 4, 1994, at 11:50 PM, a witness watching television had their attention drawn to a yellowish light growing in size in the sky. Through the window, they saw a bright, cone-shaped object with multicolored horizontal bands rotating on itself. The witness locates the object below a layer of clouds, approximately north-west above the Château de Bonnemare, but at a relatively close distance. The witness observes the object receding, then returning as a yellow sphere before growing larger again. Panicked, the witness goes to bed without waking their spouse. A few minutes later, they hear noises outside and see regular flashes. They notice their dog is frightened, and they themselves feel muscle contractions in their legs and lower back. The witness does not report the object's disappearance. They are deeply shaken by their experience. The witness contacts the gendarmerie the next day. No other testimonies are collected.

This testimony is primarily characterized by its lack of consistency:

- We have no information on the duration of observation, though it can be assumed to be short. Indeed, observations lasting more than a few minutes are generally reported by the witness. We have no angular height information in the sky, no variation in position, no angular size of the object, and crucially, the witness did not observe the object's disappearance because, panicked, they went to bed.
- Reliability is poor. There is a strong state of stress: "panicked, I went upstairs," "in a panic," "out of fear, I ran up the stairs," "very frightened, I hid under my blankets."

Regarding the final phase of the description, given that the witness is then under the covers and panicked, the descriptions of pale-white circles might not be related to the previously observed object and could be a visual illusion caused by the stress state. The dog's barking, which is not typical behavior, might be due to the stress of its master or the sounds (branch breaking? animal? ) heard outside by the witness. The state of stress was already established during the visual observation phase, which also compromises the reliability of these descriptions.

The description of the object and the presence of clouds suggest the possibility of a misidentification with a skytracer. This type of misidentification was common in the 1990s (see numerous cases on the Geipan website: www.geipan.fr). The gendarmerie's investigation into local event venues and nightclubs found no evidence of pyrotechnic displays or laser shows on the night of the observation. A skytracer generally involves significant angular movements in the sky that the witness does not note. Furthermore, due to a lack of observation duration information, the witness might have seen a short phase of stability of the light spot. Notably, the observation took place on a Monday evening, an atypical time for skytracer activity, unless it was testing or calibration.

Given the limited decisive descriptive elements, the most opposing factor to this hypothesis is the negative neighborhood investigation by the gendarmerie. The hypothesis of an aircraft is also conceivable. The direction of observation of the object corresponds exactly to the direction of the Rouen Vallée de Seine (76) airport, located 14 km northwest. Thus, the description of the object as a bright point approaching the witness could suggest an aircraft taking off from this airport and heading towards the witness. The multicolored cone shape could then be explained by the aircraft being close enough to the witness and partially obscured by the cloud layer, making it difficult to identify while still emitting lights. It's also worth noting that the witness describes the object as being surrounded by "lively, very pretty colors."

The lack of noise reported by the witness can be explained by them observing the object from inside their home, windows closed, with a television on in the early stages of the observation. The object's retreat and return can be explained by clouds partially obscuring the aircraft, leading to changes in angular size and light intensity that the witness could reasonably interpret as changes in distance. Despite this, the visual aspect remains quite unusual for this type of misidentification. The perception of the cone's rotation is curious, except if it's a helicopter in stationary hover rotating on itself. The witness does not describe alternating bright flashes of white or red from anti-collision lights, but there are other verified observations of aircraft without this type of perception. The decisive descriptive elements for confirming or denying this hypothesis are missing. For an aircraft to be visible as a cone, it must be relatively close (a few kilometers), and the witness should have perceived an angular movement, unless the observation is very short or it's a helicopter. An aircraft always ends up moving and disappearing, and this phase provides characteristic information, but we have no observation of its disappearance. The fact that we have no evidence of aircraft presence is not decisive as traffic information from 25 years ago is unavailable.

However, we note (see report) that someone from the village saw a light moving in the sky at the same time but didn't pay attention, thinking it was an airplane.

In conclusion, the strangeness of the case exists because it is not possible to support a hypothesis as probable at more than 50%. On the other hand, the lack of consistency does not allow us to strengthen or deny any hypothesis, and thus validate an unexplained character for this case. It is not possible to draw a conclusion. The GEIPAN classifies the case as C: lack of reliable information.
