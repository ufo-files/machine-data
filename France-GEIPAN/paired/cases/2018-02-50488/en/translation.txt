Summary: Long, discontinuous observations of multicolored light phenomena moving slowly across the night sky: lack of reliable information.
--- seg-5c68264ea2edb9acd3ed81f9 [needs-review] ---
Description: On Monday, February 12, 2018 (not February 18 as indicated in the report) around 7 PM, a witness (T1) leaving their home was intrigued by a PAN bright, circular, multicolored object in the sky. The PAN is stationary, and the witness decides to get a pair of binoculars to observe it better. After 15 minutes of observation, the witness (T1) calls his son (T2), who is biking 1km from his father's house and who has also just seen two bright lights ("white balls") in the sky before they disappeared. T2 joins his father (T1) at Moureuille (63) and they will observe together, intermittently until 23:00, luminous phenomena with changing colors and very slow movement. Witnesses gave a statement at the Gendarmerie in February 2018, and T1 drew what he saw through the binoculars. On 11/15/2018, an investigator visited the site and provided additional information (see the investigation report). The witnesses likely observed stars (see the investigation report). The elements in favor of this are: the appearance, the quasi-immobility, the duration of the observation, the compatibility of the observations with proven positions of stars. The witnesses do not observe continuously and do so within a limited field of view. The initial strangeness of a first star (PAN 1) leads them to look at a second angular field (PAN 2) and to return to examine these fields, and in fact to continue to be confused with different stars that are actually visible (magnitude < 3) within 10° of the witnesses' indication. It is classic that a first strangeness experienced by a witness triggers an alert effect that can lead to alerting a second witness who might not have noticed anything otherwise and/or incorrectly associating later observations with the first. What remains most surprising here is the reason why the initial strangeness for T1 with the PAN 1 is attributed to the star Deneb, which is not known as a significant source of misperceptions, unless atmospheric conditions here have particularly enhanced multicolored effects. However, subsequent observation with binoculars did enhance the strangeness with the introduction, due to a misting phenomenon, of an iridescent appearance around a zoomed-in bright point. Nevertheless, even if the nature of the explanations is plausible, the extent leading to this triggering of strangeness is uncertain. In the end, the consistency is limited to confirm a probable misidentification with observations of several stars, even if this hypothesis appears plausible in many points. The consistency and strangeness assessment using the GEIPAN method gives a limit value C "unidentified phenomenon due to lack of reliable information" but close to B "probably identified phenomenon" as an astronomical misperception of stars given the observation by two witnesses. As a result, the GEIPAN concludes with a classification C: "unidentified phenomenon due to a lack of reliable information."
