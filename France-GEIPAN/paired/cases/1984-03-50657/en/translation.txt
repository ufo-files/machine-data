Description: GEIPAN continues to publish all its archives on its public website www.geipan.fr. Its publications include older cases classified at the time (A, B, C or D) that are now being re-examined solely to improve the pertinence of the conclusions. Thanks to new technical resources (software) and the investigative experience acquired over all these years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or even a change in classification. This observation case, previously classified D and named LA FOA (988) 05.03.1984, is one of a group of recently re-examined cases. It concerns observations of a PAN by several residents of LA FOA (988), New Caledonia, on different dates in March 1984: the 02 and 05 and an unknown day (see the investigation report). On March 07, 1984, the gendarmerie are informed that several people in a neighbourhood of LA FOA have repeatedly observed luminous phenomena in the sky. The gendarmerie investigation records in the same official report that: - On March 05, 1984, between 04:30 and 06:30, two witnesses in a car (T1 and T2) make a very long observation of a particularly bright phenomenon in the sky; - On March 02, 1984, around 20:00, two witnesses at home (T3 and T4) observe a fireball emitting white light; - On a date whose day he does not remember, also in March 1984, another witness (T5) observes a "broad band of light" three times from his home. One final witness (T6) is interviewed by the gendarmerie because he is mentioned in T5's statement. However, this last witness made no observation. The witnesses or pairs of witnesses observe a PAN on different dates and at different times, and the PANs' positions and movements also differ. An explanation specific to each of the three observations (T1-T2, T3-T4 and T5) must be sought, without initially assuming a single phenomenon. Furthermore, the gendarmerie intervened following rumours about residents repeatedly seeing luminous shapes in the sky. No witness spontaneously reported an observation. Because of these rumours and approximate comparisons between witnesses, the strangeness reported in their statements may have been greater than that experienced at the time, and some observations, considered separately, might have gone unnoticed. This may explain the anecdotal or at least insubstantial nature of T5's statement: - The witness no longer knows whether it was the previous day or the day before ("one or two days ago"), which is never the case when an observation is truly strange; - The available information is imprecise, beyond just the date, and permits no precise analysis: "something like a broad band of light, intensely illuminating the sky, occurred on three separate occasions. It looked like the passage of a very luminous object moving from right to left above the trees facing my home"; - The witness has an initial meteorological explanation, which does not remove the perceived strangeness but reduces its extent: "For a while I thought it heralded bad weather, while still remaining shocked by the phenomenon"; - In the prevailing context and given the limited information, we cannot exclude a particular arrangement of vehicle headlight beams projected into the sky producing an initially limited strangeness that increased retrospectively. T5's observation has very little substance, in both information and reliability, given the evidence available 35 years later. This apparently rather unremarkable observation cannot be usefully analysed (classification C) because reliable information is lacking. In contrast, T1 and T2's accounts indicate a real level of perceived strangeness, even if it increased after the observation, and contain a good level of information. These two witnesses undoubtedly misidentified Venus: - The PAN's described appearance is consistent: a luminous ball, white colour and strong brightness; features described beneath the PAN recur fairly often in misidentifications of Venus; - The long duration, 2 hours according to T1, is characteristic of an astronomical misidentification, and the PAN's disappearance during a half-hour after sunrise is characteristic of Venus; - Venus was in the direction indicated by the witnesses and should have been near the PAN if the latter was not Venus. Yet the witnesses describe a clear, starry sky and do not mention the brightest star near the PAN. The witnesses note movement of the PAN, apparently inconsistent with Venus: - They were in a car at the beginning, which could have caused an illusion of the PAN moving relative to the surroundings and nearby horizon, whereas those surroundings were changing in front of Venus because of the hilly, winding track they were following; - T2's description could suggest movement after the vehicle stopped: "he stopped the vehicle. That was when the light beam disappeared, the ball stabilised and then went towards the Ouipoin–Koindé mountain". This supports the previous explanation because the vehicle's stop is followed by stabilisation of the ball. The description of its final movement towards the mountain is also compatible with the hypothesis. A decrease in Venus's intensity, due to changing atmospheric haze, is logically interpreted by the witness as an increase in the PAN's distance along the line of sight, which corresponds to that mountain's direction. T2 reports that "the light beam disappeared" after T1 flashed the vehicle's headlights. T1 does not report this conjunction, saying only, "At one point the trapezoidal shape went out and I perceived only the balloon's glow", although he was presumably seeking a reaction from the PAN by flashing the headlights. The beam's disappearance resulted from changing atmospheric haze, whereas T2's memory and/or interpretation of a conjunction or causal link with the headlight flash should be attributed to the experienced strangeness, possibly increased afterwards. For T3 and T4's observation, the description of a slowly moving luminous ball may suggest an aircraft or satellite. But nothing appears either in the air-operations record compiled just after the observation or in the satellite-pass record that can be reconstructed 35 years later. The observation therefore has a strange character. Its substance, however, is weak: - In terms of information, there is no indication of the PAN's duration or angular size; - Reliability can be questioned: both witnesses, T3 and T4, give minimal accounts with no expression of experienced strangeness, as though simply answering questions from gendarmes who came to see them following observation accounts they might have shared in the neighbourhood. T2 had indeed reported to the gendarmes an observation related by T3. T3 and T4's short accounts are almost identical, with the same expressions and words, as though they had conferred before being interviewed. T3 reportedly told T2 about two earlier observations similar to T2's, but does not mention the second (March 03 at 04:00) to the gendarmes, and describes the first (March 02 at 19:00) in a way bearing little resemblance to T2's, with very different direction and movements. The substance of T3 and T4's observation does not appear sufficient to support its apparent strangeness and characterise it as unexplained. Consequently: Case FOA (LA) (988) 05.03.1984: T1 and T2's observation is classified A, observation of the planet Venus. Case FOA (LA) (988) 02.03.1984: T3 and T4's observation is classified C, potentially strange but unusable because reliable information is lacking. Case FOA (LA) (988) --.03.1984: T5's observation is classified C, potentially of little strangeness but unusable because reliable information is lacking.
