Summary: Long night-time observation of a very bright point emitting red reflections: very probable observation of the star Capella.
--- seg-e8df99545d3591e1dc488f25 [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On August 16, 2023, the main witness, from their home in Solomiac (32), observed from 2:00 to 2:30 a.m. in the northeast, at a low altitude above the horizon, a very bright PAN of round shape slowly rising into the sky. This PAN maintained a constant shape throughout the observation and emitted red reflections. The observation ended when the PAN was obscured by clouds, as well as due to the late hour. The witness's partner also briefly observed the PAN, but did not provide a statement. Initially, the witness thought they had observed a helicopter lighthouse, but noted an anomaly due to the slow and steady movement of the object over a 30-minute period. The consistency is average, with one unique witness and no photos or videos of the PAN. However, the witness was able to take a reconstruction photograph that proved particularly useful for determining the origin of the PAN. According to the criteria of GEIPAN, consistency refers to the amount of reliable and objective information gathered for a testimony. After ruling out the possibilities of an airplane and a helicopter, the investigation concluded that the witness likely observed the star Capella (see the investigation report). This conclusion is based on the following elements:

- "Round" shape, often described by witnesses in the presence of brightly lit point stars.
- White color, consistent with that of a star, and red reflections, characteristic of a twinkling effect caused by the low angular elevation of the star above the horizon.
- Slow movement over a 30-minute period.

Using the reconstruction photograph provided by the witness, we were able to verify and refine the estimation of the PAN's azimuths and elevations (initial and final) that were initially incorrect in the questionnaire. Once this precision was established, the difference between the PAN's position and that of the star varies from 5 to 9 degrees in azimuth and 6 to 8 degrees in elevation, which is not significant given the margins of error associated with the calculations and the reconstruction of the PAN's positioning. It is also worth noting that if the PAN had not been Capella, it would have been surprising that the witness did not mention it, as they would have observed two identical bright points close together, which was not the case. The witness ruled out the star hypothesis by stating, "No star as bright as this was present in that part of the sky." However, Capella is the fourth brightest star visible from the Northern Hemisphere (source: Wikipedia) and was indeed in the portion of the sky observed that evening. The GEIPAN classifies the case as "A," very probable observation of the star Capella.
