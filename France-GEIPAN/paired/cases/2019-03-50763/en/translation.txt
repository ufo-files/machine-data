Description: On March 29, 2019, during an EASYJET flight (EZY926L) from Lille (59) to Nice (06), the pilot contacted air traffic control regarding wake turbulence and potential "heavy" (large airliner) traffic 1000 feet below. The pilot confirms to the controller that a "heavy" crossed from right to left a few minutes ago and he wonders what type of aircraft it could have been as the "lit up lights were 'quite unusual'." There are two people who saw these lights. In addition, this PAN appeared on the onboard TCAS instrument. However, no aircraft corresponding to the trajectory and altitude described by the pilot is visible on the radars of the Aix-en-Provence control center. The observation is based on exchanges between the aircraft and air traffic control. No other testimony is collected. Here is the translation of the GEIPAN text from French to English:

This is the summary of the observation (see the investigation report or CR) based on the exchanges between flight EZY926L and air traffic control: - The pilot of flight EZY926L reports tail turbulence while at the NISAR hold point, which the pilot attributes to the passage of a "heavy" aircraft (see CR annex) seen a few minutes earlier. - A few minutes before reaching this point, the crew observes an aircraft with unusual lights from the cockpit. This PAN appears on the onboard TCAS instrument (see annex of CR) and locates it ahead at approximately 10 to 15 nm (20 to 30 kilometers) 1000 ft lower (300 meters), flying from right to left, with an estimated heading by the pilot of 060°. - No aircraft corresponding to the trajectory and altitude described by the pilot is visible on the radars of the Aix-en-Provence control center. The TCAS (Traffic Collision Avoidance System) is an on-board aircraft instrument designed to avoid in-flight collisions between aircraft. Its role is to detect surrounding aircraft if they are equipped with a transponder (it is not a radar, it does not detect an aircraft without a transponder or with a transponder off). The transponder found in airplanes is an electronic device that allows its identification by radars or TCAS. Without the pilot's testimony, we have no information regarding the characteristics of PAN and can only analyze its exchanges with air traffic control in an attempt to determine the position and trajectory of the witnesses as well as that of PAN. The analysis shows that: - the atmospheric conditions on that day are not favorable for the persistence of turbulence from another aircraft's wake. - the weather conditions on that day suggest that there is no necessarily correlation between the turbulence and the passage of PAN, which is likely one of the origins of the strangeness felt by the pilot. - the PAN observed by the crew is probably the plot visible on the TCAS as it seems to correspond with what they see from the cockpit. - under this hypothesis, we can conclude that the PAN is equipped with a transpondor, but in that case, it should also have been identifiable by the air traffic control radars. - without a description of the PAN, we cannot determine why the lights seen from the cockpit were unusual to the pilot. The investigator called upon an expert pilot of the GEIPAN who confirms this analysis (see the expert's report attached to the CR). The lack of information (no pilot testimony) does not allow us to advance a precise hypothesis. Indeed, it seems there is an inconsistency in the exchanges between the pilot (EZY926L) and the air traffic controller (CTL): - if the onboard TCAS instrument detected the PAN, the likelihood that it is an aircraft equipped with a transponder is high. - but in this case, air traffic control should have also detected it on their radar, which is not the case. In the absence of the pilot's testimony, the strangeness and consistency of the case are rather weak. The GEIPAN classifies this observation as C due to a lack of exploitable information.
