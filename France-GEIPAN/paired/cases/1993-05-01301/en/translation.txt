Description: In the publications of GEIPAN (www.geipan.fr), there are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed with the sole purpose of being more relevant in the conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over many years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named SAINT-GERMAIN-SUR-VIENNE (37) 16.05.1993 is part of a recent set of cases that have been reviewed. This case is also known as CORMERY (37) 1993. On May 16, 1993, between midnight and 1 a.m., three people in a vehicle were intrigued by the sight of a phenomenon they had seen several times during their journey. Only one witness reported their observation: the PAN was described as a blue, formless shape, most often in front of the vehicle at the level of the headlights, at an impossible distance to define. The PAN seemed to move from right to left in a continuous back-and-forth motion, always at the same height. Intrigued witnesses did not stop and made a discontinuous observation over 4km. Only one testimony was collected. It is very likely that the witnesses observed a disco laser during a Saturday to Sunday night (see the investigation report). The described aspect is typical of other well-known observations today of the GEIPAN (which was not the case at the time): elongated shape, lit from below by a blue light, back-and-forth movements from left to right, formless and thin object. Although the witness noted that the sky was clear at the observation location, the presence of clouds that could reflect a laser was confirmed towards the North horizon, as a disturbance centered on Britain brought lightning described by the witness. In 1993, disco lasers were indeed not numerous, but they already existed. It turns out that a GEIPAN case in the same sector and named (ILE BOUCHARD (37) 11.12.1993) proved that a disco equipment called "Sky Rose" had been responsible for observations with the same typical aspects as this one. Other GEIPAN observation cases have shown that the generating equipment could be several dozen kilometers away from the witnesses and that for the same equipment, the effects in terms of aspect and appearance distance were variable (atmospheric factors), which of course increases the potential for misidentification. Even though it was not possible 25 years later to find the generating equipment (nor, of course, to prove its absence), the consistency of the aspect with what the equipment used at the time could generate allows the hypothesis to be considered very probable. The case is classified as A: very probable observation of a disco laser.
