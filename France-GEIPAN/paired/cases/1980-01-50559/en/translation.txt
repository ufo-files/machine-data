Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. The observation case previously classified as D and named PANTIN (93) 30.05.1993 is part of a recent set of cases that have been re-examined (see the investigation report).

On May 30, 1993, at 4:15 a.m., a witness, returning home after a night out, observed from their balcony an orange rectangular shape in the sky of PANTIN. The shape was estimated to be about 1 meter in size and appeared stationary for 15 minutes before slowly moving, changing direction, and moving away to disappear on the horizon. The witness observed a flickering red light but heard no noise. On the same balcony, at 4:30 a.m., the witness observed a second shape, this time circular and white, not blinding, that was stationary. The size of this disk was also estimated to be approximately 1 meter. The witness continued to observe this second phenomenon until 6 a.m., when the PAN disappeared very slowly. In fact, there were two observations of two distinct PANs:

Initially (see the case PANTIN (93) 30.05.1993 T1), it is likely the landing lights of an aircraft in a VFR night approach on one of the two Paris airports located in the observation axis of the witness. Aircraft landing lights can indeed be seen from very far away, as demonstrated on the GEIPAN website in the case "EZE (06) 16.05.2014," where such an aircraft was observed at a distance of approximately 36 km from the witness, and in the case "FONTRAILLES (65) 31.10.2017," where the aircraft's lights are visible and nearly stationary during the day from 100 km away for over 10 minutes. The distance prevented the witness from distinguishing anything other than these powerful lights, whose color and brightness blend with the red anti-collision light located under the aircraft's fuselage, giving the "orange rectangle" shape noted by the witness. The eastward direction of movement, the low angular elevation above the horizon, the observation of a flickering red light after the aircraft's turn on its axis, positioning it facing the runway, the slow movement, and the absence of noise support this hypothesis.

In a second instance, the duration of the observation, the slow movement of the PAN in the sky, its circular and white shape, and its disappearance due to the clouds present in large numbers that night all support the hypothesis of a confusion with Venus, rising in the east and moving slowly towards east-southeast while gaining altitude. The witness likely does not habitually observe the sky at this late hour of the night and is unfamiliar with these astronomical phenomena. They likely scanned the sky due to the attention or concern aroused by the first observation, which is the main observation to remember. The consistency is average and benefits mainly from the identification with buildings, partially compensating for the absence of angular measurements taken by the gendarmes during their on-site investigation.

This case PANTIN (93) 30.05.1993 T2 is classified as A: Observation of Venus.
