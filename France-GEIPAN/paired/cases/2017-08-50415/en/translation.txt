Summary: Observations of the presence at low altitude of two red and green lights adjacent with oscillating movements and a change in trajectory: probable observations of an aircraft.
--- seg-27e4aee1c0c71730e0a7440d [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On August 25, 2017, at 02:15 (time confirmed by T2), two witnesses observed in the night sky colored lights (one red and one green) that intrigued them. At first perceived as stationary, these two lights moved together, changing trajectory, and were quickly hidden by the landscape. No noise was heard. The witnesses very likely observed an aircraft, as the red and green lights were navigation lights:

- The two lights were always seen side by side or attached,
- During the final movement to the right, only the green light remained visible, as expected for an aircraft with a right green position light, the only one visible when the plane flies transversely at low altitude (in this case, the plane then disappears behind trees). The left red light would be hidden by the fuselage.

Arguments leading T1 to exclude an aircraft include the absence of noise and flashing ("A helicopter or an airplane can also be ruled out, given the silence and absence of flashing of the lights"). However, the perception of noise depends on multiple factors, including distance (the witnesses may have thought the PAN was closer than it was), wind that could be contrary to the noise (which is the case here with an east wind reported on Météociel), and the strong visual attention caused by the strangeness, which can override auditory attention or memory (many examples exist in GEIPAN).

Moreover, navigation lights do not flash; the flashing of an aircraft comes from white anti-collision lights ("strobes"). While the former are mandatory at all flight phases, the latter are not. There is also doubt about the perception of no flashing, at least for part of the observation, as T2 states, "Then I also saw a green light flash."

The strength of the evidence in favor (green and red navigation lights) and the questionable and non-determinant nature of the objection elements (absence of noise and flashing) lead to the conclusion of an aircraft. The stationary and oscillating nature of the PAN suggests a helicopter, which would have primarily presented its left side (red navigation light) before partially turning (both red and green lights) and then heading to the right, showing only its right side (green light).

This aircraft did not appear in commercial traffic records, but these are not absolutely reliable, and helicopters are often non-commercial. It would be more likely a private flight, civil security, medical transport, or military origin. The request for radar trace from air traffic services (CNOA) was made too late, and GEIPAN also knows from experience that flights at low altitude (as in this case) may not produce radar traces.

Consequently, GEIPAN classifies the case as B and concludes: very probable observation of an aircraft.
