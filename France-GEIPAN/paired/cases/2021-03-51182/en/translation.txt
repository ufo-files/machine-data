Summary: Observation over several nights of a bright point in the sky at low altitude: mistaken for Sirius at sunset.
--- seg-247f0ecd397d95ea4c036b49 [needs-review] ---
Description: A witness reports successive observations of a colored light point in the night sky at low altitude between March 24 and 30, 2021. The witness photographs and films the PAN on March 29, 2021. A neighborhood investigation by the Gendarmerie does not identify any other witnesses. Analysis of the testimony (narrative, descriptive characteristics, recurrence of events, etc.) suggests an astronomical confusion. A search in this regard identifies the presumed PAN as a misidentification of Sirius. In this case, it is not necessary to invoke various potential hypotheses (see the investigation report). Compared to other stars, the witness was disturbed by the intense brightness of the PAN. (Sirius being the brightest of all visible stars!). He observes a slight movement of the PAN to the right (which perfectly corresponds to the azimuth drift due to the Earth's own rotational motion). He does not really notice the gradual decrease in angular height, given his "cluttered" horizon, and the fact that he himself is not in a completely fixed position (he must move slightly around his average position). However, he notices the disappearance of the PAN without understanding the reason. During another observation on March 30, 2021, the witness notes that the disappearance occurs at 12:16 local time. At this date and time, Sirius was at an angular height of 2.5°, a position very compatible with an apparent setting (due to the witness not being able to see the real horizon because of local relief). The strangeness largely rests on the fact that the PAN randomly changes color. These colors cover a good part of the solar (stellar) spectrum, which is, of course, a diffraction effect. Thus, optical effects due to the atmosphere (diffraction, turbulence, refraction, absorption, diffusion) can fully account for the aspect described by the witness. As for the non-pointual aspect of the PAN, it is not visible to the naked eye and simply results from optical distortions generated by the camera, distortions commonly (or even systematically) observed in such situations. The astronomical context leaves no doubt about the explanation of the case. The GEIPAN classifies the case as A: Misidentification with Sirius at sunset.
