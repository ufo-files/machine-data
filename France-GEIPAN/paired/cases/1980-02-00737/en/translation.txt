Summary: Observations of silent movements of light phenomena in the sky and ground traces: Phase 1 likely observation of an aircraft and Phase 2 is unusable as it exceeds the sole competence of GEIPAN.
--- seg-30807701d74d126848ca3d82 [needs-review] ---
**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reexamined to enhance the accuracy of conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this reexamination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This previously classified observation (D) named SAINT-REMY-DE-PROVENCE (13) 17.02.1980 and involving three witnesses who saw luminous phenomena is part of a recent batch of reexamined cases (see the investigation report). T1, startled by a glimpse of an orange glow through a cleaned interior stairwell window, calls his son T2 to confirm the sighting. T2 first searches for the PAN on the ground around the house. T1 and a witness T3 look out a window and then observe two red lights and a flickering white light in the sky, heading north (as reported by the witnesses). These witnesses direct T2 to the PAN in the sky. No unusual noises were heard. The witnesses then searched the ground around the house and found suspicious ground tracks. The Gendarmerie was notified that same evening and arrived at the scene. T1's initial observation under poor conditions (nighttime, a few seconds, through a cleaned fenestron window) led to fear and stress due to the unexplained phenomenon, which influenced the other two witnesses. This first observation is consistent with a phenomenon entirely on the ground; the witnesses even considered the possibility of vehicle fires and conducted a simulation. However, under T1's influence, they became convinced that they had witnessed the landing and takeoff of a PAN in front of their homes, despite no one seeing the initial arrival or departure. The light in the sky could be explained by an airplane in landing phase at Avignon-Caumont Airport, though not recognized as such by the witnesses despite a clear white anti-collision light that T2 perceived as flickering regularly, like aircraft strobe lights. The two red or pale orange lights are likely landing lights observed from behind. The zigzag impression could be caused by: 1) physiological factors (perceptual illusions) of micro-ocular motion (or "autokinesthesia"), where stationary bright objects are perceived with small aberrant movements; or 2) observation conditions. Against the dark night sky, the aircraft is seen only by its lights. The witnesses describe a single flickering white light, but it's likely there were several, located in different parts of the aircraft (drift, wings, fuselage) and visible intermittently. This intermittency is caused by either temporary blocking of one light by an aircraft component or rapid alternation of different types of anti-collision lights. The intermittent lights could create a zigzag impression. Thus, there's no link to be established between the initial ground light and the sky light. It was T1's initial fear and communication to the others that led the witnesses to associate the sky light with the strangeness of the ground light, when the sky light itself might not have been noticed. Therefore, GEIPAN concludes, regarding the sky light, with a B classification: probable observation of an aircraft. After this observation phase, T2 searches for tracks around what he considers a landing site and notes the presence of nearly identical tracks in a sand pile in front of the residence, as described by T1. The initial ground light and the ground tracks are perfectly compatible with phenomena entirely on the ground (without any aerial phase). Consequently, GEIPAN concludes, regarding the initial light and ground tracks, with a C classification: unexploitable as it exceeds the scope of GEIPAN's expertise. However, GEIPAN offers a hypothesis for these ground phenomena without claiming a definitive explanation. The ground tracks are very similar to the impressions a mason's bucket could leave in loose sand (same dimensions, identical ridges). Thus, they can be linked to the initial light by crafting a scenario that accounts for the whole. A dishonest person might have taken advantage of the night and the absence of a fence to access the property, using a mason's bucket placed in the sand pile for easier filling. To avoid detection, the perpetrator might have turned off their engine and position lights while filling their bucket. They could have used a motorized two-wheeler for easier access to the garden, which could explain why the observed (isolated) light doesn't match simulations conducted by the witnesses using a car and its position lights. But there's no indication that the initial light necessarily came from the garden. Determining the precise origin of light received through a polished glass window is challenging. It could be from farther away, outside the property, and the ground tracks would then be unrelated to the glow, even if the bucket hypothesis remains relevant.
