Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time as Type 'D' cases, which are now being reassessed with the sole aim of being more accurate in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over many years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or explicit changes in classification in an investigation note. This observation case previously named VOUZIERS (08) 1997 is part of cases classified 'D' by the SEPRA. On February 19, 1997, the newspaper L'Union published an article in its columns about the observation of a PAN by a resident of VOUZIERS (08) a few days earlier. Reading this article caught the attention of the local gendarmerie brigade. The witness was interviewed on February 21. He reported that while driving his van, he saw, intermittently, a bright orange ball of light moving from west to east in the sky. With the window open, no noise was heard. The investigation conducted on the following days in the area did not yield any other witnesses or information regarding the observation of this PAN. This case is weak (see investigation notes): - only one witness, inconsistencies between the statements made to the journalist and the gendarmes. - lack of precision or consistency in the positions in the sky of the PAN, duration, and date. It is possible (by cross-referencing) to resolve some of the inconsistencies or imprecisions. What one can reconstruct from the observation has many common characteristics (observation duration, shape, size, color, trajectory) with a perfectly known astronautic object: the Mir space station. Furthermore, Mir was indeed passing through the sky in the evening at the time of the observation, and under conditions consistent with what one can reconstruct from the observation. However, the Mir hypothesis would correspond to an observation, not on February 6 but on February 5 or 7, which would still be consistent with the testimony since the latter leaves room for doubt about the exact date: the witness speaks in the press article of "Thursday, February 6," while in the gendarmerie report, he speaks of "Tuesday, February 6, 1997." February 6, 1997, was actually a Thursday. On February 6, the station was visible under the conditions of the observation, but 45 minutes later than the time testified to the observation. One can think that the witness's error concerns the day rather than the time, although one cannot rule out the opposite. Note that the testimony was given 15 days after the observation. In total, the observation of the PAN has many similarities with the visibility of the Mir station during this period. However, the impreciseness or inconsistencies in the testimony (date, departure direction of the PAN, exact position of the witness) prevent the formal identification of this case as a misperception with the Mir station. On the contrary, this same inconsistency does not allow giving an unexplained character to this observation. Depending on whether one admits or not the date error, one can conclude: - that it is an observation of Mir on February 6 (but with a probability barely above 0.5) and the conclusion would be PAN B observation of Mir. - or that one does not know what the witness saw without having reliable elements to conduct a search or to accredit a strange observation, and the conclusion would be PAN C lack of reliable information. The GEIPAN chooses the second option and classifies the case as PAN C: lack of reliable information.
