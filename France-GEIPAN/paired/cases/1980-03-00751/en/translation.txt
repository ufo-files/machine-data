**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions' relevance. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named EGUILLE (L') (17) 16.03.1980 (also known as ROYAN (17) 1980) is part of a recent batch of cases re-examined (see the investigation report).

On March 16, 1980, around 11:30 PM, three witnesses were intrigued by a bright, orange-yellow light mass in the overcast, dark night sky. For 45 minutes, despite their comings and goings (including to alert the gendarmerie), the witnesses followed the silent movements of the phenomenon. It quickly disappeared between Marennes and Rochefort (17). A sketch showing the movement of PAN and the surrounding landscape is attached to the protocol, along with a location plan and a photographic plate annotated with the position of PAN. No other testimonies were collected. Only two hypotheses were considered, studied, and explored; they are ultimately quite similar. The hypothesis of a hunter's spotlight (by boat or on foot) fishing for eels (fishers never go out on Sundays) impacting the lower cloud layer is plausible given the witnesses' privileged position, located immediately nearby and observing along the Seudre river, where this fishing is practiced. The circular shape, distorted by perspective, the "small glowing tail" described by witness 1, and the size estimates given by the witnesses are all compatible with this hypothesis. However, it is contradicted by four main factors:

- The spotlight's color, which is white, not orange-yellow.
- The brightness of PAN, described as very strong, even blinding, by all three witnesses. Witness 1, for example, stated that the surrounding landscape was illuminated as if it were daytime, which is impossible with a spotlight impacting clouds, even at low altitude.
- The risk a hunter would take by leaving their spotlight aimed at the sky, making it easily detectable by authorities.
- One might also wonder why a hunter would "play" with their spotlight, moving it up and down in the sky for 45 minutes.

The other hypothesis, involving a disco spotlight, is also plausible, similar to the spotlight of a hunter, considering the observation axis, the coastal area, the shape, size, and "small glowing tail" described by witness 1. The witnesses' movements and the orange-yellow color are also compatible with this hypothesis, as is the date, in the night from Saturday to Sunday. However, there are data that disprove this hypothesis. Notably:

- The brightness of PAN, described as very strong, even blinding, by all three witnesses. Witness 1, for example, stated that the surrounding landscape was illuminated as if it were daytime, which is not compatible with a spotlight on clouds, even at low altitude.
- We are out of season, and no establishment using this type of spotlight is open.
- The distances are too great for it to be a spotlight used by a coastal establishment, unless one imagines a local device closer, but it's too late to conduct any on-site verification.

Thus, there is no sufficient probability hypothesis to explain the observation. In terms of consistency, although all witnesses were heard at the gendarmerie and the resulting protocol is quite complete (with maps and photographs), important data are missing, such as azimuth readings for each witness (only witness 1 provided the information) and PAN elevation angles. Following the current GEIPAN methodology, this level of strangeness and consistency requires a "field investigation" with the witness for a reconstruction and cognitive interview. This interview may positively or negatively impact the evaluation of strangeness and consistency. However, it is not possible for a case revisited so long after the event. Therefore, this case's evaluation is made according to the current criteria for considering strangeness and consistency (see GEIPAN http://www.cnes-geipan.fr/index.php?id=181&no_cache=1&tx_ttnews[backPid]=211&tx_ttnews[tt_news]=209) as if the field investigation had been conducted and was entirely neutral (no additional input, plus or minus), which is sometimes the case. In contrast, to mark this methodological gap, the classification is based on the ABCD system, not the current ABC D1/D2 system, which for unexplained classifications corresponds to a stronger validation of the unexplained character. Here, the case is the boundary between an unexplained and an exploitable case due to a lack of consistency in the current file, lacking a GEIPAN on-site investigation. Consequently, the case is classified as D: unexplained.
