Summary: Simultaneous observations from two control towers in France and Spain of a stationary light phenomenon in the sky: astronomical observations of Capella.
--- seg-7fbd0c5567e12abcee9733e0 [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: This is routine for Emma in the Biarritz-Parme control tower on the morning of January 16, 2001. The day is dawning clear. Around 7:45, the phone rings. It's José from the San Sebastian Fuentarrabia control tower, observing a fixed bright point above the sea and seeking confirmation if the observation is also detected in Biarritz. Indeed, the phenomenon is detected, though no radar detection is recorded. The observed light is flickering, prompting the two controllers, who remain in telephone contact, to follow their established safety procedures. José, the Spanish air traffic controller, contacts Madrid air traffic control, which confirms no air traffic in the area, then the Bilbao military air control, which provides the same response. However, a plot is recorded by the Torrejon radar control. Is this the same phenomenon? Emma conducts equivalent investigations with the Bordeaux air traffic control, which reports nothing, nor do the military, also queried. The phenomenon disappears after about half an hour. All these information are faxed the same day to SEPRA, who in turn contacts the Taverny CCOA. The latter detected nothing unusual but is tasked with confirming the unknown radar detection made by Torrejon military. This is later denied by Torrejon. Within CNES, a check is launched to determine if a balloon was launched from Aire sur Adour. Again, the result is negative, leaving the observation shrouded in mystery. A decision is then made to investigate on-site on February 2, 2001. The SEPRA responsible interviews both witnesses in Biarritz and San Sebastian and they conduct a reconstruction and take measurements. These measurements, taken with a theodolite, provide crucial information. Indeed, the direction of observation of the phenomenon is identical from both Biarritz and San Sebastian, which are approximately 40 kilometers apart. The observed phenomenon is therefore very distant and cannot be explained by an aircraft. This case is classified B by SEPRA: probable astronomical observation.

REEXAMINING THE CASE: GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archives on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time as type 'B' cases, which are now being reexamined with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. This observation case is part of cases classified 'B' in 2001 by SEPRA. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this reexamination sometimes leads to new observations or explicit changes in classification in an investigation note. This case is consistent (two witnesses, both air traffic controllers), and the description of PAN by the witnesses is very precise. The sincerity and credibility of the witnesses have never been questioned. The described phenomenon shares many common characteristics (observation duration, shape, size, color) with a perfectly known astronomical object: the star Capella, which is experiencing exceptional scintillation due to particular atmospheric conditions. It is not the visual perception of the witnesses that is in question, but the interpretation they make of their observation through their feelings (surprise, night observation, unusual scintillation). In the current classification of GEIPAN, this case of low strangeness is classified as PAN A, an observation related to a misidentification with the star Capella.
