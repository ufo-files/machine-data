Summary: Long observations of the evolutions of luminous phenomena in the night sky: star observations.
--- seg-271b4ac2491371932a1748ec [needs-review] ---
Description: On August 27, 2017, around 10:30 PM, two witnesses in a natural setting observed the starry sky. They are intrigued by the jerky movements of luminous phenomena in a restricted portion of the sky. Two testimonies are collected. The observation reports very little strangeness: - when one intensely fixes on a star (T1 says "I started looking at a star" T2 says "T1 is fixated on a star"), it is classic to feel that it is moving, this is the phenomenon of autocinematic. In addition, the method of tracking the movement ("I took references in the sky, the clouds, or with my finger without moving it") is unreliable, especially in the presence of changing winds and thus irregular cloud movement (the Angoulême weather station recorded a 90-degree change between 10:00 PM and 11:00 PM (see Infoclimat for details). - That a star is accompanied by two less bright stars in a triangle position is not strange provided that the observation conditions are good and allow to distinguish the weak intensity stars. The effect of autocinematic motion is not necessarily the same for each star as it may depend on their intensity. - The observation lasted 40 minutes and the PAN moved from a high northern elevation to a lower elevation of 15° north, which corresponds to the movement of the stars (and rules out a satellite hypothesis). The fact that T2 confirms T1's perceptions regarding the movement does not objectively validate that movement. Kinesthetics provides a perception of motion, but its direction and intensity are poorly quantifiable and easily influenced by the perception of others. As it stands, to oppose the hypothesis of autokinematics, we would need a photo record, or two independent, precise, and timed observations showing concordance. Even in this case, the "cloud" origin of these movements would remain the most probable hypothesis given the tracking method used. One might wonder if prior knowledge of a seemingly even stranger testimony at the same location, also involving a triangle, has influenced the perception of strangeness of T1. Finally, the testimony is of average consistency in the sense that what makes it strange (the jerky movement, the formation of a triangle) is imprecisely geometric (no drawings or values for displacement and angular separations) and sequentially (we only know that it occurs within a 40-minute range). As a result, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: star observation.
