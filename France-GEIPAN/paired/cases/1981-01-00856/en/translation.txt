Description: In addition to processing standard witness statements, the GEIPAN is re-examining old cases previously classified as 'D' and published on its website. Thanks to new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or explicit changes in classification noted in an investigation report. This observation case, formerly named CLERY SAINT-ANDRE (45) 1981, is among cases classified 'D' by the GEPAN and recently re-examined. On January 29, 1981, around 7:15 PM, a witness noticed in the sky a very bright point they could not explain. The PAN displayed color variations and small movements. At 7:20 PM, the witness called the gendarmerie to report the incident. Two gendarmes immediately went to the main witness's home. Together, they observed the PAN until 9:00 PM. At the end of the observation, the PAN was still visible. A night service from another brigade later reported that the PAN was "still visible at midnight and 4 AM." Photos were taken by the gendarmes, but they turned out negative. The description of the observation locations and the PAN is precise, allowing for a more detailed analysis of the case (see investigation notes). It is worth noting that the witness's and gendarmes' statements are entirely consistent, demonstrating the reality of the phenomenon. The case is consistent (three witnesses, including two gendarmes), and the description of the PAN observed is fairly precise, except for the elevation in the sky, which can be considered low based on other indications (e.g., "between the horizon and the lowest star"). The described phenomenon shares many common characteristics (observation duration, shape, size, color) with a perfectly known astronomical object: Sirius. Indeed, Sirius is located exactly in the direction of the main observation (from 7 PM to 9 PM). The main witness rejects the hypothesis of a mistake with a star because the PAN changes color and moves erratically, but:

- Sirius's angular height remains low throughout the observation (20° to 9 PM), which can explain the twinkling (light ray passage through the atmosphere) according to a palette of colors entirely consistent with the witnesses' statements.
- The other unusual aspect of the movements (with limited amplitude) may be due to twinkling or could result from the autokinetic effect (see the article on the autokinetic effect). The mention of the PAN's presence at midnight and 4 AM is consistent with the Sirius hypothesis. In fact, this is noted only by another gendarmerie team that is not reported as having made the initial observation of the PAN. Sirius is no longer in the Southeast direction reported for the PAN. On the other hand, it is very likely that this second team noticed another oddity in the sky created by Jupiter in conjunction with Saturn. Present in the Southeast, Jupiter outshines other stars and displays a double glow that night due to the conjunction. This second team could have legitimately thought that this strange glow was that of the PAN reported in the Southeast at 9 PM. The very strong consistency between all the observations and the view Sirius could have provided during the observation period from 7 PM to 9 PM (and the view the conjunction Jupiter-Saturn could have provided at midnight and 4 AM) leaves little doubt about the mistake with Sirius for the main observation between 7 PM and 9 PM. The strangeness caused by Sirius is quite classic (twinkling and/or autokinetic effect) and remains at the origin of many mistakes (see on the GEIPAN site with the keyword Sirius, also see, for example, the Grand-Couronne (76) 17.04.2016 case). Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: Observation of Sirius.
