**Description:** On January 1st, 2026, between 06:35 and 06:40, a witness driving between Briis-sous-Forges and Limours (91) observed a circular, yellow-orange luminescent phenomenon in front of them. The light had a diffuse brightness that was not blinding, allowing it to be seen directly. The object, perceived as a sphere with slightly irregular contours, appeared at a low altitude and disappeared below the horizon after a few moments. The observation lasted less than a minute and occurred in a dark environment, just before dawn. The case consistency is considered good, despite relying on a single witness and lacking exploitable recordings. Information from the technical questionnaire, combined with sketches and the reconstruction of the journey, allows the witness's directional and angular estimates to be compared to astronomical data and atmospheric conditions at the time of the event.

According to GEIPAN criteria, consistency refers to the amount of reliable and objective information gathered from a witness's testimony. The witness's strange report mainly stems from the unusual appearance of the light source, its orange hue, and the impression of a rapid descending trajectory towards the horizon. The perception of a phenomenon accompanying the vehicle's movement also enhances the feeling of abnormal behavior.

However, analysis of directional and astronomical parameters reveals a close match between the witness's estimated position of PAN and the position of the Moon, which was approximately 309° azimuth and very low on the horizon at the time. The low real angular height, combined with clouds in that direction, explains the rapid disappearance of the phenomenon due to progressive occultation. The yellow-orange hue and the slightly altered contour appearance are also consistent with observing the Moon near the horizon under unfavorable atmospheric conditions.

The sensation of movement or accompaniment of the vehicle is due to a well-known perspective effect: the Moon's great distance causes its apparent position to change very little as the observer moves, unlike nearby landscape elements that pass quickly. None of the observed elements justify resorting to an alternative hypothesis. All available data converges towards identifying the observed phenomenon as the Moon during sunset. Therefore, the case is classified as A: Observation of the Moon.
