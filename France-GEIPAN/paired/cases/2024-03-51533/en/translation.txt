Summary: Observation of a bright PAN next to the full moon: phenomenon unidentified due to lack of data.
--- seg-0bfa1d1672fa55b2753b0551 [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On the evening of March 24, 2024, the witness is in their living room in Le Mans (72) when they observe the full moon and notice the presence of a PAN next to it, appearing as a golden yellow sphere. They take a photograph, with the window open, using their phone and see the PAN on the screen, then take a second image later, observing that the PAN has disappeared. Upon later reviewing the photos, they notice the PAN is also present in the second image, nearly indistinguishable from the moon disk. The witness did not visually observe the movement of the PAN, but deduced it from its successive positions in the photos (see investigation report). The consistency of this case is average, with only one witness, but with a field investigation, cognitive interview, and the presence of two photos that could be analyzed, though without metadata.

Using IPACO* analysis (see annex in the investigation report) of the two photos, the following was determined:

- The capture time (19:43, to the minute) thanks to the timestamp of the files recorded on the phone, as no metadata was generated on this older phone model.
- The impossibility, despite appearances, for the PAN to be a simple lens reflection.
- That the circular appearance of the PAN is likely an artifact, as it appears more punctual.
- The estimated angular distance traveled by the PAN, approximately 1.5°.
- Using Google Maps, the initial and final observation azimuts were determined, 99° and 100.5° respectively.

A field investigation on June 13, 2024, demonstrated a memory inversion regarding the order of the captures, ultimately showing the witness's perception of the PAN moving from left to right. It also established a precise timeline of events, likely with the witness initially intending to photograph the moon, not seeing the PAN, then observing it during the second capture and losing sight of it immediately after, obscured by trees.

Two hypotheses were evaluated: observation of a ship's lighthouse light and observation of an LED balloon.

- It was not possible to determine the presence of an aircraft in the observation axis at the time, using either CAPCODA* outputs (unexploitable due to scheduling issues) or private restitution sites.
- For the LED balloon hypothesis, the wind direction data was inconsistent with the movement of an object carried by the wind. However, a certain margin of error is to be considered, as the wind being recorded on the ground or immediately nearby, and the indicated azimuts could have slight deviations depending on the time.
- Additionally, it was confirmed during the field investigation that the movement and disappearance of the PAN were not directly observed by the witness, and the aspect depicted in the photos is misleading given the zoom used and the image sensor capabilities of the phone.

Based on these findings, the GEIPAN classifies this observation case as 'C': Unidentified phenomenon due to lack of data.
