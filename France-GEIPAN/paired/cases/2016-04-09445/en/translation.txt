Summary: Observation of a luminous object in the clear night sky with a particular scintillation and disappearance: astronomical misperception (Sirius).
--- seg-07f684dfa51d634665e029fe [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On Sunday, April 17, 2016, at approximately 9:12 PM, a couple was intrigued by the presence in a clear sky of a round and bright object with a peculiar shimmering of several colors. The Moon is visible to the left of the point, as well as another very bright star. A witness took a video (this video has been temporarily removed from publication due to a technical issue). The witnesses looked at the sky again around 10:25 PM but the object was gone. A colleague at work reportedly saw the same thing. Only one testimony was collected. The case is well documented (photo, video) and of low strangeness. The description of the phenomenon as a "pinhead" and the shimmering corresponds to that of a star. The witness was surprised by Sirius, which often happens. Sirius (El 17°, Az 214°) is in a direction consistent with the witness's description relative to the opposite roof (photo and sketch). Color changes can be explained by atmospheric turbulence. The witnesses noticed that, unlike the phenomenon that intrigued them, the "star near the Moon did not shimmer," which is accurate since it was Jupiter and the turbulence effect is not perceptible on planets (see the following article: scintillation). The movements "in a straight line from left to right, from front to back" are quite classic when observing a star. It's the autocinematic effect. The witness confirms that these movements remained of low amplitude. The lack of disconnection from a visual reference (opposite roof) reduces the possibilities of autocinematic without excluding it. A misidentification with the ISS is very unlikely. Indeed, the plane the witness saw above the PAN has been identified and the moment of passage in the direction of the PAN is posterior to the disappearance of the station. The hypothesis of an observation of Procyon should be eliminated. The deviation from the reference of the PAN above the roof is too significant, and especially the plane is seen above the PAN while its elevation (which can be precisely restored to 20°) is lower than that of Procyon. Sirius is indeed visible lower than the plane and attracts more attention and misidentifications than Procyon (because much brighter, more prone to color change and shimmering effects due to its lower elevation). At 10:25 PM, when they went back outside, the witnesses noticed the phenomenon was no longer there, which can be explained by the presence of a cloud (present that day) in front of the star, and even more certainly, in the hypothesis of Sirius, by its disappearance behind the roof (elevation then of 9°). Among these multiple hypotheses, Sirius stands out as a very strong hypothesis. The GEIPAN classifies the case as A: misidentification with Sirius.
