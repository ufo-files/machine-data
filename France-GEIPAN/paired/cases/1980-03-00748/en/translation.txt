**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. A case of observation (also known as VRIGNES AUX BOIS (08) 1980) was revisited in 2007, classified as C, and recently re-examined (as detailed in the investigation report).

On March 11, 1980, around 9:00 PM, a driver (T1) was alerted by his young son (T2) about a bright light phenomenon in the clear sky, described as "like a star." The object appeared to get closer, changing from the size of a coin to that of a plate, frightening the witnesses. In a panic, the driver turned around, and his son indicated that the point had turned red. The vehicle's engine stalled, and the lights went out. After several attempts, the vehicle started again. The phenomenon then flew overhead and gradually moved away. The frightened witnesses returned home. No unusual noises were heard.

The driver reported the incident to the gendarmerie at 10:30 PM, and an on-site investigation began at 11:40 PM. No traces on the ground or vegetation were found. No other testimonies were collected. The witnesses likely observed an aircraft approaching them with its landing lights or searchlight (helicopter) illuminated. This powerful light prevented the visual perception of any other lighting signals. Only the left navigation light was briefly seen by T2, either temporarily or towards the end of the observation, before the PAN disappeared from view.

No noise was perceived, but this factor is not decisive. It depends on various factors, such as the witnesses' individual auditory perception, the distance from the phenomenon, potential noises inside the car, etc. Given the unknown factors, wind speed and direction, and especially the witness's statement, "I didn't hear any noise from the phenomenon" (investigation report), it's unclear whether the observation was made with the car window open or closed.

The investigation demonstrated that the visibility conditions inside the witnesses' vehicle significantly limited their visual field, and the observation was made while the PAN remained low on the horizon, even after the driver turned around. It was likely still quite distant from the witnesses when they turned around, causing a chain of unrelated events (engine stalling, lights going out, not using a flashlight) due to their stress and panic.

The witnesses were completely overwhelmed by their stress and desire to leave the observation site as quickly as possible. The PAN, still far from the witnesses, had "all the time" to disappear from their visual field, possibly after turning a corner. T1 (the driver) claimed to have seen the red point of the PAN again, as his son had described. We showed that this red point, observed at that moment, was likely just the planet Mars, visible in conjunction with Jupiter, in the direction of observation (southeast), facing the witnesses.

In a state of panic and only thinking about going home, T1 didn't pay more attention to this final observation of Mars, which was probably very brief, preventing him from noticing that this "red point" wasn't moving and couldn't be linked to the PAN. The necessity for the witness to focus on driving, at night on a poor-quality road, combined with their stress, reinforces this point. The rapid and progressive disappearance of Mars could have been caused by passing a thick bank of fog, confirmed by meteorological data.

This disappearance contributes to the illusion of the PAN because the decrease in intensity can be interpreted as an increase in distance. The final confusion with the planet Mars renders invalid the second part of the PAN's trajectory as described in the two information sources related to the case, after its passage overhead of the witnesses' vehicle. They would have observed it only in the northwest direction.

Regarding the ancillary phenomena of the engine stalling and simultaneous extinction of the car lights, we showed that a malfunction causing the immediate and complete stop of both the engine and lights is highly improbable, given the vehicle's mechanical and electrical design. The hypothesis that the stalling was caused by T1's inattention while driving in a state of extreme stress is more plausible. It's also possible that, out of fear and unconscious protective reflex (not to be "noticed"), the witness deliberately turned off his lights and then turned them back on.

The fear of embarrassment, when retrospectively recalling this episode, might explain why T1 didn't explicitly mention it in front of the investigators. However, this remains highly hypothetical, as no concrete evidence supports this hypothesis.

Considering all these points, it now seems inappropriate to link the two events. The first is likely a simple aircraft observed under particular conditions (nighttime, face-on, with landing lights or searchlight illuminated) leading to a cascade of panic reactions from both witnesses, who were in an extreme stress state. The consistency is average. There's a lack of angular data (apparent dimensions of the PAN), testimonial data (windows open or closed, T2's position in the vehicle, T2's testimony), and photographic evidence (no photos or videos of the phenomenon).

The absence of these data neither confirms nor strongly supports the aircraft hypothesis, which remains sufficiently probable to be considered. The classification D (which was temporarily used for this case) is excluded due to this hypothesis, reducing the strangeness of the case. The GEIPAN classifies it as B: probable aircraft observation.
