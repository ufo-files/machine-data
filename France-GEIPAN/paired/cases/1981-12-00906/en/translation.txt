Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named LANN-BIHOUE (56) 1981 is part of a recent batch of cases that have been reassessed. On December 21, 1981, around 10:10 PM, a family observed for 10 minutes in the sky towards the southeast a round, orange-colored glowing object. The witness reported that the object moved at an extremely high speed with straight-line movements and sudden, sharp changes in direction. This point was surrounded by glowing fragments of the same color that alternately appeared and disappeared for about a dozen minutes. The phenomenon then disappeared towards the south, and a second glow was seen to the east at a lower altitude for a few seconds before completely vanishing. Only one testimony was collected. Regarding the main phenomenon (observed for 10 minutes), the witnesses likely observed the star Sirius, which at the time of observation was low on the southeastern horizon in the direction of the observation (as detailed in the investigation report). Favorable observation conditions with a clear sky, but with some passages of low clouds, may have caused this confusion, as the sparkling star could have been potentially obscured after about a dozen minutes by a bank of low clouds. The witness's description of the presence of glowing fragments aligns with the hypothesis, as at this elevation, the star sparkles randomly, its light rays passing through a thicker atmospheric layer more prone to atmospheric turbulence. The description of "movements both in position and direction, at a very high speed" also aligns with the hypothesis and supports it. Indeed, the fact that the witness felt the need to mention that "these movements tended to move towards the south" clearly indicates that the overall angular motion of the PAN over 10 minutes (observation time) remains weak. We can conclude that this is the natural physiological phenomenon of micro-ocular mobility ("micro-nystagmus"), poorly known to the general public, which occurs when fixating objects, especially at night. It gives the false impression that individual bright objects are affected by small aberrant movements, such as zigzagging. Two elements prevent us from firmly confirming this hypothesis: the witness does not indicate the altitude of the PAN in the sky, although we can infer from its comparison with a landing plane that it is a low altitude, compatible with that of Sirius. - The presence of some clouds. A cloud may have obscured Sirius, while in the certain absence of clouds at least in the direction, the fact that the witness does not notice Sirius in the vicinity of the PAN would be (further) evidence that the PAN and Sirius are confused. Nevertheless, considering all the other concordant elements, the hypothesis of Sirius has a probability well above 50% and should be retained. The second phenomenon is much more fleeting, and unlike the first, we have very little information to establish and test any hypothesis. The consistency of the testimony is average, with only one testimony while several witnesses were present, and partial and imprecise data for the first observation, and very insufficient for the second. Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the first phenomenon as B: Observation of Sirius. The testimony on the other phenomenon is unusable.
