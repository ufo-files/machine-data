**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reexamined to ensure more relevant conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this reexamination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named WORMHOUT (59) 1997 is part of a recent batch of cases reexamined. On October 22, 1997, around 8:22 a.m., a witness, driving for medical appointments, observed in the sky a rectangular, bright, yellowish-white light. After tending to a patient, both witnesses noticed the object was still there and had gotten closer to the house. The patient's husband, without stepping outside, also caught a glimpse of the object through the window. The witness continued their journey and noticed a bit further ahead a second bright object, slightly larger. Both objects disappeared at 08:40. This observation might have seemed strange due to static, disappearing bright objects observed by multiple people. Reexamination reveals significant inconsistencies in the testimonies:

- Only T1 is a spontaneous witness. T2 gave a statement to the gendarmes upon returning home. T2 states her husband saw the object inside through the window but did not move when T1 and T2 were outside observing the PAN.
- Large inconsistencies between witnesses: T1 states they left the T2 residence three times to observe the PAN, twice with T2 (before and after the appointment), while T2 says they did not leave after the appointment. T1 describes a rectangular object that did not change shape before reaching T2's and during two outings, then changed into a disk on the third outing. T2 provides no shape description and only positions herself based on T1's statements made before "I can't tell if it's an oval or rectangular object." The T2 husband is cited by T2 as having seen the object move in the sky, while T2 mentions nothing about movement and T1 states the object was static during the first two outings. T2 states they saw the object disappear with T1 during their outing, while T1 never saw it disappear (it was no longer there or in the same place with the same shape during the third outing).
- Little information about the object's position in the sky. It is understood that at 8:25 a.m., T1 and T2 were observing the sky together in the direction that was likely east or southeast because T2 mentions "in the direction of Esquelbec" (155°) and T1 indicates the object moved towards southeast during the final observation phase. We can assume T2 did not share T1's perception of strangeness and only cooperated minimally with their testimony after being prompted by the gendarmes.

T1 describes a very bright, yellowish-white PAN, while T2 describes an object that shone like the sun. At 8:25 a.m., the sun is in the east (106°) and slightly below the horizon (-1°). It is not yet visible but can brightly illuminate a cloud located in the east or southeast and isolated in the morning blue sky (T1 describes the object in a blue sky). This cloud hypothesis can explain the disappearance of the PAN between T1's second and third outings, as well as its reappearance (it could also be another cloud) later during the third outing in a changed disk shape. The final perception of the PAN's oval movement might be due to T1's own car movement. It is likely that the perfect rectangular shape and strong glow of the cloud misled T1. As for T2, this could be a case of minimal complicity with T1. In the emotion of the moment, T1 drew T2 and her husband into this testimony that would not have been given spontaneously. The same emotion later led T1 to repeat their mistake with this cloud or another with a different shape. This set of testimonies lies at the border between the unexploitable due to low consistency and a probable explanation (cloud). The GEIPAN classifies the case as B: probable cloud observation.
