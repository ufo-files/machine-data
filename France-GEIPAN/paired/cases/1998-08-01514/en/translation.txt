Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over all these years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named [AERO AOM] LAT 27.58, LON -33.35 (INT) on 26 August 1998 is part of a recent set of cases that have been reassessed. On August 26, 1998, at 00:32, the crew of a DC10 observed, for approximately 10 seconds, the very fast passage of six bright points of light with a very intense visible radiation, leaving behind a long trail of light. The trajectory is horizontal and straight. The only witness, the captain, wrote a report on August 28, 1998, which was later transmitted to SEPRA. However, a sketch depicting the PAN was made by four out of the five witnesses, all of whom were included in the report. We can affirm that the observation likely concerns the atmospheric re-entry of a meteoroid, fractured into a meteor shower (see the investigation report). The horizontal trajectory reported by the witnesses suggests it could be a type of low-flying meteoroid. However, without more data to perform more precise calculations, we cannot definitively determine if this meteoroid actually passed through the Earth's atmosphere or if it merely bounced off of it, as the 1972 meteor in the USA did. The observation duration, magnitude, and portion of the sky traversed make this case similar to a low-flying meteoroid event that occurred in October 1990. More generally, the witnesses' description aligns well with the hypothesis: white color, straight trajectory, very fast movement, very bright trails. One might wonder why this truly rare and exceptional phenomenon did not receive more attention at the time. However, this fleeting observation (10 seconds) took place in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean, far from any land-based detection stations and visual observers, so it was probably only observed by the crew. The consistency is good, with a well-detailed testimony, particularly regarding the initial and final positions of the PAN and their physical description (appearance, magnitude, etc.). It is unfortunate, however, that we do not have testimonies from the other crew members. Additionally, no photographic or video records of the phenomenon were made (which is common for such a brief duration). Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as B: observation of meteoroids.
