Description: At 10:15 PM, a witness located in the backyard of their home in QUIÉVY (59) observed, in a cloudy sky, a "huge" or "round spot" white light, which they placed above the clouds. The phenomenon moved quickly from northwest to southeast, paused for three seconds, then resumed its course towards southeast. The witness estimated the apparent size of this "spot" to be approximately 30 mm (measured using a rule held at arm's length). The observation lasted a total of about 10 seconds. The consistency* of this case is judged average, with one unique witness and no photos or videos of the PAN.

According to the criteria of GEIPAN, consistency is the amount of reliable and objective information gathered for a testimony. The appearance of the PAN described by the witness corresponds to that of a beam projector aimed at the sky, commonly known as a "sky-tracer." This type of equipment, used for event or festival displays, is a favored explanation in this case (see the investigation report). The essential strangeness for the witness lies in the phases of movement and stop of the phenomenon. These characteristics of movement are consistent with the operation of a "sky-tracer," capable of rapid scans in all directions and prolonged periods of immobility.

Although the witness considered the hypothesis of a projector, they rejected it, thinking the source was above the clouds. This impression is a common optical illusion generated by the distance between the ground-based source and the cloud layer, as well as by the density of the clouds. The power of the beam gives the light spot a diffuse appearance and blurred contours, leading the witness to believe it is moving in altitude, beyond the cloud cover. See examples on the Sky Misperceptions website. This perception is further reinforced by the invisibility of the beam caused by the low concentration of suspended particles and excellent horizontal visibility, preventing the physical connection between the ground-based projector and the light spot on the clouds from being distinguished.

However, the investigation did not allow for the identification of the user of such a projector on the night of the observation. Such a device could have been tested very briefly that evening by a community, association, or even an individual, before being used for a longer period the following weekend for a festive occasion. The GEIPAN classifies this case as "B": probable observation of a sky-tracer.
