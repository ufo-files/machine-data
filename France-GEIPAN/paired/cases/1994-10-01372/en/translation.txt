Summary: Long observations of silent, low-altitude movements of a luminous object: probable observations of a Mylar balloon.
--- seg-23d26a5dbb2eae1cb8d66438 [needs-review] ---
Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over many years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named LAVOUTE-CHILHAC (43) 09.10.1994 is part of a recent set of cases that have been reassessed. On October 9, 1994, around 9:45 a.m., a witness (T1), posted during a hunting drive, observed for 25 to 30 minutes the silent movements of a white cylindrical, metallic-reflecting, luminous object. The witness also noticed blue and red lights. He followed it with his eyes until it was out of sight. Later, meeting his brother (T2), the latter told him that around 9:30 a.m., near his home, he had also seen a luminous object that intrigued him. T2 described a cylindrical object with a semi-circular lower part, reflecting the sun and moving across the landscape. After 30 minutes of observation, this witness decided to stop following the object. The physical characteristics (shape, but especially the metallic appearance and the "silver reflections" mentioned by the witnesses) and dynamic characteristics (slow movements, mainly horizontal, close to the ground and in a valley, generated by local weather conditions in a mountainous area in the morning) are entirely consistent with the hypothesis of confusion with a metallic nylon balloon, type "Mylar". One of the witnesses (T2) also thought it was a balloon (see the investigation report). At the beginning of the observation, the sun had not yet had time to warm the closest parts of the atmosphere to the ground, especially those sheltered in valleys. Thus, the Mylar balloon is still subject to local sunlight conditions, weak or non-existent, and to very weak local currents, causing it to "wander" with these currents, remaining close to the ground. This state persists until the sun rises higher, warming the balloon's envelope, causing the contained air to expand. This results in the balloon becoming lighter than the surrounding air and thus leaving the ground to slowly and regularly ascend in altitude, as described by witness T1 at the end of the observation. It is quite plausible that this balloon was already present the previous evening. It was a Saturday, and the balloon could have been launched from a neighboring village. The temperature drop during the night caused the air it contained to contract, bringing it down to the ground in a valley, from which it only emerged the next morning, when temperature conditions became favorable. The case's consistency is good, with two very precise independent testimonies and a gendarmerie investigation on the spot, which established a situation plan. During the observation, the GEIPAN had little or no experience with balloon cases. This is no longer the case (search on the www.geipan website with the keywords "balloon" or "Mylar"). Therefore, the GEIPAN classifies the case as B: probable observation of a Mylar balloon.
