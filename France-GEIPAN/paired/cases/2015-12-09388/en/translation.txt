Summary: Observation of the silent movement of an orange light phenomenon in the night sky: probable observation of Thai lanterns.
--- seg-8abc252cb195c2ffad9fb7da [machine-unreviewed] ---
**Description:** On December 19, 2015, at 8:17 PM, a witness, alerted by his son, observed from a distance a large, slightly oval, glowing ball with a very bright orange color. The ball moved across the sky towards the witness. He then distinguished a widened "V" profile with small, regularly spaced lights, and took three photographs. The object passed directly overhead without any sound. The trajectory was straight, then took a slight upward slope as it moved away; the PAN dimmed and eventually disappeared. The witness's son saw three distinct phenomena. Only one testimony was collected. The observation of orange balls crossing the sky in a few minutes, one weekend night, strongly suggests Thai lanterns, likely released nearby after a private party leading up to the end-of-year holidays. The movement was observed from the southwest, which is consistent with the southwest wind recorded in Salon de Provence: the Infoclimat record indicates a wind from sector 110° at 6-11 km/h. The only usable photo from the witness is defocused (focus blur, very common in night photos under these conditions) and only shows a blurred, orange circle. It does, however, prove the reality of the phenomenon, and the color of the phenomenon is entirely consistent with that of Thai lanterns. The distance estimate made by the witness based on his aeronautic experience cannot be accurate since he had never seen this type of object before. Due to the inherent low brightness of Thai lanterns (large candle flame), they are always perceived much farther away than they actually are, and thus seem much larger and faster than they truly are. See Estimation of size and distance of an unknown phenomenon. The very regular movement of the objects in the sky is entirely normal for an object carried by a light wind. Since lanterns are often released one after the other, after the same delay corresponding to the time of the burner ignition, the result is an organized file of flying lights. Due to their low intrinsic brightness, Thai lanterns, intuitively compared to airplane lights, are most often interpreted by witnesses as being much farther away than they actually are, and thus much faster. The GEIPAN classifies this case as "B": probable observation of Thai lanterns.
