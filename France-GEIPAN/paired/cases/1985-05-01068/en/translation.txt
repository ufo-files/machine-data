**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reexamined with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this reexamination sometimes leads to new observations or even a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named LA TRINITE PORHOUET (56) 1985 is part of a recent batch of cases reexamined.

During the night of May 25-26, 1985, between 11:00 PM and 1:30 AM, four witnesses observed various light phenomena. First (Phase 1), witnesses in a vehicle were intrigued by numerous flashes of light in different colors across the sky from various points along their route. Upon returning to their family property (Phase 2), the witnesses, gazing at the starry sky, noticed two bright points moving at equal distances from each other at variable speeds. These points then disappeared, hidden by the landscape. Then (Phase 3), through a gap in the trees, they saw a red, rectangular light object at the top of a hill. Terrified, the witnesses returned to their home and discussed what they had seen without understanding it. They finally heard a deep rumble (Phase 4) and noticed a total silence in the countryside that alarmed them. After emerging a few minutes later, they found nothing. The witnesses went to the local gendarmerie the next day. No other group of witnesses regarding these nighttime phenomena was found among the local population. There is a sequence of observations in four phases, each leading to different descriptions of the PAN (see the investigation report).

The starting point (Phase 1) is likely due to lightning from a storm (recorded by weather reports in the West that evening), which the witnesses could not recognize with certainty (although the hypothesis was formulated) for multiple reasons: the witnesses are unfamiliar with the area, the lightning is very far away while the local sky is clear and not stormy, and distance prevents them from hearing thunder. The following three phases are the subject of fleeting, imprecise, or contradictory descriptions that do not allow for a sufficient probable explanation to be established. Nevertheless, none of these observations seem strange, as there is at least one explanation for each that the limited information available cannot confirm or refute:

- The description of the PAN in Phase 2 resembles the lights of a single airplane, which, under certain observation conditions and depending on the type and state of the lights present, can take on a somewhat peculiar appearance.
- The description of the PAN in Phase 3 could correspond to a break in a red moon through the clouds.
- The description of the noise in Phase 4 (no visual observation as the witnesses were indoors) could correspond to a rumble of thunder.

The entire observation may have seemed strange at the time due to its apparently complex nature and its doubled strangeness, as at least perceived by the witnesses. The accumulated experience since GEIPAN leads to thinking of a triggering phenomenon. The witnesses may have been placed in a climate of strangeness by their first observation (here probably amplified collectively since none of the witnesses could bring moderation by developing the initial hypothesis of lightning), leading them subsequently to scan the sky and associate other phenomena with this initial strangeness that might have gone unnoticed without it.

The fact is that the gendarmes conducted investigations "with villagers and farmers to verify the testimonies" and found nothing, even though that Saturday evening corresponded to the "fair" in the town of Josselin (56) and was thus conducive (not only for the witnesses) to many late-night outings, including on the roads taken by the witnesses. The consistency of the observation is poor. The data collected are too imprecisely, or even contradictorily, recorded when they are not entirely absent. For example, essential elements such as angular measurements, initial and final observation directions, observation durations, etc., are missing.

However, the available elements allow us to establish:

- The first observation is likely caused by lightning from a storm.
- The other observations are not very strange, would probably not have existed without the persistent strangeness created by the first observation, and cannot be explained due to the lack of reliable information.

Consequently, GEIPAN concludes in B (lightning) for the PAN in Phase 1 and C for the rest of the observations.
