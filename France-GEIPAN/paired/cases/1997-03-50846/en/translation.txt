Description: GEIPAN continues to publish all its archives on its public website www.geipan.fr. Its publications include older cases classified at the time (A, B, C or D) that are now being re-examined solely to improve the pertinence of the conclusions. Thanks to new technical resources (software) and the investigative experience acquired over all these years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or even a change in classification. This observation case, previously classified D and formerly named MONTPELLIER (34) 1997, is one of a group of recently re-examined cases. It proves to be a combination of 4 different observations, made at different times and allowing as many possible explanatory hypotheses (see the investigation report). GEIPAN's perception of strangeness at the time arose from not recognising the multiplicity of both observations and phenomena. Reading gendarmerie PV 019/97 shows that the gendarmerie initiated the investigation following a regional newspaper report on March 18, 1997, mentioning an unidentified aerospace phenomenon (PAN) reported above Montpellier on the morning of March 17 by several airline pilots. This morning observation has since been explained by GEIPAN as a probable atmospheric entry of a meteoroid; see the case [RENTREE ATMOSPHERIQUE] (REG) OCCITANIE, PACA (OCC, PAC) 17.03.1997. The gendarmerie's search for additional witnesses to the morning observation thus collected these accounts of several evening observations. The context of the newspaper report and the active gendarmerie investigation may have made the evening witnesses perceive greater strangeness; outside this context, perhaps not all of them would have reported spontaneously. Indeed, the observations from Mauguio (34) T1, Cabestany (66) and Montpellier (34) are not very strange. Only the observation from Mauguio (34) T2 has an initially strange aspect requiring detailed analysis. The observation in the MAUGUIO (34) 17.03.1997 T1 account strongly suggests a misidentification of a satellite. The observation in the MONTPELLIER (34) 17.03.1997 account very strongly suggests an astronomical misidentification: Sirius's position is highly consistent with that of the PAN. The observation in the CABESTANY (66) 17.03.1997 account is a probable misidentification of a Mylar balloon reflecting street lighting below its path. This conclusion rests on the following points: - The balloon's actual shape cannot be observed at night; only the reflections can be seen. The shape initially observed with the naked eye is curved, consistent with the balloon's possible round or oval shape, visible only through surface reflections of ground lights. The witness then takes binoculars and distinguishes several dome-shaped luminous patches corresponding to different reflections on the balloon from several streetlights, which are not separable with the naked eye; - Globe-type streetlights, casting light skywards, are confirmed in housing developments in the immediate vicinity; - The path is regular in speed and altitude and consistent with the prevailing wind direction; - Such a balloon could have been used during the week for a particular private celebration, such as a birthday, or during the preceding weekend, because Mylar balloons last a long time. The observation in the MAUGUIO (34) 17.03.1997 T2 account is a probable misidentification of an MD-80 aircraft, with a particular lighting system, that had just taken off from Montpellier airport. This conclusion rests particularly on the following points: - The aircraft's actual shape could not be observed because of the distance and nighttime conditions. Only its powerful landing lights were seen, and these were described consistently with the MD-80: point-like lights at the "extremities" and a rectangular central light. The mention of an oval shape in the photograph in the official report and in the witness's statement should therefore be attributed more to interpretation when faced with something unexplained; - The colours described are normally used in aviation, although the witness describes the initial lights as "blue-white", and blue is not used in aviation. It could nevertheless be a predominantly white colour tinged with blue; - The repeated switching off and on of the landing lights may have resulted from the pilot's need to check visibility during and after the turn. The MD-80 allows switching between the two types of lights described; - The MD-80's particular lighting system, in which most wingtip lights are close together and flashing red anti-collision lights are present on the fuselage, together with the aircraft's distance and changing heading, could have caused the witness to observe a change from white (the initially dominant headlights directed towards the witness), to orange (mixed colour), then red (the red anti-collision lights becoming dominant), without the flashing being visible; - The PAN's positions, movements and path are entirely consistent with an Air Liberté aircraft recorded by radar, which had just taken off from Montpellier airport and was turning and climbing simultaneously to reach its heading towards Paris Orly. Air Liberté had just begun using MD80 aircraft on this route; - The absence of noise is explained both by the considerable observation distance and by the wind blowing towards the aircraft; - The initial strangeness perceived by the witness could have arisen because, although familiar with the location, he had never before observed an MD-80 taking off. It was then heightened by the chance coincidence between switching on his torch and the aircraft's nose landing lights coming on. The substance of each account is moderate or weak because of imprecision about times, durations and sky positions. Nevertheless, they are sufficient for a conclusion given the limited strangeness remaining after analysis. Consequently, GEIPAN classifies the cases as follows: - MAUGUIO (34) 17.03.1997 T1: B, probable observation of a satellite; - MAUGUIO (34) 17.03.1997 T2: B, probable observation of an aircraft taking off; - CABESTANY (66) 17.03.1997: B, probable observation of a Mylar balloon; - MONTPELLIER (34) 17.03.1997: A, very probable observation of a star, specifically Sirius.
