Description: On March 28, 2016, a storm hit the Blain region (44). After reclining and making a phone call to EDF to report a power outage, the witness (T1) observes around 03:30 a white light coming from the outside. Through the window, he sees a white ball 2 meters from the ground. He decides to go upstairs to get a better view of the PAN through a velux. Rejoined by his family, everyone observes this white light. The witness then went outside their home, but the phenomenon had disappeared. No particular noise was heard during the slow lateral movement of this white ball. The witness remains outside and then observes a blue-green light in a single flash at 100 meters above ground on one side of their farm. Five minutes later, another light but of orange, coral-yellow color appeared on the other side of his farm in a single flash as well. A shadow is observed by three witnesses (T2, T3, T4) on the property, then the colored flashes reappear. The witnesses are frightened and remain intrigued by these phenomena. The analysis of the testimonies and the search for explanatory hypotheses led to distinguishing two phases in this observation. This distinction is necessary due to an interruption of the phenomenon followed by a radical change in the appearance of the PAN (color, size, number). It allows for evaluating hypotheses in light of the entire observation (two phases) or solely of one or the other of the phases (see the investigation report). Phase 1 concerns observation as long as the PAN is seen by witnesses as a white light and until its observed disappearance. Phase 2 begins with the observation of multiple lights and distinct colors until the end. In the end, only one hypothesis can be retained, and only for phase 1: that of the globular lightning. The description of the PAN in this phase is highly compatible with the expert characterization (read the presentation on the Aspects, Manifestations, and Classification of Ball Lightning), and the stormy or stormy context further corresponds to a production condition. The second phase is longer but also more confusing in its description. It may possibly be attributed to a combination of lights produced by a distant thunderstorm and light reflections (on the ground but distant) on the cloud layer (intervention vehicle, Donges refinery in the southwest, city halo (Nantes/St-Nazaire), ...). Even the Moon (~full), normally hidden by clouds, could have momentarily inserted itself into the spectacle, via a passing gap. But the compatibility analysis with descriptions or the likelihood assessment in terms of place and date does not provide any of these hypotheses with sufficient probability to be retained. The consistency (level and reliability of the information) is strong in the initial phase because the PAN is close and has a well-defined shape. In the second phase, the descriptions are more vague, and sometimes even contradictory. Emotion is also stronger there. In the annals of the GEIPAN (with nearly 4000 observation cases), it is common to encounter situations where an initial perception of strangeness creates in the witness a comprehensible state of alert (on a background of emotion that can reach panic) and which can lead them to associate with this initial strangeness (and thus amplify it) other strangeness or subsequent information that might not have even been perceived (or might not have caused concern) in the absence of the initial strangeness. The situation with multiple witnesses sharing their emotions in real-time greatly facilitates the "avalanche" phenomenon of the strange experience. It is possible that this occurred here between phase 1 (initial strangeness) and phase 2. The distancing of T4 (much less strange experience) also relativizes the objective strangeness of the observations of the second phase. The divergence T4 presents on a key element (colors of the lights) of T2 and T3, which penalizes an explanatory hypothesis (lightning lights), constitutes another element undermining consistency. The purpose of a cognitive interview GEIPAN conducted on-site is to create conditions that allow the witness to return as closely as possible to their initial perception and prevent (or at least detect the signs of) factors that alter that initial perception (false memories, interpretations including the avalanche effect of the perceived strangeness, influence between witnesses, especially when they shared the emotion in real-time and then exchanged information before the investigation …). A cognitive interview is thus a means of increasing or at least assessing the consistency (level of information and reliability of the information) of (a set of) testimonies. The very foundation of this cognitive interview is to be specific and tailored to each witness, isolating any potential active or passive influences from other witnesses. It wasn't possible here; the investigators perceived a family too "close-knit or supportive" in the emotion of this observation, even 6 months later, to risk an additional emotional strain by separating them. In doing so, the collective interview was able to bring forth additional information without being able to favor or assess the overall consistency. In the end, we have no explanation for the observation in phase 2, but the consistency on this phase is too weak to validate an unexplained character for this observation. Accordingly, the GEIPAN classifies the case as: - B for phase 1: probable observation of a globular lightning. - C for phase 2: lack of reliable information.
