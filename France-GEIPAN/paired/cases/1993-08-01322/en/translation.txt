Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archives on its public website www.geipan.fr. In its publications, it includes older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in the conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over all these past years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or even a change in classification. This previously classified D case named SAINT-GAUDENS (31) 14.08.1993 is part of a recent set of cases reviewed (see investigation report). On Saturday, August 14, 1993, around 10 PM, several people in two different locations observed an unusual phenomenon towards the Pyrenees. Three witnesses report their observations to the Gendarmerie. The described phenomenon is a circular, red glowing point that is stationary to the right of Mont Valier (for T1 and T3) and in the direction of Cagire (for T2). One of the witnesses (T3) takes a pair of binoculars and observes 5 red-orange glowing points inside the mass. The observations lasted approximately 10 minutes, then the point disappeared in a few seconds. The entire oral statement of T2 is perfectly compatible with a phenomenon that would be solely on the ground. In fact, it is perceived "in the woods" at first and then "descending into the Pyrenees mountain range, in a horizontal movement." It is worth considering whether this also applies to other testimonies, in which case the search for an explanation would exceed the sole competence of the GEIPAN. - T1 and T3, whose observation points are close, place the PAN at the height of Mont Valier, slightly to the right, according to their statements or drawings. Only T3 mentions the sky for the end of its observation with the expression "the strange phenomenon disappeared horizontally in the sky." That does not mean the PAN was in the open sky. Mount Valier is not an isolated peak and extends to its right with a visible ridge line at a slightly lower angular height, as seen from the observation location (Saint-Gaudens). Above all, there are several reliefs in the foreground of Mont Valier that are also visible at the same angular height on the right. One notes in particular the mountain bar at the level of the Summit of Cournudère (1563 m). The drawings of T1 and T3 may refer to one of the successive relief lines but certainly not to the horizon line, which T1 and T3 may not have even distinguished. - T2 and T3 orally describe a horizontal disappearance ("disappeared while sinking into the Pyrenees mountain range, in a horizontal motion"). Or, the drawings of T1 and T2 taken alone, without knowledge of the mention "à l’horizontal," could lead one to think of an ascent into the sky. The oral mention "horizontal" cannot in any way apply to a PAN that has been seen at increasing angular elevation. Therefore, it should be understood that witnesses T2 and T3 intended in their drawings to represent a horizontal displacement that mainly involves a change in distance. This last observation cannot be directly translated into a flat drawing, so the two witnesses represent it in perspective as a cone with PAN of decreasing size. They wanted to represent a retreat in distance rather than an ascent in elevation. This distance escape might only be an illusion. Indeed, witnesses perceiving a dimming of a PAN's light, whose actual distance they cannot gauge, may interpret this as an actual decrease in light emission from the PAN that is stationary or as a distance increase from a PAN that has constant light. The GEIPAN experience shows that the context of strangeness experienced very often leads the witness to interpret the object as moving when, in fact, the PAN is proven to be stationary. The T1 witness testimony introduces a form of inconsistency more difficult to mitigate. T1 does not orally describe any movement of the PAN, including during its disappearance. The "forward tilt" associated with the appearance of "three beams of light" can only be understood as a perception of a change in orientation of the PAN since it was already perceived with an oriented shape ("crushed oval"). The main drawing is consistent with the account, with a fixed point (no angular movement). On the other hand, the secondary drawing mentions a "change in course" with an arrow pointing upwards. In this context, it can be thought that T1 also wanted to express, in perspective, a retreat in distance, but the inconsistency prevents a conclusion. The search for an explanation therefore goes beyond the sole competence of the GEIPAN since the observation, once the unusable portion of T1's testimony is set aside, is compatible with a totally ground-based phenomenon. The GEIPAN at the time should not have classified this case as D, as nothing indicates that it is an Aerospatial Phenomenon. Despite all, the GEIPAN can provide some clues for investigating a ground phenomenon, without being able to claim to define the scope of the search (see the investigation report). However, this investigation faces the fact that the observation direction of T2, as reported by the Pic de Cagire, does not align with those of T1 and T3, which were reported as being to the right of Mont Valier, except under the assumption that T2 designated the Pic de Cagire as a reference point instead of Mont Valier. Nothing allows for confirming or denying such confusion. The angular difference between the two peaks is indeed small (20°), but for a witness familiar with the area, the two landmarks are quite distinct, even at night. A more in-depth investigation at the time would have allowed the identification of this discrepancy, sought clarifications in the testimonies of the witnesses, and either eliminated or, on the contrary, confirmed this discrepancy. At this stage, this discrepancy adds to the strangeness as the match in appearance, time, and duration suggests a phenomenon of the same nature that would have occurred in two different locations. An absence of explanation after a search that also utilizes all the skills in the search for land phenomena (and not just aerospace phenomena) would in turn create a level of demand for consistency in testimonies (level of information and assessment of its reliability) that is unlikely to be met 25 years after the events, leading to the uninvestigable rather than the unexplained. As a result, the case is classified as C: Unexploitable as it exceeds the sole competence of GEIPAN.
