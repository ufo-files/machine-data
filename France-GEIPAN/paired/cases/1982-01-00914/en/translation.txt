**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions' relevance. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named MARTIGUES (13) 1982 is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases.

On January 14, 1982, around 6:40 a.m., a driver on the Martigues road at Saint-Mitre-Les-Remparts (13) was intrigued by a luminescent phenomenon of a color that seemed to follow his movements, remaining stationary when he stopped. No noise was heard. Only one testimony was collected at the gendarmerie. This testimony, as reported in the gendarmerie protocol, exhibits several typical characteristics of an astre (star) observation. These characteristics are now well-known to the GEIPAN with the accumulation of explained cases of this type, particularly over the last decade (see www.geipan.fr and search for cases involving astre phenomena, comets, moons, or sun).

The typical aspect here is the perception of being followed by the PAN:
- The PAN remains stable when the witness stops and moves when the witness does. The star's direction is fixed in absolute terms. When the car follows a roughly constant course, the star appears fixed relative to the vehicle and moves across the landscape. Since the witness was already surprised by the PAN's appearance and considers it at a close distance, this movement along the landscape is interpreted as the PAN moving parallel to the vehicle, mirroring its speed, here to the left of the witness on the road.

If the car changes direction, the star's direction changes relative to the vehicle and the road, potentially leading the PAN to cross the road (as in this case). The point of misperception by the PAN as a follower is the failure to recognize the star. This is fairly common with bright stars at low elevation, where atmospheric thickness alters colors, creates twinkling (or here, "flashing lights"), and perceptions of scattered or slightly moving lights.

Observing through a window or windshield also causes the star to appear as multiple bright points or display different colors, regardless of the star's elevation. On the morning of the observation, several stars could have been the source of the misperception, at low elevation (Capella 12° and 323° azimuth) or at higher elevation (Jupiter 32° and 165° azimuth, Vega 32° and 6° azimuth).

The hypothesis is plausible but cannot be confirmed because another characteristic of the observation is its lack of precision. Elevations and azimuths of the PAN are not indicated, nor is the exact location and direction of travel of the witness. If the witness was traveling towards Istres (which is more plausible as they are based in Istres and traveling to work), the cap would oscillate between 285° and 360° (depending on their exact position on the road). Capella would be a hypothesis as it did indeed cross the road from left to right several times, ending up as much as 40° to the left of the vehicle axis.

If the witness was traveling towards Martigues (more plausible as they are based in Istres), the cap would oscillate between 180° and 105°. Jupiter at 165° azimuth could indeed cross the road, ending up as much as 15° to the left of the vehicle axis. The testimony's statement, "very low on my left," seems a bit vague and could correspond to either of the above situations, particularly the second one, which is the more plausible direction of travel.

However, the witness did not undergo a detailed gendarmerie questionnaire or GEIPAN protocol (only 10 free-form lines by the police) to clarify positions and directions, so it's not ruled out that the witness took an alternate route. The testimony is not precise enough to validate or invalidate the star hypothesis. It's not particularly unusual, though, as there's also a hypothesis of a helicopter. Far enough away, this helicopter might be unrecognizable and inaudible, creating a perception of a following sphere during the witness's movement.

The short duration of the observation makes this helicopter hypothesis plausible, and again, the testimony does not allow for validation or invalidation. Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as C: lack of reliable information.
