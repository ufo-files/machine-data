**Case Description:**

The witness, an amateur astronomer and astrophotographer, noticed during a photo sorting session over 4 days a bright, star-like white point appearing in only one image from a batch of 170. The witness precisely determined the celestial coordinates and exposure time of this PAN. They conducted research to identify the phenomenon, utilizing several technical databases and celestial catalogs. They contacted various specialized groups like Webastro, Hoys Citizen Science Project, and the French and Touraine Astronomy Society, but did not receive a satisfactory response. The case consistency is excellent thanks to the photo provided by the witness, allowing precise localization of the phenomenon; however, the timing is imprecise due to the 5-minute exposure time required to capture the image. The light event occurred during these 5 minutes, but it's challenging to pinpoint the exact timing.

The PAN was not observed with the naked eye but only through instrumentation, an astronomical telescope equipped with a dedicated camera. The observation duration is lengthy enough for numerous low-luminosity satellites to orbit above the witness. The GEIPAN contacted the space surveillance service of the CNES (Operational Orbitography Center). Typically, the GEIPAN only deals with direct naked-eye observations, but after receiving the questionnaire and supporting materials, an investigation was conducted because the photos and information provided by the witness correspond to a classic PAN phenomenon—a reflection of sunlight off a satellite. The data is notably precise and detailed.

Given the briefness of the event, two initial hypotheses were considered: a satellite flash or a gamma-ray burst astronomical phenomenon. The latter hypothesis involved observing the visible spectrum portion of the gamma-ray burst signal. The observation duration under analysis is very short, too short. This hypothesis was quickly dismissed. The GEIPAN suggested submitting the video to their astronomy expert, but no known astronomical events were recorded in the observation direction and time that night. Photo analysis revealed a faint trail seemingly coinciding with the PAN (see attached documents).

For the satellite flash observation hypothesis, the GEIPAN initially searched a database and their dedicated "astronautical environment" tool for a satellite with characteristics matching the PAN. Due to a lack of results, the space surveillance service of the CNES was contacted. An initial Starlink satellite, not properly referenced (orbital elements outdated), was ultimately not confirmed after analyzing its actual trajectory. Further searches within the time frame (image exposure duration) found another satellite. However, this satellite was in the shadow zone, not illuminated by the sun, and thus invisible from the observation location.

Finally, after searching at a much higher altitude, it was determined that the observation very likely involved a reflection of sunlight off a second stage of a DELTA rocket. Trajectory reconstruction of the visible trail on the photo supports this hypothesis. The object was orbiting at 20:22:10 UTC at an altitude of 6780 km and was 0.5 degrees from the estimated observation direction for that time. The margin of error is attributed to uncertainties regarding the exact timing of the flash appearance during the 300-second exposure.

This case is classified in category A: observation of a satellite flash, specifically a reflection of sunlight off a DELTA rocket stage. The case analysis resulted from collaborative efforts between the Institute for Astrophysics and Planetary Science (IRAP) and the space surveillance service (Operational Orbitography Center) of the CNES. This study highlighted, among other things, a problem with public applications updating planned versus actual satellite orbits for newly launched satellites.
