**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions. This re-examination, facilitated by new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, sometimes results in new observations or explicit changes in classification noted in investigation notes.

This case of observation previously classified as D and named NEUVY SUR BARANGEON (18) 1987 is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases (refer to investigation notes). On August 18, 1987, around 10:15 PM, a resident (T1) of NANCAY (18) and two residents (T2 and T3) of VOUZERON (18) observed from their homes a luminous phenomenon in the sky heading north-northwest. T1 described "two lights" moving together silently. T2 described the PAN as a "bright white glow" in the shape of a "lit cigar" making a turn to leave for Vierzon. T3 described the PAN as a "large bright white glow," and after careful observation, he saw "two smaller bright white lights" and a "flashing red light" located below the PAN. T3 confirmed the turn and its departure towards Vierzon. Although the witnesses describe the same appearance direction for the PAN, it's unclear if they observed the same thing, as the descriptions of the PAN seen differ, especially between T1 on one hand and T2 and T3 on the other.

The witnesses are convinced they saw a "OVNI." However, the observation exhibits many characteristics of a misperception with a commercial airliner: multiple lights, intense brightness, and a change in trajectory. The significant delay between the observation and this re-examination by the GEIPAN prevents any formal identification of the potential responsible aircraft. Nevertheless, a reconstruction shows that flights departing Paris for West Africa (e.g., Dakar) regularly traverse the region where the observation occurred. Notably, during these overflights, the planes reach their cruising altitude and make a turn towards southwest in the Nançay area. This change in trajectory aligns perfectly with the turn described by the witnesses. Since planes typically traverse this sector, unusual elements were required. These elements vary among witnesses, even between T2 and T3, who are certain they saw the same PAN.

It appears that:
- The intense white glow (T2 and T3), unique to T2, double for T1, and (less distinctly) also for T3, possibly along with a stronger single light.
- The cigar-shaped glow for T2, an elongated shape for T3, and none for T1.
- The absence of noise, which would be unusual due to a limited perception of distance since the shape of the PAN is discernible (for T2 and T3).

This dominant white glow could be due to the plane's headlights (or multiple PAN with headlights). Thus, they could be planes with headlights on either after takeoff (most likely) or before landing. T1 might have seen the headlights of two planes. Headlights can be illuminated below 4000 meters or during altitude changes. These planes might not be in cruising phases or commercial airliners. The presence of headlights could explain the perception of a cigar-shaped glow due to reflections on the fuselage (headlights in the wings), and it's consistent with the flashing red lights observed by T3, also used on the ground and at low altitude by many aircraft. The lack of perception of fixed navigation green and red lights could be due to the headlights' glow, which might outshine them. This could also explain the non-perception of white strobe lights, but they might not have been present as they are not mandatory for all planes and flight phases.

The absence of noise is common in misperceptions with aircraft. Notably, the wind is transverse to the observation direction and doesn't facilitate noise. The observation elements suggest an unusual flight (in this sector) at low or medium altitude by one or more planes. We have no means to validate such a hypothesis 30 years later, but it's evident that it's possible and could produce the same perception (including multifaceted) for the witnesses. These testimonies and collected data lack consistency. No aeronautical records were made at the time, and we have no angular measurements of the PAN's position in the sky or its angular size. No efforts were made to understand or validate the differences between testimonies or detect potential influences between witnesses (they spoke to each other before their depositions).

After re-examination, it's impossible to classify this case as unexplained, as:
- On the one hand, the aircraft hypothesis could explain it under non-usual conditions (not extreme, however) for the location (medium or low altitude, one or two planes very close together).
- On the other hand, the level of strangeness testified cannot be validated, as the case's consistency is too low.

Although the aircraft hypothesis is plausible, the low consistency of testimonies leads to classifying the case as C: lack of reliable information.
