**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions' relevance. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named ROUSSES (THE) 11.08.1988 is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases. It involves the observation of the movements of a PAN with intermittent flashes by two witnesses on August 11, 1988, at approximately 00:50. The witnesses, military trainees, were bivouacked at a location known as "Le chalet de Bonnefoy" in the forest called "Risoux" in the commune of MOREZ (39). Initially, the witnesses were intrigued by the presence of white flashes in the night sky, followed by the PAN's unusual speed and trajectory, as well as the absence of noise. The PAN, described as an elongated shape, disappeared from their view as it took a descending trajectory and was obscured by the landscape. The Gendarmerie, alerted, found no further witnesses during its investigation. The peculiarities noted by the witnesses during their observation can all be explained by a simple confusion with an airplane and its anti-collision lights observed under specific conditions. The speed of the PAN, described as being "superior to that of a Mirage type F1" by T1, is actually particularly difficult, if not impossible, to gauge at night without any fixed references and without knowing the altitude of the PAN. This is even true for a witness who claims to be accustomed to seeing mirages. The perception of noise is also highly dependent on other factors, unknown in this case, such as the distance between the witnesses and the PAN, the sound environment, each witness's individual auditory perception, the sound power emitted by the PAN, and the wind's force and direction. The GEIPAN archives contain numerous verified cases (including with radar evidence) of aircraft observations without noise perception. Therefore, in the absence of details on these parameters, the absence of noise perception is not decisive. T1 also claims that the lights were "too intense" to be from an airplane. However, this observation scenario is particular and unusual for the witnesses. Indeed, they were in a temporary night observation situation in the forest, far from any light pollution and stray lights, while usually residing in an urban area. Additionally, having been outside for several hours, their night vision had ample time to adapt to the environment, which is particularly dark. Consequently, any strong light, even distant, will appear very intense to them. Another peculiar aspect for the witnesses, especially T1, is the impression that the PAN moves in a "sinusoidal" manner. The fact that T2 states the PAN moved in a "straight line" and that "the flight path was irregular, it [the shape] rose and fell at a constant, rapid speed," gives us clues about what T1 perceived and the actual nature of the PAN's movement. The rapid alternation of anti-collision lights placed at distant locations on the airplane, and the impossibility of perceiving the airplane's shape at night, can, under a favorable observation angle, create a perceptual illusion of rapid sinusoidal movement from the lights alone, while the actual movement is a straight line. This can be observed in videos available online. The final peculiar point noted by the witnesses concerns the apparent halt, followed by the PAN's descent "towards the ground." This is easily explained by a temporary altitude gain by the airplane according to a movement along the observation axis, creating an effect of apparent immobility. Once the airplane reaches the altitude plateau, the observation angle decreases again until the airplane disappears behind the trees. Furthermore, the intense brightness of the airplane's anti-collision lights, visible from very far, enhances the impression that it remains relatively close; therefore, this final observation phase is perceived by the witnesses as a "descent," while it is actually a distance increase. The likelihood of an airplane at the location and time, aside from the compatibility with the observation as evaluated above, is good. There are 5 airports within a 120-km radius of the location, including 4 international and 1 military airport. An airplane from one of these airports is not yet at cruising altitude and can thus create the illusions explained with its various lights and altitude changes. The consistency is poor. All useful angular data (dimensions of the PAN, initial and final horizon elevation) are absent. Temporal data are imprecise or also absent. Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as B: observation of an airplane.
