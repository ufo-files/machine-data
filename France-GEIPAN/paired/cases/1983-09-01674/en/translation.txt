--- seg-413837deee7c55616b2db449 [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archives on its public website www.geipan.fr. In its publications, it includes older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in the conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over all these past years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or even a change in classification. This previously classified D case, named COLMARS (04) 1983, is part of a set of cases recently reviewed. On September 28, 1983, around 9:30 PM, four witnesses on a balcony observed above the mountain of "La Femme Couchée" intense white and red lights "like flashes." The witnesses notice that the PAN seems at first immobile and then moves in "zigzag" patterns. The PAN quickly crosses a narrow valley to disappear in the direction of the summit of the "Chalufy". The brevity of the observation does not allow the witnesses to describe the shape of the object precisely. Two hypotheses are explored to explain the observation of the witnesses (see the investigation report). The first concerns a confusion with an atmospheric reentry of an artificial object (satellite, debris...). Although some parameters of the testimonies (shortness of the observation, flashing white and red lights, etc.) might suggest such a phenomenon, the fact that no other testimonies exist in an urban area that could have been crossed by this phenomenon makes this hypothesis unlikely. Furthermore, in the catalogs that list all these atmospheric entries since 1958, none is identified for the month of September 1983. The absence of a light trail is also a parameter that goes against the hypothesis. The second hypothesis relates to a simple confusion with a civil or military aircraft, observed at relatively low altitude. This hypothesis better fits the testimonial data (particularly the flashing white and red lights, and the perception of a noise...), as well as the calculated data involving estimates of height, speed, and duration of observation. It is not necessarily necessary to consider that the witnesses may have slightly underestimated the duration of the observation. In fact, an appropriate combination of the two factors of height/speed allows for a correct explanation of the observation, which remains consistent with both the duration of the observation as reported by the witnesses and the angular distance traveled by the aircraft, within the geographical context of a limited visual field due to a deep valley. It is therefore appropriate to conclude that this plane would have likely flown both faster and lower than usual in the area. The observation is all the more valid as it is likely what made the witnesses think it was strange and not an airplane. Although we have been able to demonstrate that civilian aircraft today perform regular flights, follow air corridors, and pass through a particular SID point, all located immediately near the witnesses and in the observed direction, we have not been able to determine if these data were already valid in 1983, but it is most likely that they were. The same applies to flight restriction maps, particularly altitudes and heights (regulated zones), which today indicate that the area is used by military aircraft, it is most likely that this was already the case at that time. It is not possible to prioritize one option over the other between a military or civilian aircraft. In both cases, it is necessary to introduce a just as plausible peculiarity: for the military plane that generally flies low, it is the fact that there had been an exercise that day with that particular flight direction; for the civilian plane that usually passes through, it is the fact that the altitude was lower. The consistency is correct, with a thorough investigation by the gendarmes. However, it should be noted that angular measurements taken on site would have allowed for better evaluation of the hypotheses. The GEIPAN classifies the case as 'B': probable observation of a civil or military aircraft.
