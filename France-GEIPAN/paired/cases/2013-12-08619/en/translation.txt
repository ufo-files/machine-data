Description: On December 17, 2013, around 7:15 PM, a witness, from a balcony, looks up at the sky to point out the positions of the stars to his parents using a new astronomy application for smartphone. The witness is intrigued by an unreferenced bright point on the application: using binoculars, he observes a bright white round point that remains stationary for approximately 15 minutes. The witness takes photographs of the PAN (n°2 and 3 in the photo file) and then goes back inside to warm up. Five minutes later, upon returning to the balcony, the object had disappeared. No particular noise was heard. No other testimony was collected. This case of observation of a static white light point has all the characteristics of an astronomical observation, but verifications prove negative: on the one hand, the witness himself, who is using an astronomy software, cannot identify this light point among the stars. A check by another software (Stellarium, see attached document) shows only the star Fomalhaut in the field of view, not very bright (magnitude 1) and, more importantly, too low and too south compared to the indicated direction. Venus is just setting, and Jupiter (mag -2.7) is in the NNE. The GEIPAN analyzed other hypotheses:

- **Plane**: The observed object is stationary for too long to be a plane.
- **Balloon**: The sun sets at 16:44; at this time, only satellites are in the sun's light.
- **Temporary projector at the top of the neighboring hill (motorcycle, car, intervention)**: It is noted that there are paths at the top of the hill (see map) where a vehicle could have been parked. But the witness speaks of an elevation of 45°, significantly greater than the 5° angle under which the hill appears from Baume les dames. - Thai Lantern: The color rendered in the photo corresponds well to that of a Thai lantern (see illustration "color space lantern"); several potential release sites are observed along the line of sight: recreational center, guest house, retirement home; lantern releases most often occur on weekends and holidays, but the observation took place on a Tuesday, close to Christmas, it is true. But this hypothesis does not fit well: Thai lanterns last between 5 and 10 minutes, this one would be at the limit of its duration. The intermittent nature of the lights through binoculars also doesn't fit well: the witness should have seen a ball or an ellipse; the lanterns move with the wind; while it's true that the weather report for Besançon (see attached document) indicates no wind, it's still surprising to see a Thai lantern with no movement at all. The hypothesis that would fit the rigor remains that of a Thai lantern, but with characteristics on the edge for this kind of phenomenon. The GEIPAN cannot clearly confirm this hypothesis. This case remains in category "C", due to a lack of precise data on the position in the sky, and a lack of corroboration.
