Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over many years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named WINGLES (62) on 28.06.1988 is part of a recent set of cases that have been reassessed. (See the investigation report). In the night of June 28, 1988, a witness (T1) and his wife (T2) observed from 10:20 PM onwards successive appearances of glowing balls in a cloudy sky. These balls move rapidly in all directions without noise, performing "somersaults" or "dances." T1 saw an object of large size in the shape of a cone appear. A grayish mass emerged from this object, and four white glowing rays flashed from it. Stationary for about thirty seconds, it suddenly disappeared at 2:10 AM. Only two testimonies were collected. The description might have seemed strange during the initial analysis of the case in 1988. With the experience gained by the GEIPAN, there is little doubt today. The description is consistent with reflections in the sky from a set of projectors used for advertising purposes. The beams rising from the projectors are barely visible here; the witnesses mainly see the impacts on a sky screen. The pattern formed by these impacts is typical of the scanning and rotation capabilities of the devices used:

- Circular shape or in the form of a cone, depending on the perspective created by the observation angle.
- Large amplitude movements, going back and forth, sometimes on the same trajectory, sometimes performing various figures, as well as stops.
- Color and brightness of the PANs: a white, non-blinding light. In the GEIPAN experience, various configurations can be encountered: visibility of only the impacts, only the beams, or both. Weather conditions determine visibility and visibility distance, which explains the random and fleeting nature of the phenomena (projectors from a nearby disco may only rarely be visible when those from elsewhere produce distant and frequent effects):

- Here, we verify the presence of a low cloud cover that can create a screen in the sky.
- We also note that the witnesses observe partial beam presence, just below the PANs, or rays turning on and off regularly, also below the PANs. We know that the 1980s saw the appearance in large numbers of this type of projector, both in discos and for various events (fairground festivals, concerts...). Since the observation date is a Tuesday evening, the disco hypothesis is less likely (except for tests?) and it would be a one-time event, possibly held in Lens (a few kilometers away) or its surroundings. It is difficult, almost 30 years later, to investigate further the possibility of the event. The presence of the event producer (projectors) cannot be established, but as it is perfectly possible, the very strong correspondence of a visual aspect that, by the way, resembles nothing else (see investigation report), and the compliance with meteorological conditions are enough to establish the hypothesis as probable. Consequently, Geipan classifies the case as B: probable observation of lasers or "skytracer."
