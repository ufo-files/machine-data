**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the accuracy of conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named [MER] LAT 44.76, LON -4.32 (INT) 03.07.1990 is part of a recent batch of cases re-examined. This case involves the observation of a PAN by a witness at sea off the Spanish coast on their sailboat on July 3, 1990, around 10:30 PM. The witness at the helm observed a bright, oval object with a red light on one side and a green-blue light on the other for approximately 30 seconds. No unusual noises were heard, and no disruptions to the sailboat were noted (engine running and boat navigation lights). The witness visited the Gendarmerie brigade office on July 20, 1990. It is highly probable that the witness observed a plane preparing for a nighttime landing with landing lights illuminated at a Spanish airport (see investigation report). The presence of red, blue-green, and white (or white-yellow) lights noted by the witness is perfectly characteristic of an aeronautical confusion. The red and green (blue) lights are indeed navigation lights. It is more likely an airplane than a helicopter because the bright, "bulb-like" main light drawing a horizontal oval corresponds well to two landing lights of a commercial airliner, close together and horizontally aligned, as observed from a distance. The observation distance must be discussed in relation to the simultaneous visibility of several types of lights with different powers and ranges. The red and green navigation lights, clearly observed by the witness here, have a much more limited range than landing lights, but also white anti-collision lights, whose characteristic flickering is visible from far away, but were not observed by the witness at any time. International air regulations mandate that navigation lights remain illuminated under all circumstances, in contrast to anti-collision lights, which can be turned off under certain conditions, such as fog, heavy rain, or when flying through an IMC cloud, which could distract the pilot by reflecting light on water droplets in suspension. It seems logical to prioritize the hypothesis of an airplane a few kilometers from the witness rather than a hundred kilometers away, which would make the navigation lights completely invisible. The absence of anti-collision lights can therefore be attributed to weather conditions conducive to their temporary extinction, conditions that it is impossible to confirm or deny. The landing lights, although appearing to have been illuminated (in the hypothesis of landing at a coastal Spanish airport) well before landing, can also be illuminated in other circumstances (congested airspace, low altitude of the aircraft, or flight level change) that could apply in this case. These landing lights have a limited horizontal and vertical field of view, which explains why the witness did not see them illuminating the ocean. The beams of these lights may not be visible because it depends on the presence or absence of particles in suspension in the atmosphere between the witness and the PAN. The maneuvers of this aircraft, as reconstructed from the witness's statements, are compatible with those that a commercial airliner could perform during its approach to an airport (loops, turns...), although these maneuvers would have taken place far from the Spanish coast. It is not possible 30 years later to have traffic recordings that could support or refute such an airplane hypothesis. Finally, the absence of perceived noise is not decisive for the hypothesis since the noise of the sailboat's engine (reported by the witness) could have masked any potential aircraft engine noise, which could also have been attenuated by a headwind opposing its propagation. The consistency is low, with few exploitable data, but it is sufficient to validate the hypothesis given its high probability. Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: very probable observation of an airplane.
