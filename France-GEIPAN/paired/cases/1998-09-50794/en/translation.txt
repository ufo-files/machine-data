Description: GEIPAN continues to publish all its archives on its public website www.geipan.fr. Its publications include older cases classified at the time (A, B, C or D) that are now being re-examined solely to improve the pertinence of the conclusions. Thanks to new technical resources (software) and the investigative experience acquired over all these years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or even a change in classification. This observation case, previously classified D and named [AERO PRB] de TOULOUSE (31) vers DIJON (21) 12.09.1998, is one of a group of recently re-examined cases. On September 12, 1998, at 21:46, the crew of an aircraft close to overhead MENDE (48) are intrigued by two luminous points in the sky. In radio contact with Marseille airport's control tower, they report observing two flashing luminous points moving and initially think they are seeing two military aircraft, one above the other. These aircraft pass them "well above". At 21:54, the crew then report that "it is turning back towards us now", followed by "the phenomenon has really been visible for 10 minutes now and it zigzags a little in all directions, going up and down". Absorbed by air traffic control's detailed instructions for filing a report and then by the descent procedure, the crew do not mention the PAN afterwards. Only the transcript of the radio communications and trajectory documents produced by CRNA sud-Est are collected. The recording of the exchange with control can be divided into two phases, before and after the reported "turning back" of the PAN. It is established (see the investigation report) that the pilots of flight PRB8081 initially observed the anti-collision lights of two long-haul aircraft passing above them, without ground control being able to identify them as such. Control could reconstruct these aircraft's trajectories only afterwards. It is also established that the PAN observed in the second phase can no longer be those aircraft, whose lights are too distant to be seen. There were therefore two separate observations. GEIPAN concludes that the first observation concerns aircraft. Case [AERO PRB] MENDE (48) 12.09.1998 T1 is classified A: observation of aircraft. When the "turning back of the PAN" is reported (21:54), the aircraft lights are at an azimuth of 70°, whereas the pilots describe an observation in their 3 o'clock direction, a difference of 20°. Another object probably then comes into play: the planet Saturn, at an azimuth of 100° and an elevation slightly above 20°. Visually bright like the anti-collision lights, the planet is also approximately the same white colour. The pilots probably lost sight of the lights and may have thought they had found them again, when they were actually seeing Saturn. They no longer mention flashing, which is consistent with the hypothesis. Nevertheless, this hypothesis encounters several objections and points requiring further examination: - It assumes that the pilots had not noticed Saturn beforehand. It is possible that, while the lights were still visible, the pilots, focused on them along a line of sight at 70° azimuth, had not noted Saturn 30° away in azimuth, and that, when scanning more widely after the lights disappeared, they did not recognise Saturn because they were convinced they were seeing aircraft lights. This explanation is valid only if the lights were lost from view before the confusion with Saturn, meaning a discontinuity in the observation that the recording neither confirms nor rules out; - As early as 21:46 (when the lights of the two long-haul aircraft are no longer visible), the pilots mention particular movements of the PAN: "the phenomenon [...] zigzags a little in all directions, going up and down". We do not know the extent of these movements. In the absence of that information, they are consistent with the natural physiological phenomenon of small eye movements ("micro-nystagmus"), also called autokinesis, which is poorly known to the general public and occurs when staring at objects, particularly at night. It creates the false impression that point-like luminous objects make small, erratic movements in every direction; - The pilots mention two luminous points, although this alternates with mention of a single point. They may not be very certain of this, or actual perception (a single point) may be mixed with an interpretation that, in context, relates primarily to the two strange points that had occupied the pilots for more than 10 minutes and had been in the same direction just beforehand; - The pilots then continue observing until 22:04 and do not mention the PAN afterwards. This does not mean that it is no longer visible, since they are occupied first with the incident-reporting procedure and then with landing. The case has poor evidential substance. In terms of information: - This is not an account taken from two pilots, but the transcript of a real-time exchange with the crew that does not distinguish each pilot's perception; - No information was sought from the witnesses after they had had time to reflect following the observation, particularly about the strangest points: the PAN's return, number and changing appearance. Consequently, information is lacking about whether the observation of the PAN was continuous and about changes in angular sizes and positions, including the extent of the zigzags. In terms of reliability: - The pilots had already been in an air-incident situation for 10 minutes because they were passing objects that control did not identify. This tension is naturally increased by the fact that a fatal mid-air collision occurred a month and a half before the observation and involved an aircraft from the same airline. The pilots were probably under considerable stress, helping to keep them focused on the hypothesis of the initial objects and causing them not to recognise Saturn. For this second observation, we have a hypothesis that could explain it, but its uncertainty appears too great in view of the limited substance of the available evidence (information and reliability). GEIPAN concludes that the second observation cannot be usefully analysed because reliable information is lacking. Case [AERO PRB] MENDE (48) 12.09.1998 T2 is classified C.
