**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and the investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named ORLEANS (45) 1982 is part of a recent batch of cases that have been re-examined. On January 23, 1982, between 4:35 and 4:55 a.m., gendarmes from the highway patrol unit, while on duty on the A10 (Paris-Province) highway, spotted in the sky a clear, undefined mass and a brightly lit area on the ground. Throughout their 30-kilometer journey, their observations continued either while driving or at a stop. No unusual noises were heard, especially during the patrol's stop. They lost sight of the phenomenon several times before spotting it again. At the end of the observation, the clear mass was no longer visible, but the witnesses noted that the glow remained to the east, on the forest edges bordering the highway. An on-site investigation was conducted by the gendarmes, who contacted the BA123 airbase in Orléans, located 7 kilometers away from the highway by air: no aircraft or helicopter was in flight, and the base's radar station had been offline since the previous evening. The Cofiroute company, which manages the A10, was also contacted; no employee from the company reported noticing the phenomenon. No other testimonies from residents located along the A10 were collected. An inhabitant of Ingré (45), who witnessed a phenomenon on January 17, 1982, made a statement to the local press on January 19, 1982, a few days before the two gendarmes' observation, but never presented herself to the police station she was supposed to report to. The local press commented on the event in an article on January 27, 1982, by linking the three testimonies. We have explored two hypotheses that could explain some physical or dynamic characteristics of the PAN (see the investigation report). It is established that the phenomenon was in motion, not static, as it illuminated various parts of the nearby landscape (pylons, warehouses) over a significant distance. It is known that celestial bodies (in this case, Jupiter, Saturn, or Mars) can create perceptions of light phenomena that follow a witness as long as the witness is in motion. However, it is unclear how one of these celestial bodies would be perceived as a "clear mass," even through a cloud, and produce such focused ground lighting. The hypothesis of confusion with a helicopter equipped with a powerful searchlight, located within or above the cloud layer, is more intriguing. It could explain the unique perception of a "clear mass" without a well-defined outline and the absence of navigation light perceptions; as well as the ground lighting phenomenon observed and illuminating various parts of the landscape, much like a helicopter's searchlight sweeping the area throughout its movement along the highway. Since searchlights are not typically aimed at cloud crossings, we must assume that the ground search with a searchlight is conducted primarily through cloud gaps, which is not incompatible with the presence of a cloud obstacle between the witnesses and the helicopter, as the low cloud cover was not continuous (3/8 octas). The absence of perceived noise and a visible beam are not decisive arguments. Depending on weather conditions (wind speed and direction, air purity), noise may not be heard, and a light beam may not form if the air is clear. The relatively uniform cloud layer (3/8 and 6/8 octas in two levels) was located (unless there was a weather error) at altitudes of 1200 and 1500 meters, placing the helicopter too far above the ground for the searchlights on civilian and military helicopters of that era to reach. Furthermore, the relatively small portion of the pylon illuminated shows that the object illuminating it, if located at cloud level, must have produced a very focused beam, similar to a laser, which was also beyond the capabilities of that era's onboard equipment. The description, particularly by T1, of the landscape lighting resembling "black light" does not align with the hypothesis. In a search context, light should be bright, powerful, and illuminate the entire surrounding landscape. Finally, why would a helicopter fly above clouds for a surveillance mission with a searchlight (through cloud gaps)? The hypothesis of a return mission with the searchlight left on clashes with the fact that T1 had time to observe the pylon's details and perceived immobility in the lights. The likelihood of this hypothesis is too low to be considered. Moreover, the consistency appears sufficient to support an unexplained character at this stage. Following the current GEIPAN methodology, this level of strangeness and consistency necessitates a "field investigation" with the witness for a reconstruction and cognitive interview. This interview may positively or negatively impact the evaluation of strangeness and consistency. However, it is not feasible for a case revisited so long after the event. Therefore, the evaluation of this case is conducted according to the current criteria for considering strangeness and consistency, as if the field investigation had been conducted and was entirely neutral (no additional input), which is sometimes the case. Conversely, to highlight this methodological gap, the classification is based on the ABCD scale rather than the current ABC D1/D2 scale, which for unexplained cases corresponds to a stronger validation of the unexplained character. In this instance, the case is the boundary between an unexplained and an unexploitable case due to a lack of consistency in the current file, lacking a GEIPAN on-site investigation. Consequently, the case is classified as D: unexplained. *(See the classification methodology at GEIPAN ).
