Summary: Observation of the silent North-South passage of a bright orange object in the night sky: probable observation of a Thai lantern.
--- seg-ffe8c592e2ec07134147043e [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On July 7, 2018, around 10:45 PM, a witness, in their garden, observes a reddish-orange glowing light several kilometers above the horizon. As the sphere silently approaches, the witness becomes increasingly intrigued and considers numerous explanatory hypotheses. The object disappears into a cloud then reappears. The observation lasts between 1 to 2 minutes. The witness has time to take a photo of it with their phone before the object disappears "on the spot." No other testimony about this phenomenon was collected. The witness likely observed a Thai lantern arriving from the North and turning off as it passes to the East: - the color ("Orange, very bright, completely illuminated, somewhat like the color of burning embers"); - the shape ("from a distance, a ball then a form... hard to describe as 'moving', like a plasma in fusion rather elongated, somewhat in the shape of a tube but the back flaring out. The outline was even brighter and very orange, like fire"). As the lantern approaches and ascends, the underside of the lantern's burner can block the light, making the outline of the lantern appear brighter. The combustion and its scintillation create an effect of fire and fluctuation of that fire's outline. Some lanterns can have an flared shape: - the North-South trajectory is consistent with the measured wind direction, which is North (see the Météociel website), - the disappearance is also consistent. The end of combustion can give the impression of "disappearing matter on the spot." The descent of an extinguished and dark lantern is not always visible, - a summer weekend night is conducive to such a festive event. In this hypothesis, the lantern passed through a cloud. The witness's perception is indeed that of a dimming glow against a cloud background, therefore likely by passing through the lower part of it. Lanterns rarely fly more than 300 meters in altitude, but the witness mentions the few low clouds. This description therefore does not contradict the lantern hypothesis. The satellite hypothesis should be rejected. The shape evolves too much in size (as implicitly deduced from the description). Only the ISS could give such an impression of glow and large, growing size but the ISS is not in the sky. Moreover, the disappearance cannot occur in this way, without sustained cloud cover. The meteorite hypothesis should also be rejected as the phenomenon's duration is too long (and its angular velocity too slow). Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as B: probable observation of a Thai lantern.
