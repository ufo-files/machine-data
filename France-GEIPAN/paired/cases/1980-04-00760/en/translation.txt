**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its website: www.cnes-geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reexamined to ensure more relevant conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this reexamination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case, previously classified as C and named LOUHANS (71) 1980, is part of a recent batch of cases reexamined.

On April 7, 1980, around 2:00 a.m., two individuals (T1 as passenger and T2 as driver) traveling on the departmental road D13 a few kilometers from Saint-Usuge (71) spotted a red-orange light at very low altitude. This light was approximately above the river "La Seille" and appeared to move along the water's course. On D178 at the location called "Varennes," the PAN was observed as a ball moving at ground level in successive jumps. The witnesses then saw the ball fall and come to a stop on one side of the road. The driver stopped, then, in a panic, reversed and made a U-turn. As a car approached in the opposite direction, the witnesses saw the ball ascend and disappear. They went to the gendarmerie. The observation lasted approximately 10 minutes. This case is consistent (two witnesses), and the description of the PAN observed is fairly precise. The sincerity and credibility of the witnesses have never been questioned (see investigation notes). The initial classification as C appears entirely unjustified.

The phenomenon described shares many common characteristics (observation duration, shape, size, color) with a perfectly known astronomical object: the Moon rising. Indeed, the Moon was rising in the direction indicated by the witnesses. The perceived motion of the PAN corresponds to typical illusions created by a celestial body:

- The PAN appears to follow the witnesses in motion along a parallel trajectory, creating the illusion of a chaser ball.
- The direction of the celestial body is, of course, fixed in absolute terms. When the witness's vehicle maintains a relatively constant heading, the celestial body appears fixed relative to the vehicle and moves along the landscape.
- The perception of the PAN's movement is introduced by its apparent movement along the landscape. In the complete absence of landscape (sea, desert), the illusion does not occur or is minimal. If the landscape markers are close, the celestial body moves quickly behind them; if they are far, the movement and, thus, the perception of the PAN's speed is reduced.

It's enough for there to be an alternation of closer or farther landscape markers for the witness to perceive the PAN's movements in jumps or bounds. Similarly, the notion of the PAN's height relative to the landscape below is relative; an alternation of poorly recognized landscape heights can induce a perception of absolute height changes in the PAN, contributing to the notion of boundary jumps or bounds.

Another movement phenomenon of the celestial body can be created by its variation in light intensity, itself caused by the evolving atmospheric veil in front of the celestial body (the sky is not entirely clear). This variation is logically interpreted by the witness as a change in distance (toward or away from the witness) of the PAN at often described "lightning" speeds, particularly during the final escape and disappearance. This final distance escape is logically described as a retreat into the sky or a "takeoff," and the witness, of course, does not see or describe any change in position on site or azimuth (the celestial body's are fixed). Here, the witness states, "the ball rose at great speed, angling to the right relative to our direction of travel." Indeed, previously, the PAN "followed" the witnesses and had a parallel trajectory to their right; the final distance escape of the PAN is logically perceived as a rightward deviation of the PAN, thus changing its trajectory.

It's worth noting the state of stress and panic in which the witnesses find themselves: "a panic attack, my sister backed up" (T1). This is not the visual perception of the witnesses that is in question but the interpretation the witnesses make of their observation through their feelings (fatigue, night driving, panic). Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as PAN A: an observation related to a misunderstanding with the Moon rising.
