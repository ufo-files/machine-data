Summary: Nighttime observation of erratic movements of a pale glow: mistaken for a skyrose.
--- seg-a1536b933ca20471fb2942a0 [needs-review] ---
Description: On August 7, 2021, at 11 PM, the witness's attention was drawn to a glow while observing the sky through a skylight. The witness describes "a 'faint' star, of low but easily distinguishable brightness, giving the impression of being relatively close... It moves erratically, like a fly, in a specific area of the sky (a 500-meter diameter sphere). Direction changes are sharp, not an astronomical speed but still quick." The observation with the naked eye and binoculars lasted from 11:00 PM to 12:30 AM, at which time the witness went to sleep, as the light was still active. The witness recorded a video of the PAN. Another witness present observed the phenomenon but did not provide a statement. A careful review of the testimony suggests a skyrose, and weather data confirm this hypothesis. The witness recorded a video that, unfortunately, is unusable: the device used is likely not suitable for night vision, and the nearby white porch, illuminated, disrupts the camera by preventing proper focus at a distance and closing the aperture a bit more than necessary to limit the incoming light, making the skyrose light imperceptible. Human night vision is far superior to most camera lenses, with very high light sensitivity. It is normal for witnesses to see the skyrose but not the camera. The description points towards a misidentification with a skyrose: - summer evening, tourist area,
- erratic movements in a specific area of the sky,
- rapid direction changes,
- long duration of the phenomenon.

To confirm this hypothesis, cloudiness and visibility weather data were searched (see attached documents). The Biscarrosse weather station reports, between 11:00 PM and 12:00 AM (local time), the following data: - a first cloud layer at 780 to 870 meters, with cloud cover of 3 to 4 octas (out of 8),
- a second cloud layer at 1380 to 1140 meters, with cloud cover of 3 to 5 octas. This indicates that the clouds were very low and the sky was overcast, ideal conditions for a skyrose phenomenon. Visibility was 20 km, which can explain why the witness could not see that the beam originated from the ground, contributing to the strangeness of the observation.

The Biscarrosse tourism office, contacted, did not report any official festive events using a skyrose at that date. Several nightclubs are located within the observation perimeter and could potentially operate one during their evenings. Only one (Safi) could be reached; it does have one but it has been inactive for 3 years, the others could not be reached. As the source could not be identified with certainty, the case is classified as B.

The GEIPAN classifies the case as "B": Misidentification with a skyrose.
