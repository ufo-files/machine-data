Summary: Observation of the Moon and Venus
--- seg-13f1303ee8da837a9471fc17 [needs-review] ---
Description: On February 18, 1980, several people observed from the courtyard of a residence a peculiar luminous phenomenon surrounded by a misty halo. A white, glowing ball stood motionless near an object resembling a crescent moon. This observation lasted several minutes, after which the phenomenon began moving from right to left and up and down for approximately 20 minutes. The object then moved at high speed on a northwest trajectory. Its volume appeared three times larger than the surrounding stars, and it shone brighter. It gradually faded away as it moved away. No sounds were heard during the observation, and no other testimonies were collected about this phenomenon. However, a few days later, local press reported the observation of a "ground glow," a grayish light accompanying the new moon, which can be accompanied by particular visual effects when atmospheric perturbations are added. An examination of the sky map suggests that the astronomical observation hypothesis is more likely. Indeed, the Moon (magnitude -7.70) is visible until 9:02 PM, after which it dips below the horizon. At this time (the new moon is on the 16th), only a thin crescent is visible. Venus is located just above the Moon (magnitude -4.19). Furthermore, the ground glow (or grayish light) is visible when Earth, Moon, and Sun are almost aligned. From our planet, the Moon appears as a very thin crescent, but from our satellite, Earth is in a full phase and shines brightly. It is the ground glow that makes the unlit part of the moon shine very faintly. After the new moon, the phenomenon is observable in the evening from the third to the fifth day, and before the new moon, it is visible in the morning, from the fifth to the third day before the next lunar phase. Therefore, it can be safely said that the main witness who observed it around and slightly before 9:00 PM saw the Moon (with a ground glow) and just above Venus. The gendarmes who observed it at 9:15 PM saw Venus before it, too, disappeared below the horizon, as the Moon had already vanished a dozen minutes earlier. It is true that the witnesses reported "rapid" movements and a rapid displacement before its disappearance. It is likely that the witnesses unconsciously exaggerated these movements due to the proximity of the horizon and perhaps also due to an "autokinetic effect" (the impression of motion when intensely fixating something).
