Summary: Long observations by four witnesses in a car of the movement of a very bright circular phenomenon in the sky and disappearance towards the West: observation of Venus at sunset.

Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in its conclusions, leveraging new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the past many years. This reassessment sometimes results in a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named SAINT-VALLIER (26) 1980 is part of a recent set of cases that have been re-examined (see the investigation report). During trips, several people in two cars observed the westward movement of a circular glowing phenomenon in the sky. The PAN was described as a very bright, white phosphorescent, orange, or red (depending on the witnesses) ball with "green reflections" resembling "feet." Once everyone had arrived at the same location, they continued their observations until the PAN disappeared behind the mountains heading northwest. The observations lasted between 5 and 20 minutes. The witnesses very likely observed VENUS. The duration of the observation, significant, is typical of this type of confusion with a star. The observation took place in the northwest or west at a low angular elevation, which corresponds well to Venus' location at the time of observation, which was around 6 degrees of elevation, west-northwest. The appearance of the PAN, primarily described as white, point, and very bright, perfectly aligns with the hypothesis. Variations in the accounts of some witnesses regarding this appearance reflect the fact that Venus was very low on the horizon and subject to atmospheric turbulences that could temporarily and randomly vary its brightness, producing brief color effects (red, green), but also intense flashes, which the witnesses would describe as "glowing feet." The perception of the PAN's rapid movement is due to the witnesses' movement in a car. The unidentified celestial body moves along and behind the environment traveled by the vehicle and is perceived as a PAN follower. Other perceived rapid movements, but with the witness stationary, result from the celestial body's light intensity decreasing (atmospheric veil thickness) that the witness logically interprets as a movement away from the PAN. The perception of the PAN's slow movement corresponds to Venus' setting and its disappearance behind the mountains of the Forez. The only element that could limit this hypothesis is the presence of a cloud obstacle preventing that evening's view of Venus. However, given the strong correlation with Venus, the sole available indication of a generally clear sky (2/8) at 35 km, the likelihood of another phenomenon over a cloud background that hides Venus is very low. In conclusion, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A, very probable observation of the planet Venus.
