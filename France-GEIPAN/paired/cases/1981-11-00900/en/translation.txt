**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reexamined to enhance the conclusions. Thanks to new technical tools (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this reexamination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named GUIPAVAS (29) 22.11.1981 is part of a recent batch of reexamined cases.

On November 22, 1981, between 07:55 and 08:00, four independent groups of people observed a low-covered sky with a reddish-orange spherical light phenomenon. This sphere disappeared instantly or seemed to fall into a field near an airport runway in Guipavas (29). The Gendarmerie was contacted the next day by the air traffic controller at Guipavas (29) airport: a total of 8 testimonies were collected, 7 of which reported observing the PAN. The observation duration was short, and different perceptions among witnesses were noted. The witnesses likely observed the atmospheric reentry of a meteoroid (as detailed in the investigation report). The following elements support this hypothesis:

- The color of the phenomenon: reddish-orange
- The shape: spherical
- The presence of "sparks" and a trail similar to that of a shooting star
- The short observation duration
- The movement: likely slow and regular
- The disappearance: by descent (and landing on the ground for one witness)

This is a tangential atmospheric reentry observed face-on (along the reentry path), which can manifest as a low, horizontal bright point moving little angularly, or even remaining stationary when the trajectory aligns perfectly with the observation axis. There are differences in witness descriptions that can be explained by the hypothesis or the observation conditions:

- The presence of sparks or a trail is only indicated by 2 witnesses (T2 and T6). T6 was a stationary witness (waiting for a bus) and, unlike those in motion, had the necessary focus for these details during such a brief period. There's no way to know if T2 (a jogger) was stationary, but it's possible, as we at least know he didn't continue running; he was ahead of T1 and T3 (other joggers) and later joined them. Conversely, T5 and T4 (firefighters on duty at the airport), also stationary, reported nothing. T4 mentioned nothing but wasn't questioned about such details, unlike T5, who responded that he noted nothing, but he seems less sensitive to brightness because, unlike all other witnesses, he doesn't report any brightness for this PAN.

- Several witnesses (T4, T5, T6) observed a change in the PAN's shape as it moved. Although there are few examples describing face-on reentry observations, these shape changes could be explained by the fact that the trail, observed from the front or face-on, is reduced (less visible) and may appear multifarious due to thickening. Other witnesses didn't report such changes but lacked the same observational focus as the stationary ones, as they made part (or all) of the observation while moving.

- Descriptions regarding the mobility or stationarity of the PAN differ. Those consistent with explicitly perceived angular movements are compatible among witnesses and consistent with the hypothesis. Perceptions of movement or immobility not linked to explicit angular perceptions may be individual interpretations (a variation in the PAN's light intensity could be interpreted as a change in distance for the considered PAN at constant brightness).

- The final phase of the reentry may be accompanied by a decrease in brightness with a more or less progressive angular descent of the residual body, depending on general conditions, the final light intensity, and the witness's visual acuity. Some witnesses may lose brightness before or during the perception of angular descent, while one (or two) witness(es) reported seeing it fall into the field. A witness tracking the falling light onto the horizon initiates a mental reconstruction of the trajectory based on an arbitrary distance. What they perceive afterward against the landscape may not be perceived by the eyes and could be an extrapolation of mental mechanics incorrectly set on a trajectory close to the actual path.

- The observation duration of T3, reported as "one to two minutes," seems too long for the observed minimal angular movement, even for a tangential atmospheric reentry observed face-on. It's likely that the observation duration of T3 was overestimated and was closer to the durations observed by the other witnesses (not exceeding 15 seconds).

In conclusion, the explanation can be accepted, and the resulting uncertainty arises less from the rarity of the phenomenon (2% of natural reentries occur tangentially, but rare events tend to create observations) than from the witnesses' different perceptions of the shape changes noted, a phenomenon that, while understandable given the geometry, has not yet been observed (possibly due to the rarity of such reentries). The consistency is good, with numerous witnesses (some independent of each other), no inconsistencies (except for the duration in T3's case), and relatively good descriptions of the PAN. Therefore, the GEIPAN classifies the case as B: atmospheric reentry of a meteoroid.

**NOTE:** The initial classification by the GEIPAN stated that the CRNA reported "a visible spot on the radar." In fact, the gendarmes noted in the protocol "that no abnormal events were observed by the on-duty controllers at CRNA West," and the mission leader of CNRA indicated that "no significant credit can be given to these spots. They could be either an echo phenomenon or interference."
