Description: On the night of May 16 to 17, 2025, around midnight, a resident of COUETRON-AU-PERCHE (41) observed the sky from her garden, facing south. No light pollution was present. She noticed a luminous phenomenon that she initially identified as a satellite moving from west to east. This straight and fluid movement lasted approximately 30 seconds. Suddenly, the object turned 90 degrees, significantly changing its trajectory to move towards the horizon, that is, towards the south, for about another 10 seconds. Then, its light gradually faded "in two seconds, as if it were leaving the sun's illumination." Due to its low consistency (single witness, imprecise positioning of the PAN and observation time), this case is determined to be a probable observation of two orbiting objects crossing their paths (see the investigation report).

*According to the criteria of GEIPAN, consistency refers to the amount of reliable and objective information gathered for a testimony. The witness specifies in her observation description that she knows the object is visible due to the reflection of the Sun on the structure of an orbiting object. The investigation results indicate that the "90-degree" trajectory of PAN is likely caused by the observation of two independent objects:

- The first with a west-east trajectory, typical for a satellite, passing at a near-vertical angle (75°) to the witness, disappearing visually as its trajectory took it out of the area where the Sun, due to its position, allowed its visualization through its reflection on its structure.

- A second orbiting object passing at a near-vertical angle to the witness as well, but with a north-south trajectory, entered this area, making it visible. The disappearance of the second object is consistent with the witness's statement ("then its light faded in two seconds, gradually, as if it were leaving the sun's light") as it entered the Earth's shadow.

Many satellites, particularly those belonging to the Starlink® network, could potentially explain the trajectory of the first PAN. A good candidate for the 90-degree trajectory of PAN would be a Russian rocket stage (SL-8 R/B) that emerged from the Earth's shadow at 11:57 PM at a 72° south-southwest angle, before disappearing again at midnight at a 25° south angle (Figure 9 in the investigation report). However, the observation timing is not precise enough to identify specific candidates.

GEIPAN classifies the case as "B," a probable observation of two orbiting objects.
