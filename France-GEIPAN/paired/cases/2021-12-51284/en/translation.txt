Summary: A motorist observed multiple yellow lights in the night sky forming a "bar": very likely observation of a group of sky lanterns.
--- seg-d17a8ff4e1f170001e4424b4 [needs-review] ---
**Report:** On December 4, 2021, at 7:45 PM, a driver observed multiple yellow lights in the sky forming a "stick." The witness observed the phenomenon for 10 to 15 seconds while driving. Only one testimony was collected. The consistency of this case is average: one witness, no photos or videos (see investigation report). The investigation concluded that the witness observed a group of sky lanterns. This conclusion is based on the following elements:

- **Color:** Yellow lanterns are most often orange, but can be described, according to the witness's color perception, as yellow. Yellow lanterns do exist, but they are less frequent.
- **Shape:** Pointed for individuals as they are observed at a great distance. For the whole group, the lanterns, released in clusters with a light wind, can remain relatively grouped for some time, as they are almost all carried to high altitude only by the sole effect of air heating inside their envelope by the flame (warm air, less dense, favors rising). Not all lanterns in a group are necessarily released exactly at the same time, so they will succeed each other in their ascent and can visually appear, for a distant observer, as a "stick" or an "inclined band."

- **Movement:** The movement of these lanterns could not be observed by the witness due to the combination of the following factors: their distance, the very slow movement of the lanterns themselves, and the very short observation duration.
- **Weather:** Calm with a light wind, and it's a Saturday night, so the conditions are ideal for releasing lanterns as part of a local or family event; although we are in December (the season is not particularly conducive), such a release remains possible.
- **Very short observation duration:** 10 to 15 seconds of observation by the witness while driving at 62 mph on the highway.
- **End of observation:** The observation ends when the lanterns reach the end of their life: they simply turn off and stop being visible. The witness states that "Shortly after (10 seconds) the object seemed to 'turn off' and completely disappeared (no movement)." The lights' extinction seems simultaneous. This point is not usual. It contributes to the strangeness. The strangeness for the witness was essentially created by the appearance of the whole, which he considered as a global phenomenon rather than a set of distinct phenomena. We have shown that all the characteristics of the appearance of the PAN conform very well to those of a likely large group of sky lanterns released that Saturday night in the sky, probably for a local celebration. The light wind was favorable for this type of release. The witness himself considered the hypothesis but ultimately rejected it, mainly due to the large number of objects (without specifying the exact number) and after viewing videos of lanterns that did not resemble what he observed. We have seen that lanterns can be released in large numbers, and that a video is not necessarily representative of everything observable during such a release, as the overall appearance can vary greatly depending on various parameters (wind strength, observation distance, observation angle, video quality, etc.).

The GEIPAN classifies this case as "B": very probable observation of a group of sky lanterns.
