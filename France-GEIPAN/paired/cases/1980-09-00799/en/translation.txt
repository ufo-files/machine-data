**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed to ensure more relevant conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. The observation case previously classified as D and named LANNION (22) 1980 is part of a recent batch of cases reassessed. On September 5, 1980, at 10:45 PM, eight witnesses were surprised by a luminous phenomenon above the ocean. Of these eight witnesses, only three were interviewed by the competent gendarmerie on September 8, 1980. During the interviews, the witnesses (T1 and T2) described two very bright red orbs leaving trails in the sky. The vehicle passengers (T1 and T2) felt the orbs were approaching and following them. Overcome with fear, they sought refuge with a local resident (T3) in PLESTIN-LES-GREVES (22), who also observed a very bright red orb at the top of the pines bordering the cemetery. His observation lasted a few seconds, after which he brought everyone home to reassure them. The appearance of the PAN as described by the witnesses (orb shape, red color, high brightness, presence of a trail) perfectly aligns with the main working hypothesis of a confusion with two distress rockets of the "parachute rocket" type (see the investigation report). The initial observation locates the PAN above the ocean, in or near the Saint-Michel-des-Grèves bay, reinforcing the hypothesis that such rockets are launched from the ocean. Regarding the behavior of the PAN in relation to the hypothesis, the "follower orb" illusion for distant objects explains the witnesses' impression that the PAN were following them while they were in their vehicle, stopping when they did, etc... Another characteristic of the behavior of the PAN is the "enlargement" noted by the witnesses, interpreted as a "closer approach" to their vehicle, which is not necessarily the case. It can be very well explained by an increase in the brightness of the rockets once their illuminating charge is triggered, making them appear artificially larger and thus seem closer. The only inconsistency persisting with this hypothesis is that of duration. Although the testimonies regarding the initial observation position in Saint-Efflam can be interpreted in two different ways, both valid, the time taken by the witnesses to travel the distance separating them from the initial observation point to the final point (corresponding to the observation duration, between 60 and 90 seconds) is longer than the visibility duration of distress parachute rockets, which never exceeds 45 seconds. Various hypotheses have been put forward to explain this inconsistency. One of them particularly caught our attention, that of two successive launches of two parachute rockets. Since the visibility of these rockets occurs once their apogee is reached and they are launched in the same direction, they can appear in the same place in the sky from the witnesses' position. Furthermore, during the second launch, visibility is less good (the rockets are observed from the back of the vehicle) and panic is spreading in the cabin, the witnesses probably did not pay attention to the transition between the two launches, general agitation reducing their discernment abilities. Unfortunately, we do not have reliable and precise data to strengthen the hypothesis. Testimonies are imprecise, especially regarding the durations of observations of the different phases. We would also have liked to have more details on possible interruptions of observation by one or more witnesses throughout the journey. This data is unverifiable more than 35 years after the facts. Although the inconsistencies related to observation duration can theoretically be explained, in practice the lack of precise data prevents a classification as "B," a possible confusion with distress rockets of the "parachute rocket" type. Therefore, the appropriate classification for this case is "C," with the lack of data not allowing for a sufficiently solid opinion.
