Summary: Observation of the zigzag movement of a spherical light phenomenon in the sky: probable observation of the International Space Station (ISS).
--- seg-043ba404b4cd0518529859bf [needs-review] ---
Description: At 7:35 on January 30, 2012, a witness observes what they initially take to be a star. The object is spherical, about the size of a 5-cent piece, white with a blue center. Suddenly, the object moves and reaches the zenith of the witness, then zigs zagging from right to left, moves very quickly, zigs zagging again, and heads south until it disappears. We note that at exactly the same time, in directions very close to those indicated by the witness, the International Space Station (ISS) was crossing the sky from northwest to southeast, extremely bright (magnitude -4, like Venus). The description of the object is consistent with the ISS, but the account of its movement is obviously not, since the ISS moves linearly. Since the witness could not have missed the observation of the ISS, we conclude that the witness was a victim of classic perceptual illusions: autokinesthesia and autostasis (perception of a bright point in the sky), frequent illusions when observing a uniform background; due to the overcast veil hiding less bright stars: the ISS was the only bright point visible against the sky. Due to the partly cloudy sky, there remains a slight doubt about the visibility of the ISS. The GEIPAN classifies this case as "B": probable observation of the International Space Station. The witness makes a second observation on January 22 but does not provide details. We note on that day a passage of the ISS from 7:06 to 7:13 above the north horizon. The witness makes a third observation on Friday, February 24 at 9:20 PM of "two spheres resembling my last testimony, but of vivid red color... following each other perfectly." At this precise moment, we note the passage of two American spy satellites, NOSS, near the north horizon; however, these satellites are often insufficiently bright to be seen with the naked eye.
