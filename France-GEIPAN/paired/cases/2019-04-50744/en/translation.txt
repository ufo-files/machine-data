Summary: Observation and photographs of 3 PAN bright lights initially aligned horizontally, first stationary then appearing to move: astronomical misperception with three stars in the Orion constellation belt, followed by photographs of ground or sea lighting.
--- seg-c592cdf28b878f95475db92f [machine-unreviewed] ---
Description: On April 8, 2019, in the evening, a witness, with his daughters, is on a first-floor terrace of a gîte, and takes two photographs (IMG_4170.jpg et IMG_4340.jpg) showing the clear view of the ocean with the setting sun. After dinner, everyone returns to the terrace to observe the starry sky. The father and his daughters were then intrigued by 3 glowing blue/white balls aligned horizontally, stationary, and silent in the sky: 3 photographs of the PAN were taken (IMG_4171.jpg,  IMG_4172.jpg, IMG_4173.jpg). Then the witness takes a new photograph (IMG_4174.jpg) after losing sight of the PAN in his viewfinder: this photo is taken at random among the gusts without a precise view of the PAN. Finally, the witness takes one last photograph (IMG_4175.jpg.) in a quick snapshot of the right side of a pine tree. That's when the witness notices that the luminous spheres seem to be leaving. The witness does not understand what he was able to observe and photograph. Only one testimony is collected. The consistency is very good, with a witness having well detailed his observation, including with angular data. Five photographs were also taken and were able to be analyzed (see the investigation report). In conclusion, we can state that the witnesses observed and photographed two distinct phenomena: - initially, the three stars of the Orion belt (Alnitak, Alnilam, and Mintaka); - and secondly, either the public lighting lights of the island of Sein, or those of a boat navigating through the Raz de Sein heading north. This conclusion is based on the following elements: For the first phase:

- immobility
- white-bluish color
- azimuth and elevation position
- angular distance separating each element. For the second phase: o azimuth and elevation position. Several parameters contributed to the witness not recognizing these objects: - he does not live locally and is therefore unfamiliar with the area; - the witness has no knowledge of astronomy; - the observation conditions, at night, took place in the absence of sufficient ambient light that could have allowed the witness to distinguish the horizon line. In addition, other parameters generated a discontinuity in the observation that did not allow the witness to distinguish between the two phenomena, which were therefore considered to be unique: - the presence of a tree in the direction of observation; - their probable movement across the terrace during the observation; - the difficulty the witness experienced in correctly framing the PAN in the lens of his phone. This adds to the witness's desire to find "his PAN" observed during the first phase in the last photograph. To do this, he meticulously examines the image and finds a succession of three lights arranged roughly in the same manner as the first three PAN, though located lower down and closer together. He thus naturally assimilates the two phenomena as being one and the same. This last photograph is an indirect observation: only the star phenomenon is retained. The GEIPAN classifies case 'A': certain observation of the three stars in the belt of the Orion constellation and of urban lighting lights on the island of Sein or a boat traveling north in the Raz de Sein.
