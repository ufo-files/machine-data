**Description:** In the archives of GEIPAN, there are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the relevance of conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. The previously classified D case, named BEAUNE (21), on 22.02.1995, is part of a recent batch of cases re-examined. On February 22, 1995, after 10:30 PM, three family members (two adults and a 12-year-old child) were intrigued by a PAN above their home. Witnesses looking through a window described an object as a bright white star. There is no unanimous testimony regarding the movement of the PAN. The observation lasted two to three minutes. The witnesses likely observed the passage of the Mir space station, visible in the sky at the time and date of the observation (see investigation report). This conclusion is based on the following points:

- The witnesses' description of the shape, color, and size of the PAN, characterized as a large bright white star, is entirely compatible.
- The speed described as relatively slow by T1 matches well with the apparent speed of Mir.
- Regarding the direction of movement, we were able to show that the Mir station, present in the sky that evening, was indeed moving towards south-southeast. (T3's mention of immobility could perhaps be explained by the brief beginning of his observation, but there is also a contradiction between T3 and the gendarmes who claim T3 clearly saw the PAN move.)
- The observation duration is mostly reported as short by the witnesses, not exceeding three minutes, which perfectly aligns with the duration of Mir's passage. (T2's mention of a 15-minute duration likely refers to the final phase after the phenomenon disappeared, as the witness may have continued observing in hope of seeing the PAN again.)
- T2's mention of the "repositioning" a little further away from the PAN without observing any movement is entirely compatible with what can be observed for some satellites, as brightness can fluctuate significantly and quickly, giving the impression of an extinction followed by a "re-lighting" further away. T2's mention of "immobility for approximately 30 seconds" requires specific assumptions to remain compatible with the hypothesis.
- Overestimation of duration or confusion between periods of extinction and lighting of the PAN, or confusion with a similar nearby object (star) during extinction periods. The absence of this immobility mention by other witnesses is a strong argument for this type of error and confusion by T2. The limiting nature of these explanations affects the probability of the Mir hypothesis without invalidating it, given the strength of other points in its favor.

The consistency of this case is mediocre:

- In terms of information, there are many very imprecise or contradictory data between the testimonies. We can also regret the absence of angular measurements (dimension, height on the horizon...) and a precise and detailed chronology of events for each witness.
- Contradictions undermine the perception of the reliability of the testimonies, especially in a context where the first child witness (T3), sensitized to the UFO concept (through a class at school on the same day) and looking for a UFO that evening, may have influenced his parents (T1 and T2) by alerting them to a strong feeling of strangeness that could affect their observational sense or critical thinking.

The level of consistency remains sufficient to support the Mir hypothesis, given the good probability associated with it. In the face of greater final strangeness (absence of other hypotheses or lower probability for the Mir hypothesis), this consistency would have been insufficient to conclude, and the case would have been classified as C (unexploitable) and not maintained at D (unexplained). See the classification methodology of GEIPAN. The case is classified as "B": probable observation of the Mir space station.
