**Case Description:**

On December 8, 2018, at 5:13 PM, a witness on a parking lot took two photographs of bright lights in a cloudy sky. They left the observation site. No other testimonies were collected. This case illustrates the casual attitude of some witnesses (fortunately very minority) who are unscrupulous in their use of GEIPAN resources. They only contact us to satisfy their curiosity, not to deal with a genuine perceived strangeness. They make follow-ups and sometimes acerbic comments about the GEIPAN's failure to fulfill its mission, even though their testimony lacks information, if not sincerity.

In their message to us, the witness writes: "I took out my smartphone and took two photos with a 9-second interval, then I got back into the car and left, I didn't check if the lights were still there. I decided to take these photos on impulse, as I do when I see something in the sky that seems unfamiliar, to later view them on a PC."

Upon follow-up, they attempt to hold us accountable with the following statement: "After 5 months, have you been able to advance the investigation? The Lyon Mont-Verdun Air Base 942 seems to have been overflown by these PAN based on their north/south trajectories. Did the military detect something given the importance of this base, something you are not unaware of?"

What about this strangeness?

- The witness takes photos and leaves without looking. If it was truly strange, they would have taken the time to follow the "development" of the PAN without significantly delaying them, as the configuration has already changed a lot in 10 seconds (from one photo to the next).
- The witness certifies in writing that they saw both PAN with their own eyes, yet we can highlight that one of the PAN (which the witness notes as "then another less bright light above the 4th lamppost") could not have been seen by them as it's a reflection created by the smartphone's lens (as indeed analyzed in the attached photo). The other, brighter PAN is not, a priori, a reflection.
- The witness is inconsistent or imprecise about the observation duration. They indicate 1 minute, but their description is limited to what is seen in the first photo, while the second photo, taken 9 seconds later, shows a very different aspect (it's not possible to note more noticeable faint lights scattered across the photo unless we consider they could be extensions of those in the first photo). Either the witness only saw and photographed a fleeting phenomenon and is mistaken about the duration, or they forgot to mention what happened during the minute preceding the photo.
- The other PAN indicated in the first photo is not a reflection. The light would thus be real and of low duration at least after the photo was taken. Given the lack of available information, this light could be compatible with aircraft headlights at low altitude. It only takes a slight change in course for the headlight to quickly lose brightness. Without descriptive elements of trajectory evolution and duration, it's impossible for us to explore this hypothesis, which cannot be neither confirmed nor refuted. The fact that there are no elements in this direction on commercial traffic tracking sites (e.g., Flight Radar) is not an obstacle, as these sites do not claim to track all aircraft, especially private, military, or defense ones.

Considering the lack of consistency in this testimony and the witness's apparent need, the GEIPAN did not bother to activate the resources of the CNOA (Defense National) to establish a traffic record. And when, 5 months later, in response to the witness's follow-ups, the GEIPAN decided to bring this type of resource request to the public's attention, it was too late for the CNOA to provide the traffic data.

This observation is not strange:

- On the one hand, it's not strange for the witness who would not have testified if they hadn't been accustomed to photographing bright points and then checking if they're strange or not.
- And because we have at least one compatible explanation.

This observation is neither strange nor consistent. It lacks information (it's limited to what's seen in a fixed photo) and, more importantly, reliability from the witness (who claims to see phenomena inside the camera and is inconsistent about the duration and evolution of the observation).

Therefore, the GEIPAN classifies the case as C: Lack of reliable information. (It's not a hoax as the witness did indeed see a light in the sky before taking the photo).
