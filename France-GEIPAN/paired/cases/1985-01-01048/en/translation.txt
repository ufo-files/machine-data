Description: The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are old cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being reassessed, with the sole purpose of being more relevant in their conclusions. Thanks to new technical means (software) and the investigative experience gained over the past years, this reassessment sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. This observation case previously classified as D and named SAINT-GERMAIN (46) 1985 is part of a recent batch of cases that have been reassessed. In the night of January 24, 1985, two witnesses were surprised by two successive flashes followed by tinkling and a sudden, intense, and brief brightness. Above their house, these witnesses caught sight of an object shaped and sized like a rugby ball. This milky-colored object moved slowly and silently, surrounded by a pale white halo. Approximately three minutes later, these witnesses and others heard explosions. No power outage was recorded in the house. The description includes key characteristics of some meteoroid re-entry phenomena:

- A great light in the night, illuminating a large area.
- Oval, elongated glow with a halo, crossing the sky.
- Explosions occurring after the flash.
- Multiple witnesses in an area that could be extensive.

The gendarmerie report collected testimonies from people under the same roof or neighbors. However, an appeal for witnesses (T1) by local press recorded around a dozen people from neighboring districts claiming to have made similar observations at the same time (no recorded testimonies in the gendarmerie). The temporal aspects of the phenomenon are offset. The light in the night that illuminates the sector so widely is caused by the meteorite at one or more particular moments (here, flashes followed by a longer period of illumination) during its re-entry into the atmosphere. This passage to extreme brightness can be more or less long, the meteorite was then not in the witnesses' field of vision or they did not have time to detect the origin point in the sky. When it entered the witness's field of vision, its light intensity had diminished (T1 says "the day seemed very long, but it must not have lasted more than a few seconds; immediately after, night returned very quickly, at that moment, I saw an object or a ball of white fire arrive,..."). Explosions can occur with a longer or shorter delay compared to the light phenomena. Indeed, these re-entry phenomena occur at high altitudes between 100 and 20 kilometers, and the time it takes for the sound to reach the ground (at 330 m/s) can create shifts of several minutes. It is also typical for witnesses to perceive the phenomenon very close in the sky.

The GEIPAN has handled a significant number of cases over the past 15 years, and today, many sites record data collected on the ground (from automatic cameras and/or testimonies) during these re-entries. The characteristics of these phenomena are now well known to the GEIPAN, both their repeatability and variability, and the testimonies received are resolved without specific investigation. It also becomes easier to reassess old cases like this one, except when the testimonies are too brief and can leave room for doubt. An example from 2016 with over 20 recordings on the site (International Meteor Organization) illustrates the functioning of one of these sites, as well as the geographical spacing and the number of witnesses who could observe such a phenomenon.

Consequently, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: very probable observation of a meteoroid re-entry.
