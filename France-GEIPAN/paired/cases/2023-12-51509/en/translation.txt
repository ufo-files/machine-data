Description: On December 25, 2023, around 7:15 PM, witnesses observed two fixed, immobile white lights below the planet Jupiter from their terrace in Elne (66), at a southerly azimuth. After one to two minutes of observation, these lights gradually faded out. The consistency of the testimony, according to GEIPAN criteria, is average as only one of the two witnesses reported their observation to GEIPAN and no photos or videos of the __UFO_PROTECTED_001__s were provided. The consistency, per GEIPAN criteria, refers to the amount of reliable and objective information gathered for a testimony. The witness who reported to GEIPAN is a regular sky observer. They provided precise data: date, time, azimuth, elevation, and Jupiter's apparent position. These details allow the observation to be situated in a straightforward context that doesn't require on-site reconstruction. The sketch submitted to the investigator, integrating both Jupiter and the Albères mountain range, estimates the observation azimuth at approximately 140° with good reliability. The residual strangeness remains high and only resides in the duration of the observation, estimated by T1 as 1 to 2 minutes, which is vague but lengthy, without this information being able to be clarified despite the investigator's follow-ups. It's too long for a satellite flash or debris. The prolonged immobility also makes the hypothesis of observing landing lights from planes unlikely, for which we've previously shown they move transversely to the witnesses and at an elevation significantly lower than that of __UFO_PROTECTED_001__s. Thus, while the point-like shape and white color align with the three explored hypotheses (satellite flashes, landing lights from planes, and luminous balloons), the prolonged immobility prevents these three hypotheses from being formally validated, even though the observation period (on Christmas Eve) is conducive to observing festive lights in the sky. The low strangeness of the phenomenon, the absence of independent witnesses for an allegedly visible and prolonged event, the silence of the second witness, and certain impreciseness in the account (particularly regarding the observation duration) justify a classification in category "C." This classification is chosen despite examining three explanatory hypotheses, none of which fully align with the characteristics described of the PAN. GEIPAN classifies this case as "C," unidentified phenomenon due to a lack of data.
