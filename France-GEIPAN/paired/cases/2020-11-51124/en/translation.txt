Summary: Sequential observations during the same night of a stationary bright orange point in the direction of South: astronomical misperception of Mars.
--- seg-002956aa383c1b8ffb04e177 [needs-review] ---
Description: On November 11, 2020, between 10:30 PM and 11:30 PM, the witness observed in the sky for approximately 15 minutes a very bright point ("like Venus, or even a little brighter") orange, located straight south and south + 5/10 at an elevation of around 60°. The witness did not note any variation in brightness, but had the impression that this point was moving slightly, going from left to right and from front to back, without any overall direction, meaning it remained more or less in the same place. Fine clouds of altitude pass through the sky after 15 minutes, obscuring the stars except for this bright point. The witness goes back home, then comes out again about 30 minutes later: the point is still there but seems much less mobile. The witness goes home again, and comes out one hour later; the point has disappeared. The witness does not find it elsewhere in the sky. The sky is once again starry without clouds. Only one testimony is collected. The witness does not specify the start and end times of each of the two observations during this same night. However, he indicates a long duration (20 minutes) of discontinuous observation of a stationary orange light point with lateral and front-back movements. The observation, given its duration and description, suggests an astronomical misperception in a cloudy context. An astronomical check indicates, according to Stellarium, that at 10:30 PM, the planet Mars is at an azimuth of 187° (which is compatible with what the witness mentions: south and south + 5/10°) and at an elevation of 49° (the witness mentions 60°, but height is often subject to interpretation errors). The described phenomenon has many common characteristics with the planet Mars, very close to the indicated axis. A weather check for that night indicates that the sky was very cloudy between 10:00 PM and 11:00 PM (6/8 octas) on November 11, 2020, and completely covered between midnight and 1 AM (8/8 octas) on November 12, 2020 (see the weather document on cloudiness). The witness estimates that it cannot be a star because fine altitude clouds mask the stars except for this point, so he considers that the point is under the clouds. The passage of cloudy streaks in the observation axis, the impossibility of estimating distances at night, and the direction of observation indicated by the witness of the only particular orange point in this direction in the sky make an astronomical misperception with Mars entirely possible. Moreover, the perception of slight movement ("there is no possible doubt that the point moves slightly, going from left to right and from front to back without any overall direction, meaning it remains more or less in the same place" free text from the witness) results from two classic effects in star observation: the ocular phenomenon of autocinetic effect that creates perceptions of angular movements, and that induced by intensity fluctuations (caused by the atmosphere) that the witness interprets as radial movement (towards or away from him). The strangeness witnessed by the witness of a strongly glowing, orange point in motion is well explained, the residual strangeness is low. Consistency is average given: - inaccuracies in timing, - the fact that the witness mentions a starry sky without clouds, while cloudiness is 6/8 octas at the beginning of the observation and 8/8 octas at the end. The GEIPAN classifies this observation as A, as a very probable misperception with the planet Mars.
