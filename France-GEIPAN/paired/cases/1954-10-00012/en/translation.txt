Summary: Distant observation by a pilot of the movement of a circular object: lack of information.
--- seg-66f5218cc43d43e49f3ce36f [needs-review] ---
Description: On October 2, 1954, an experienced pilot (approximately 10,000 flight hours, specializing in Alpine flying) was flying a Nord 1203 at a heading of 250°, at 220 km/h and an altitude of 2,000 meters. At 4:20 PM, he observed to his right a "craft" moving at the same heading at an altitude of approximately 4,000 meters, located at a distance he estimated to be about 10 kilometers. Its trajectory followed an upward curve. Its shape was that of a tilted disk on its axis by 30 to 40°, with the rear edge exhibiting a phosphorescent glow. Its diameter was estimated at 3-4 meters. It disappeared over the horizon after 70 seconds. The pilot filed a brief report of his observation. No other testimony was collected. The sun (azimuth 245°, elevation 18°) was in the flight axis. The plane traveled approximately 4,300 meters during the observation duration. The object was seen at an elevation of about 11° in an azimuth close to 340° (no reported astronomical confusion in this area, see sky map). It followed an upward trajectory then disappeared over the horizon, indicating the witness omitted describing the descending portion. The witness interpreted what he saw as a 30-40° tilted disk "on its axis" but which axis is in question? One could imagine a disk with its front lower than its rear (or vice versa) or with its left edge lower than its right (or vice versa). In the latter case, the object would have the shape of an ellipse. Did the witness have objective reasons to describe a "tilted disk" or was it simply an interpretation? Furthermore, the angular size of the object was very small since an object of 3-4 meters at 10 kilometers distance would be seen under an angle of 1' to 1.4' (0.15 to 0.2 mm at arm's length), which is the limit of human eye resolution (in high contrast and luminance). The pilot flying at a heading of 250° was almost facing the sun (azimuth 244°, elevation 18°): one cannot rule out a reflection of the sun off a small object much closer (balloon) or even off the cockpit window. The GEIPAN classified this case as C due to lack of information and corroboration.
