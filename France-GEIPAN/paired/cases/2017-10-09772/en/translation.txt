Summary: Observations of a point then three bright points in the sky and extinction: observation of an airplane in flight.
--- seg-8d58e483a76dc85505ee25f6 [needs-review] ---
Description: On October 31, 2017, at 6:05 PM, three witnesses in a garden were amazed to see a bright point to the east. The witnesses then notice the presence of three white glowing points that seem to be at each end of an anthracite object. No noise is heard. The lights go out and the object disappears completely. The witnesses observed an aircraft on a long, straight trajectory towards them, giving the appearance of near-stationary flight in the sky. The plane was approaching them with its headlights on, producing a bright light in the fading evening (6:00 PM). This is not a regular flight, but an Airbus test flight. The location, altitude, and speed of these flights are often unusual and have created a sense of strangeness for the witnesses. The intermediate altitude (4000 m) and the test context can explain the flight with lights on, as these are very visible at over 100 km. The attached reconstruction shows the portion of the aircraft's trajectory that was long oriented towards the observation location. This portion of the trajectory is over a length of more than 60 km, which corresponds to a duration of approximately ten minutes (for an aircraft flying at 200 kt, or 360 km/h). The hours and durations are consistent with witness statements, considering the imprecision (a few minutes) of the aircraft's position reports and witness datings. It is verified that the direction of observation of the PAN by the witnesses (around 103° Azimuth, found according to the photograph taken by T1 which contains ground references and the Moon) corresponds to that according to which the plane is seen. At the beginning of the observation, the plane is approximately 100 km away, and at the end of the aligned portion, the plane is about 40 km away. This corresponds to an airplane flying at an altitude of approximately 4000 meters with a skyward progression between 2 and 5 degrees, which can be perceived by witnesses as near-stationary. The photo of the PAN next to the Moon at the end of the observation presents a configuration consistent with the respective positions of the plane relative to the Moon (the Moon is at 18° altitude when the plane is at 5°). The dimming of the PAN's intensity is caused by the beginning of the aircraft's change in heading and the loss of optimal alignment of the headlights. The final extinction corresponds to a complete change of course, the aircraft being less than 30 Km away. Witnesses report distinct triangular light points at the end of the observation. T1 says "the light dimmed gradually, allowing us to see that it was three lights in a triangle." The witnesses were able to distinguish the plane's headlights (here A350/900) as they are spaced approximately 12.5 meters apart for both on the wings, and the normal human eye's separation capability is around 1/3500 of a radian, which equates to 8.5 meters at 30 kilometers, and 6 meters at 20 kilometers (the trajectory report does not allow for the precise determination of the minimum distance). In this hypothesis, the third light in the triangle would then be the lighthouse located on the front train. It is entirely possible that the landing gear deployed during this low-altitude and low-speed test flight. The triangle formed by the 3 lighthouses has a height of 14.5 m and a base of 12.5 m. When the aircraft is at a zero attitude, this triangle is tilted down by 18° because the forward light is 4.5 meters lower. But the aircraft is seen from the ground at a height in the sky of 5°, so the triangle is seen at a shallow angle of 13° for an aircraft with no pitch. In this case, the central lighthouse has an apparent distance of 7 m from the two lateral ones and can be distinguished. It is, however, difficult to detect a central beacon lower than the others and thus a triangle, even less considering that the plane may have a pitch attitude that would thus mitigate or even cancel the steep angle of vision of the triangle from the ground. Another phenomenon can explain the three bright points. It is noted that they are distinguished when the initial, unique light weakens. This light is then that of the headlights which fades away until disappearing because of the plane's turn. At its altitude of 4000 m, the aircraft is still directly illuminated by the sun, even though it is located at 2° below the local horizon (at 18:05). During the change in direction, the rear vertical stabilizer and the two vertical "winglets" at the wingtips could have been in a sun-mirroring position while previously the aircraft presented no flat surfaces facing the sun. It is also possible that the roll angle caused by this turn puts significant portions of the undersides of both wings and the horizontal tail in a mirror position. The sun reflection hypothesis better explains the ability to distinguish three lights points by the great separation (more than 60 m) between the points and by the shape of a triangle. This explanation has its plausibility; it cannot be proven or disproven. This unknown element is not in a position to invalidate or even diminish the airplane explanation that corresponds perfectly in geometry and time to the position of the PAN observed. We have three witnesses. Nevertheless, the overall testimony lacks consistency on several points. The questionnaires translate into possibilities of influence between witnesses. There are expressions borrowed and the use of "we". Above all, despite the repeated request from GEIPAN, the witnesses were unable to provide the source files of the photographs with the attached metadata. It was therefore not possible to use these photos to specifically find or precisely analyze:

- the moments of taking the photos and observation (a key element in deepening the hypothesis of a sun reflection) that are the subject of certain inconsistencies or differences between witnesses.
- the position of the Pan in the sky.
- the structure (pointual or not) of the light. As a result, the GEIPAN classifies the case as A: airplane observation.
