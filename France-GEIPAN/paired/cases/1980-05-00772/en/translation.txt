**Description:** The GEIPAN continues to publish its entire archive on its public website www.geipan.fr. Among its publications are older cases classified at the time (A, B, C, or D) that are now being re-examined to enhance the conclusions. Through new technical means (software) and investigative experience gained over the years, this re-examination sometimes leads to new observations or a change in classification. The case of observation previously classified as D and named TOURNON (07) 1980 is part of a recent batch of re-examined cases. On May 8, 1980, around 10:30 PM, military personnel, during night firing exercises, observed a peculiar phenomenon above the training ground. Their attention was drawn to a bright, white-light emitting point. The witnesses described an object moving towards them from the west, getting closer and farther without making a sound. The observation lasted a long time before the object disappeared to the west. These testimonies from career military personnel, as reported in the gendarmerie protocol, exhibit characteristics of an astronomical observation. These are now well-known features of the GEIPAN with the accumulation of explained cases of this particular type, especially over the last decade (see www.geipan.fr website and search for cases involving astronomical phenomena, comets, moons, sun).

- **Long observation duration:** Over 30 minutes, at a low altitude in the sky, nearly stationary or very slow trajectory. None of the witnesses described any angular movement of the PAN, despite the observation lasting over an hour.
- **Back-and-forth movement sensation:** The witness who interpreted the astronomical body as a close object naturally attributed any variation in its light intensity as a movement towards or away from the object, which would maintain its own constant brightness. T2 states, "This bright point must have been quite far away, above the mountain! It drew closer, becoming as big as a soccer ball, and was extremely bright. It stopped facing us, in the direction of the targets, and its brightness illuminated the area where we were." T1 states, "This object then left at a speed much faster than it arrived. When this craft left, we continued our firing session. About 10-12 minutes after it had moved away, the craft returned but at a much greater distance."
- **Unidentification of a remarkable star:** Despite its extreme brightness and presence in the direction of the observation, the witnesses did not identify it as a star because the sky was described as clear and starry. It was verified that it was indeed Venus. The direction of Venus (moving from azimuth 290° to 300°) is consistent with T1's observation, the only witness who provided a direction: "This phenomenon came from the west. It left in the same direction." (The back-and-forth movement is an optical illusion.) The initial misunderstanding, which leads to the other (forward-backward) perception, is due to the failure to recognize Venus. Venus is the source of many misunderstandings. It is the brightest star from a distance (here magnitude -4.5), visible at low elevation in the sky (here from 16° to 11°), and its light passes through a thick atmospheric layer, creating variable effects, including changes in brightness, color distortion, halos, and even oscillations due to atmospheric turbulence. In this case, the PAN is seen as yellow or white, with varying intensities. All these effects exist even in the absence of clouds and can be enhanced (changes in intensity, almost disappearing) by the presence of cloud patches that are otherwise invisible. It was verified that Venus is indeed visible given the surrounding terrain: the elevation-limiting obstacle in the direction of Venus from the firing range is Mont de Rosier or Cerisier, 700 meters high, a 200-meter elevation change from the firing range to more than 1.5 km, or a 8° angle, too low to obscure Venus. The witnesses' indications regarding the direction of the PAN are not precise enough to ensure an exact match with Venus. However, the witnesses confirm the absence of clouds and "a starry sky," they observed to the west, at a height similar to the mountains, and did not notice Venus, which is the "obvious star" when looking at this part of the sky (other bright stars are Capella and Procyon, much fainter, magnitude 4 points lower!). If the PAN had not been Venus, Venus would have been used to identify it. Other strange elements, such as the PAN's "distance" when the lights are directed at it or the watch that was fast the next day, are mere coincidences that caught the witnesses' attention due to the existing atmosphere of strangeness. The above elements leave no doubt. The GEIPAN classifies the case as A: observation of Venus. These testimonies are imprecise in terms of information. They illustrate the strangeness that an astronomical body, here Venus, can create, even for career military personnel.
