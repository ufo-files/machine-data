        This is a final response to your May 5, 2022 Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) request,
a copy of which is enclosed for your convenience. We received your request on May 5, 2022,
and assigned it FOIA case number 22-F-0898. We ask that you use this number when referring
to your request.

       The Office of the Assistant to the Secretary of Defense, Privacy, Civil Liberties, and
Transparency, a component of the Office of the Secretary of Defense, conducted a search of their
records systems and located 96 pages determined to be responsive to your request. Ms. Lyn
Kirby, Director, in her capacity as an Initial Denial Authority, has determined that portions of the
responsive pages are exempt from mandatory disclosure pursuant to the Privacy Act, Title 5
U.S.C. § 552a and the Freedom of Information Act, Title 5 U.S.C. § 552. Access rights under the
Privacy Act are limited to information that is about you, and it was determined that the 96 pages
located contain information about a party other than yourself. This information is also exempt
from disclosure pursuant to 5 U.S.C. § 552 (b)(6), which protects information, the release of
which would constitute a clearly unwarranted invasion of the personal privacy of third parties.
Please note that we have considered the foreseeable harm standard when reviewing records and
applying exemptions under the FOIA in the processing of this request.

      In this instance, fees for processing your request were below the threshold for requiring
payment. Please note that fees may be assessed on future requests.

 IMPORTANT NOTE TO REVIEWERS OF CLASSIFIED INFORMATION
Executive Order (EO), 13526. "Classified National Security Information." December 29, 2009, prescribes a uniform
system for classifying, safeguarding, and declassifying national security information including information relating to
defense against transnational terrorism. The order provides for the classification of information for up to 25 years under
Tier I criteria and for classification beyond 25 years under Tier II.

TIER I Qnformation less than 25 years old)

Under Section 1.4 of the Order, information shall not be considered for original classification unless its unauthorized
disclosure could reasonably be expected to cause identifiable or describable damage to the national security, and it
pertains to one or more of the following:

    a) Military plans, weapons, or operations;
    b) Foreign government information;
    c) Intelligence activities (including covert action), intelligence sources or methods, or cryptology;
    d) Foreign relations or foreign activities of the United States, including confidential sources;
    e) Scientific, technological, or economic matters relating to the national security;
    f) United States Government programs for safeguarding nuclear materials or facilities;
    g) Vulnerabilities or capability of systems, installations, infrastructures, projects, plans or protection services relating
       to the national security, or
    h) The development, production or use of weapons of mass destruction.

When reviewing classified information in response to a FOIA request, the basis for classification is also the basis for the
continuation of that classification and denial under FOIA exemption (b )( 1).

TIER II Qoformation more than 25 years old)

Information which is of permanent historical value and more than 25 years old must meet one of nine criteria as described
in Section 3.3(b) of the Order to qualify for continued classification protection. The criteria in the second tier are more
narrowly defined. To be exempt from automatic declassification, the release of the information could be clearly and
demonstrably expected to:

    I) Reveal the identity of a confidential human source, a human intelligence source, a relationship with an
       intelligence or security service of a foreign government or international organization, or a nonhuman intelligence
       source; or impair the effectiveness of an intelligence method currently in use, available for use, or under
       development;
    2) Reveal information that would assist in the development, production, or use of weapons of mass destruction;
    3) Reveal information that would impair U.S. cryptologic systems or activities;
    4) Reveal information that would impair the application of state of the art technology within a U.S. weapon system;
    5) Reveal formally named or numbered U.S. military war plans that remain in effect, or reveal operational or tactical
       elements of prior plans that are contained in such active plans;
    6) Reveal information, including foreign government information, that would cause serious harm to relations
       between the United States and a foreign government, or to ongoing diplomatic activities of the United States;
    7) Reveal information that would impair the current ability of United States Government officials to protect the
       President, Vice President, and other protectees for whom protection services, in the interest of national security,
       are authorized;
    8) Reveal information that would seriously and demonstrably impair current national security emergency
       preparedness plans or reveal current vulnerabilities of systems, installations, infrastructures, relating to the
       national security; or
    9) Violate a statute, treaty, or international agreement that does not permit the automatic or unilateral declassification
       of information at 25 years.

   LANDMARK SUPREME COURT RULING ON THE USE OF FOIA EXEMPTION
                                              illlill
On March 7, 2011, in Milner v. Navy, the U.S. Supreme Court decided that FOIA Exemption
(b)(2) can only be applied consistent with the plain meaning of the term "personnel rules and
practices" which encompasses only records relating to issues of employee relations and human
resources. This ruling effectively negates the use of (b)(2) high.
Therefore, Exemption 2 can only shield those records relating to "personnel rules and practices" .
An agency's "personnel rules and practices" all share a critical feature: They concern conditions
of employment in federal agencies-such matters as hiring and firing, work rules and discipline,
compensation and benefits.

If you have information that you were protecting, or planning to protect, under (b)(2), you should
re-evaluate the information and make one of the following determinations:

       a. Release the information.

       b. Consider using the narrow, strict reading of exemption 2 that the Court requires, i.e.
"personnel rules and practices".

       c. Evaluate the information to determine if it should have originally been classified.
Consult E.O. 13526, Section l .7(d) for procedures concerning classification or reclassification of
information after the receipt of a FOIA request.

        d. Evaluate the use of Exemptions (b)(7)(E) and (b)(7)(F). Keep in mind that Federal
agencies must meet the threshold requirements of Exemption 7 before they may withhold
information on the basis of any of its subparts. That threshold requires the records or
information to be "compiled for law enforcement purposes". Exemption (7)(E) affords
protection to all law enforcement information that would disclose techniques and procedures for
law enforcement investigations or prosecutions, or would disclose guidelines for law
enforcement investigation or prosecutions if such disclosure could reasonably be expected to risk
circumvention of the law. Exemption (7)(F) protects law enforcement information that could
reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any individual.

Agencies need not show that files are "investigatory" in order to withhold records under FOIA
Exemption (b)(7) (E) and (b)(7)(F), but they must show that the documents were compiled for
criminal, civil or other law enforcement purposes. The United States has been in a state of
national emergency continuously since the aftermath of Sept. 11, 2001, thereby qualifying
documents that contain critical infrastructure information, homeland security or national security
information to be withheld for law enforcement purposes under FOIA Exemptions (b)(7)(E) and
(b)(7)(F). Generally, in order to withhold information under these Exemptions, agencies must
demonstrate the harm that could reasonably be expected to occur.

In your analysis of whether the information qualifies for protection under Exemption (b)(7)(E) or
(b)(7)(F), you may wish to consider the following court decisions in crafting your basis for
exempting the information. You may also wish to consult the Department of Justice Guide to the
Freedom oflnformation Act available at: http://www.justice.gov/oip/foia guide09.htm for
assistance in interpreting these exemptions.

Edmonds, 272 F. Supp. at 55, states that the "deference that has historically been extended to the
executive when it invokes FOIA Exemption 1" must be extended to Exemption 7 in the national
security area.

Pratt, 673 F .2d at 420, applies the two-part test, and allows that the threshold may be satisfied if
the agency's investigatory activities "relate to the enforcement of federal laws or to the
maintenance of national security".

Zadvydas, 533 U.S. at 696, recognizes that terrorism can warrant "heightened deference".

Should you invoke Exemption 7 (E) or (F), your rationale should be articulated on the SD Form
472 in Block 11, "Rationale for Denials". If Block 11 does not provide sufficient space, please
attach a continuation sheet or memo to the SD Form 472.
