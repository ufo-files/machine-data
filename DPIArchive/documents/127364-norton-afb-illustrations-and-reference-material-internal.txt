/ Ine Sun SUNDAY, November 16, 1986
Norton AFB has outlived threats of closure
By JOHN WHITEHAIR
Sun Staff Writer
SAN BERNARDINO - Almost from the day a
dirt runway and biplane hangar were transformed
Force Base has lived with the threat of closure.
So far, the base has survived two orders to pad-
lock its gates. Air Force officials say the base has a
secure future as an air transport center and home of
four important service-wide agencies.
Fretting about the base's closure began in 1943,
effort saw 102 acres of enclosed buildings rise in an
area known as East Third Street, said base histori-
an Clayton H. Snedeker.
In October, Snedeker published a 109-page histo-
ry of the base, the first ever, he said.
Five days after the Dec. 7, 1941, Japanese attack
on Pearl Harbor, the War Department (now the De-
partment of Defense) condemned 962 acres along the
new air depots. The property was owned by Morrow
Aircraft Corp., which had a hangar, a dirt runway
and a pig farm.
Shortly thereafter, former San Bernardino Coun
ty Board of Supervisors Chairman Gene Grier sold
547 adjoining acres to the government for $1 as part
of his personal campaign to have a major defense
installation built in San Bernardino.
Construction crews moved onto the site on March
23, 1942, in the first phase of the $20 million project.
A year later, 26,000 tons of supplies were being
shipped each month to Pacific battlefields from the
warehouses and production shops.
The first commander of the base, which was then
known as the San Bernardino Air Depot, was Brig.
Gen. Lucas V. Beau Jr. His job was difficult, Snedek-
er said, because he had to build a base when con-
struction supplies were limited and employment was
high.
Housing was also scarce, so the government as-
sisted in building homes for base workers. The Wa-
terman Gardens apartments at Base Line and Water-
man Avenue in San Bernardino and a smaller
complex at Lugonia Avenue and Orange Street in
Redlands resulted from that program.
There were other problems, Snedeker said, in-
cluding a shortage of produce that was solved by
opening a 117-acre "victory farm on the base.
By the end of World War II, more than 13,000
civilians and 5,000 military personnel worked at the
base. In addition to shipping war supplies, the base
also was an overhaul depot for aircraft and engines,
including the Army Air Corps' first gas turbine en-
gine repair and training center.
Rumors became so strong that the base was only
a wartime installation, Snedeker said, that the base's
Staff photo
second commander, Col. James G. Taylor, addressed
the San Bernardino Chamber of Commerce on the
issue.
Two days after he took over on Dec. 7, 1943, he
went before the chamber "to stifle rumors that the
(San Bernardino Air Depot) was strictly a wartime
installation."
But the closure order came anyway, and the base
was to be relegated to inactive statust on June 30,
1947. The civilian work force had already been re-
duced to about half.
But a delegation of local business leaders and
chamber officials traveled to Washington, D.C., and
lobbied Congress to keep the base open. They out-
lined its wartime accomplishments and its economic
The delegation had some success, for in 1949 a
new 2.000-member Air Force unit was established at
At the time it was known as the San Bernardino
Air Materiel Area, and by 1951, civilian employment
had climbed to 6,700. Most worked in airplane over-
In 1954, the runway was lengthened to 10,000 feet
to accommodate jets. Several years later, the base
took on a new role as a center for ballistic missile
design and maintenance work.
In the early 1960s, the civilian work force had
grown to 8,300, but the base was ordered phased out
as an airplane and missile maintenance depot on
Nov. 19, 1964.
But another, more formal delegation of civic lead-
ers, Inland Action, was again dispatched to Washing-
ton, D.C., to lobby to keep the base open.
Formed in September 1962, Inland Action is still
an elite group of business and civic leaders that pro-
motes the base and the economic development of the
San Bernardino Valley.
Inland Action, Snedeker said, succeeded in get-
ting the Air Force to station a reactivated transport
unit, the 63rd Military Airlift Wing, at Norton AFB
instead of El Toro Marine Corps Air Station in
Orange County or March AFB in Riverside County.
The wing began operations in 1968 and the base
became an air cargo center for the Vietnam War.
Over the years, the base also became the head-
quarters for the Inspection and Safety Center, the
Audit Agency, the Aerospace Audiovisual Service
and the Ballistic Missile Office. Currently, about
12,000 military personnel and civilian employees
work the base.
