Because of the presence of the transmitter pulse and the

clutter that immediately follows the cessation of the transmitter pulse,

the receiver experiences a certain "dead time" interval before useful

echoes can be obtained. In use, with a 1000-Hz signal propagacing into

limestone, wanted echoes from the first 1O m in range were obscured by,

and indistinguishable from, the transmitted pulse and clutter echoes.

This problem was alleviated by shifting to a frequency of 4000 Hz, in

which case the clutter obscured only the first two meters of the data.

Clutter is a term borrowed from radar, which refers to humerous,
unwanted reflections from scattering objects in the path of the
transmitted beam, Clutter obscures the echoes coming from nearby
targets, and is caused by the particularly intense scattering that
takes place in the immediate vicinity of the transmitter and receiver.

For computerized data reduction, a multiple-channel tape recorder

Early in our previous work we found it was essential to take great
care in mounting the transducers on the rock surface. The transducers
must be held tightly against the walls and ceilings with special pressure
rigging. One rigging tool that has been used with success consists of
a modified carpet installation device (Figures 4 and 5).

Several types of water-based thixotropic gels have been used for a
coupling agent between the rock surface and the transducers, Flexible
plastic wrapping material was used to cover decorated walls and ceilings
before the gel was applied (Figure 5). Using this method, it is possible
to avoid staining, marking, or otherwise damaging chamber walls or ceilings.
Mounting the transducer carefully to ensure geod coupling was our most
critical and time-consuming task during the last field trip. It is
proposed to develop a more rapid coupling technique to speed data acquisi-

The first strong echo we saw on the A-scope had an arrival time of about
12.5 ms after the beginning of the transmitted pulse. Assuming a velocity
of 950 m/s, the sonic path is 11.9 m. Since the acoustic pulse travels

4 UCwo-wey trip distance--to the target and back--the actual range was

half the total distance of 11.9 m, or 5.9 m. This value ig in good agree-
ment with what we expected from physical measurements of the location of
the tomb.

The second sounding path used, which gave smaller but better defined
echoes, was from the roof of Tutankhamun's annex tO Che entrance ramp of
the tomb of Ramses VI (see Figure 11). The sounder position was offset
Several meters to the south of the ramp leading to Ramses VI's tomb.

The distance from the annex roof at this position to the Ramses ramp
floor varies from 7.5 to 9.5 m; from the side wall of the ramp, the range
igs even greater, The echo we observed at 7.8 m lies within the predicted
range interval (see Figure 11). The next echo, at 11.5 m range, is prob-
ably from the ground surface, which we estimate to be 11.25 m away. The
early echo at 3.8 m is from the fault previously noted, which measured
4.25 m to the southeast. The farthest echo was 18 m distant; it is an
echo source that cannot be identified without further measurements.

During the work in the antechamber of Tutankhamun the first sub-
stantial unexplained echoes were seen. These were two sets of mutually
consistent echoes to the south and west of the chamber at a range of
adout 8 and 16 m. The importance of taking data at close spatial intervals
was illustrated by this work, in that far from not seeing any echoes at
all, our problem was that we observed a great many and did not have the
time to eliminate those caused by known sources by means of acoustic
interrogation from other sites. Figures 12 snd 13 show the equipment

in operation in the tombs.

In all, some 500 acoustic soundings were taken during the 1976-77
experiments, and in none of the data was there any indication that areas
such as the Valley of the Kings could not be sounded effectively with
the acoustic technique.
