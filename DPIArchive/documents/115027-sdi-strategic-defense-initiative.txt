No party for Star Wars
The Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) celebrated its ninth anniversary in
March (1992), at about the same time that "star Wars" costs passed the $30
billion mark - making it by far the most expensive military project in
history.
The March 31 Defense Media Review, a publication of the Center for Defense
Journalism,

with the exception of Newsweek, the Review continued, the media
marked the event "with little more than a whimper."
Newsweek (March 23) did review the record, noting that as the program's
X-ray laser, neutral- and charged-particle beams, and electromagnetic
rail
guns each failed to work out as their proponents claimed, SDI "evolved"
into a less ambitious but no less costly program. Or as Elliott Kennel, a
former air force nuclear engineer, put it, by 1987 he and many of his
colleagues at SDI "realized that the game was over."
Anniversary coverage was more of a roast than a celebration. Kosta Tsipis,
director of MIT's Program in Science and Technology for International
Security, described SDI simply: "It's a fraud." And Richard Garwin, a
weapons consultant who has advised every administration since Eisenhower's,
reiterated his belief that SDI's goals were technologically "impossible to
accomplish" (Philadelphia Inquirer, March 22, 1992).
The Star Warriors were also busy fending off charges by whistleblower
Aldric Saucier, an SDI analyst who accused SDI officials of falsifying
data, redirecting money Congress allocated for one program to others, and
underestimating various costs from between $222 billion and $1 trillion.
Saucier also said that SDI hid or destroyed hundreds of scientific reports
that found flaws in its systems. SDI officials had sacked Saucier, and
dismissed his charges with dark hints about his sanity. Then in April, an
independent inquiry led to Saucier's reinstatement. But the Pentagon soon
suspended him, this time claiming that he had made misleading statements on
his Defense Department job application- 23 years ago.
