 Foo fighters fly again:


 Not all mid air sightings are easy to resolve. During World War Two a strange phenomenon in the form of balls
 of light plagued Allied aircraft engaged in bombing raids on German bases such as Peenemunde. The USAF
 crews gave these the nickname `foo fighters' from the French word `feu' for fire, and a strip cartoon then popular
 in which the character used the expression - `foo'.

"No explanation for foo fighters was ever found. They were not secret weapons as first assumed. German and
 Japanese crews saw them too and they never posed a threat to the aircraft. It is believed they were some form of
 atmospheric phenomena first recognised in the 1940's because this was when the air was seriously flown by
 thousands of aircraft at once rather than as before just the occasional pioneer aviator.

 What is significant is that foo fighters are still seen today. For example, on 28 January 1995 (just three weeks
 after the British Airways close encounter) Mrs              from Bradford was walking heir dog around 6.30 pm on a
 dark, windy night. Living by. the flight path into Leeds/Bradford Airport she often saw planes but this one — a
 small turboprop buffeted by the gales — had a companion. She reports seeing a bright white light heading
 towards the aircraft.. .only a few inches in diameter it was `escorting' the aircraft just a few feet away from it. It
 was almost as if it was guiding the plane towards a safe landing.. .The light travelled with the aircraft a short way
 towards Yeadon Airport and then fl ew off at a sharp angle disappearing into the night.

 At just that time on that very night I landed in a Jersey European Airways turbo prop aircraft on my return from
 Ireland where I had been on a TV chat show talking about UFOs. The gale force winds had delayed our flight
 from Belfast to Blackpool by several hours. We had been unable to land at the wind lashed seaside resort and
 were diverted onwards to Leeds/Bradford instead. Nobody on board saw the UFO. We were too concerned with
 getting down in one piece! But it seems we did have a UFO escort.

  A daylight encounter of this sort occurred in April 1980 at Shipston-on-Stour, Warwickshire, when witness Kevin
  Owen describes how a small white dot was `playing tag' with a light plane flying overhead. He and his brother
  watched this through a small telescope. It was a silvery ball. After some minutes it `bounced' off at an angle and
  headed into the sky at great speed.

  Many cases of this type are on record, indicating some phenomenon can attach itself to aircraft.

  Close Encounters:


  In cases where small balls of light `tag' aircraft - perhaps because they home ir1 on the metal as a lightning bolt is
  attracted in the same way - it is rare for people on board to see anything. But that is certainly not the case for
  another type of sighting. Lozenge shaped objects, dark brown or black in colour, have been reported in close
  proximity to dozens of aircraft in recent years. They have been pursued by military fighters and rated as a definite
  threat to life and limb by commercial pilots and air traffic control.

  A frightening example befell a Britannia Airways holiday charter flight from Crete to London Gatwick, exactly
  the sort of journey undertaken by millions of people every year in ignorance of the threat posed. It was 15 July
  1991 and the Boeing 737 was approaching its destination on a warm sunny evening. At about 5.30 pm whilst
  descending through 15,000 feet over Sussex the co pilot spotted the UFO, yelled to his colleague and they both
  watched as the thing rushed past their right wing. It came within 300 feet — which in air miss terms is dangerously
  close. The thing resembled a small black lozenge.

  Upon touchdown the incident was reported as a near miss, collision risk was rated `high' by the crew and the 737
  was even inspected for possible damage in case an impact had occurred. As the CAA investigation proved both
  the Britannia plane and the UFO had been tracked on Gatwick radar. The echo that crossed the path of the fully
  loaded jet was seen to streak away at a speed of 120 mph. It then changed direction and disappeared over the
  Channel. Because no electronic code signal was detected by radar we can be sure that it was not a recognised

 Fdtal Contact:

 If UFOs are flying so close to commercial air traffic are there any occasions where disaster was not averted and
 tragedy actually resulted? It seems that there could well have been.

  On 24 August 1984 a Britten-Norman Trislander operated by Kondair was ferrying freight from Stansted to
  Holland when it was struck mid flight by some heavy object as it crossed the famed tJFO hotspot of Rendlesham
  Forest, Suffolk. The plane lost an engine and had a hole punched through the fuselage. Thankfully the pilot
  brought it safely to land at Amsterdam. What hit the aircraft is unexplained.

  An Aer Lingus Viscount was-much less fortunate when it faced still baffling circumstances on 24 March 1968.
  The final poignant words of the pilot of the Cork to London shuttle reported descending, spinning rapidly'
  before the plane and all 61 people on board plunged into the cold waters of the Irish Sea. Despite a massive
  investigation no cause was ever found. It was not a bomb blast. The most likely option was that the Viscount
  suddenly collided with another aircraft.. As the Irish Inspector of Transport, Richard O'Sullivan, reported in June
  1970 after exhausting all hope of a solution — `the conclusion that there was another aircraft involved is
  inescapable.' But what sort of aircraft? None were missing and none were ever traced to the area at the
  appropriate time — or rather no conventional aircraft were.

  However, witnesses at Carnsore Point, Ireland, beyond which the Viscount was lost reported seeing a cloud in the
  sky out of which emerged the now ominously familiar `dark cigar-like object' — seemingly very much like that
  which nearly struck the Gatwick bound Britannia 737 some 23 years later.

  Another near identical object flew past the cockpit (and was tracked by London radar) as an Alitalia jet headed
  across Kent descending into London Heathrow in April 1991. One also flew past Australian Airlines Flight 405
  over Tasmania on 14 September 1992. A long black lozenge crossed the path of a military helicopter near
  Brignoles, France, on 8 July 1992. On 9 August 1997 a Swissair Boeing 747 flying from Philadelphia to Zurich
  encountered a `long, dark wingless' cylinder that came `dangerously close' as they headed towards Boston. These
  are just a few of the cases on record.

  The list of such identical encounters is constantly increasing. For Alitalia, Swissair, Australian Airlines and
  dozens of others disaster did not follow. For Aer Lingus, the unthinkable tragedy struck. It would seem a matter
  of terrible certainty that it will strike again unless we put aside our natural caution and inherent phobias about
  reporting UFOs and learn precisely what is flying through our airspace undetected.
