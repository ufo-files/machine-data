EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This advanced diesel techology test and demonstration project was conducted
by Mobile Energy Research Center, CBE, Inc. with the cooperation of Ameri-
can General Transportation, Inc.. External guidance and financial assist-
ance was also provided. The project consisted of both laboratory and on-
board testing and demonstration of a unique air treatment process which
"treat%!% r "condition% 'he total inlet air of diesel engines. The charge
air pre-cooler system (CAPCS) includes a unique application of the venturi
principle in which water is converted into a vapor state in the inlet air
stream. The result is a significant mass flow improvement of the intake air
as evidenced by reduced temperatures, improved horsepower, improved brake
specific fuel consumption, and a reduction of all noxious emissions to in-
clude smoke/soot. The new technology establishes that use of water vapor
inducted via the CAPCS process provides a novel means of introducing subtle
combustion precursor chemical reactions, with the benefit of increased cy-
cle efficiency. Subject project was designed to ascertain if previously
reported benefits associated with use of the CAPC system could be demon-
strated on and applied to the larger diesel marine engines common to the
tow/push-boats utilized on the inland waterways.
Results of both the shoreside testing and onboard demonstration phases were
basically positive in nature. The results confirmed previous findings that
the system is more efficient when applied to four-cycle engines than two-cy-
cle engines, due to the large air scavenging requirements of two-cycle engines.
A medium length onboard demonstration of the CAPCS was conducted on the M/V
GENERAL WASHINGTON, a push-boat operating on the inland waterways of the
southern United States.

1.O BACKGROUND
The CBE/MERG original research of the charge air pre-cooler system concept
began in early 1974 with concentration on gasoline fueled engines. During
the next six years, tests were conducted on gasoline engines utilized in a
number of operational modes such as: automobiles, automobiles and pickup
trucks while towing, medium sized trucks, small boats, generators, pumps,
race cars, recreational vehicles, and one airplane. Although the horse-
power and fuel economy results varied considerably, relative to the utili-
zation and load factor, they were consistently of a positive nature. Lower
noxious emissions were also observed in those instances where emissions
were measured. During high engine load periods such as in racing, towing,
and marine utilization, significant fuel economy savings were reported.
During the period 1979-80, a series of standard Highway Fuel Economy Tests
were conducted under Federal Test Procedures (FTP) by an independent testing
laboratory regarding the long term effect of the CAPCS on the engine of a
1979 Dodge Colt. From the initial test at CAPCS installation (9000 miles)
until the last series was conducted after 30,000 plus miles with the CAPCS
installed, the miles per gallon (mpg) was increased by 5.15 mpg or 13.2%. (1)
In June 1979, a series of engine dynamometer tests of the CAPCS were con-
ducted on a 327 CID, 12.5/CR racing engine. The results were published in
the April 1980 issue of Super Stock. (2) The dynamometer tests disclosed
that at 3000 rpm, (the optimum rpm/air flow match with that CAPCS unit) a
gain of 33.2 horsepower or 24.9% was achieved, while utilizing 92 octane
luel. At that rpm, the GAPCS also enabled a slightly higher horsepower
output with 92 octane fuel than achieved with 105 octane fuel, (166.1 vs.
162.8 hp).
During 1979-80, while in the latter stage of the primary gasoline research
period, experimental use of the CAPCS was extended to gasoline fueled race
cars. In the first fourteen races in which the CAPCS was installed, and
the vehicles finished the race, there were twelve first place and two sec-
ond place finishes. There were no failures of the CAPCS equipped engines.
The U.S. Army Race Team reported that use of the CAPCS: 1) provided in-
creased horsepower, 2) improved speed sufficiently to reduce lap time by an
average of 4 seconds per lap, and 3) improved fuel economy by an average of
50% during dynamometer testing, practice, and racing conditions. Existing
U.S. and World Land Speed records in the I-GT Class were broken by the
CAPCS equipped Army Race Team vehicle. Engine dynamometer tests conducted
in 1980 for the U.S. Army Race Team disclosed that use of the CAPCS was re-
sponsible for horsepower gains on a small 1000 cc, 12.5/0r racing engine,
even when 125 octane So. Cal. Blue racing fuel was used. The gain of 4.4%
was attained in a brief test phase of less than 10 minutes. (3)(4)
On April 22, 1983, an independent laboratory conducted a series of chassis
dyno tests on a 1977 pickup truck (351 CID engine) set to run on propane.
Test results disclosed that installation of a CAPCS improved fuel economy

2.0 INTRODUCTION
2.1
Introduction
This advanced diesel technology test and demonstration project was designed
to determine if the established benefits of increased power and reduced
emissions, previously associated with use of the charge air pre-cooler insert
(CAPC) or charge air pre-cooler system (CAPCS) could be replicated in larger
type marine diesel engines, under different operating environments, and if
these attributes could be quantified. The onboard phases of the project were
conducted with the cooperation and support of American General Transportation,
Incorporated, owner/operator of the demonstration vessel, the M/V GENERAL
WASHINGTON. Both the onboard and shoreside phases were supervised and/or
conducted by the Mobile Energy Research Center, CBE, Inc. with external
guidance and financial support.
The CAPC unit can be considered a porous ceramic venturi device installed
immediately before the air intake manifold. It relies upon the basic Bor-
nouellian principles to achieve velocity increase/pressure drop; with the
pressure drop providing the mechanism for water, under back pressure, to
be transmitted through the porous ceramic material and vaporized in the air
inlet stream.
The result is a device which "conditions¿¿ or "treats'¿ ¿the inlet air of the
diesel engine by adding moisture.
The addition of moisture to the intake
air of any type of internal combustion engine has been shown to improve
horsepower output and performance. The general theory is well known; how-
ever, the CAPCS unit is unique in concept and principle of simultaneously
achieving the addition of moisture to intake air, decrease in air tempera-
ture, increase in air density, and increase in air velocity. The combina-
tion of these factors, plus possibly an unknown aspect, frequently results
in improved engine performance.
As previously stated, the CAPCS has been applied in limited quantities to
several engine types, including small two and four-cycle diesel truck en-
gines up to 475 hp over the past six years, and has been shown to provide
the claimed benefits; however, it has not previously been applied to the
larger two and four-cycle marine diesels typically found aboard inland wa-
terway towboats. The development of subject project was predicated on the
previous reported successes and was designed to ascertain if the CAPCS
technology is suitable for use on the larger marine diesel engines.
2.2 Technical Description
The CAPC, or heart of the system, is a porous ceramic membrane, shaped as a
venturi, which is surrounded by a water retaining jacket. The ceramic unit
is then installed in the intake air stream. The air passage in the unit is

{"alpha_words": 439, "chars": 2887, "method": "ocr", "page": 15, "variant": "plain-vision"}
of sufficient size to allow movement of high quantities of intake air with
minimum restriction. At the point of maximum pressure differential, or the
venturi throat, minute water or alcohol droplets are drawn through the men-
brane and picked up by the incoming air stream. The greater the air flow,
the greater the pressure differential (negative) and hence, greater rate of
migration. The CAPC, or ceramic insert, is installed in the intake air
stream to enable treatment and enrichment of the total air flow. Support-
ing water tanks, pump, solenoid, filter, and necessary hoses to complete
the CAPCS are also installed. The broad engine response to the process has
been demonstrated by successful application to engines operated on either
gasoline, propane, or diesel fuels.
2.3 Theory of Operation
No single theory has been developed which fully explains either the scope
or magnitude of the combustion, or cycle efficiency, changes brought about
by use ol the CAPGS. However, in relation to charge air, when the known
effects of humidity, reduced temperatures, increased density, and increased
velocity are combined, they explain in large measure the mass flow improve-
ments and the overall positive results achieved by use of the system.
2.4 General Effects of CAPCS
Recognizing that accepted combustion theory is counter to successfully in-
creasing torque/horsepower simultaneously with reduced peak cylinder pres-
sures, extensive empirical data to include laboratory testing and several
million highway test miles confirm that use of the CAPCS results in a slow-
ing of the rate of burn. The result of the retardation of the combustion
process is reduced peak cylinder pressures, improved mean cycle efficiency,
and more effective power strokes.
The new technology clearly establishes
that the use of water vapor inducted via the CAPCS process provides a novel
means of introducing subtle combustion precursor chemical reactions which
tend to control the more normal explosive chemical reactions, with the ben-
efit of increased cycle efficiency, (i.e. the positioning of the rate of
heat release with respect to the geometry of the engine cycle or expansion,
or net work extraction process). (14) The lowered peak pressure also reduc-
es those aspects of engine wear normally associated with higher combustion
pressures. Cylinder temperatures normally are slightly reduced or remain
approximately the same, and the exhaust gas temperatures are reduced. The
general effect is that the system performs as an octane/cetane enhancement
factor in the combustion process.
Improved combustion efficiency of engines
equipped with the CAPCS is normally shown by a gain in horsepower and a re-
duction in required fuel.

data of a practical nature regarding use of the CAPCS on engines either in

with specific emphasis on horsepower, fuel economy, and emission changes.

3.0 TECHNICAL APPROACH
The original approach to the project was to restrict all demonstrations and
evaluations exclusively to an underway situation. A thorough review of all
of the parameters established the strong desirability of conducting limited
shoreside laboratory testing in order to provide sizing and placement data
relative to optimum CAPCS installations, prior to the onboard demonstration.
A further contribution to the project, by the laboratory tests, was to be
the determination of the effect of the CAPCS on power changes, fuel consump-

3.6.1 Torque Measurement Equipment
Torque measurement was accomplished by the attachment of strain gauges to
the port main propulsion shaft. The strain gauge bridge was connected by
wire to an FM radio transmitter powered by an internal 12 volt DC Lithium
battery. The transmitter assembly was strapped to the shaft. A loop an-
tenna was mounted forward of the transmitter and received the FM signals
transmitted from the strain gauge bridge. The installation is shown by
Figure 3.1. These signals were transmitted via cable to a data logger/re-
corder installed in the vessel's engineer's quarters as shown by Figure 3.2.
A split 60 tooth gear was installed around the main propulsion shaft for-
ward of the strain gauge bridge. A magnetic pickup was mounted to induc-
tively receive 60 pulses per shaft rotation from the gear, and was trans-
mitted via cable to the data logger/recorder. The data logger/recorder
thus received a 0-10 volt DC signal from the strain gauge bridge that was
converted electronically to foot pounds of torque and a 60 pulse per revo-
lution signal that was converted electronically into shaft revolutions per
minute. The specifics of load and operating environment were manually re-
corded for later comparison and analysis along with the torque data.

3.6.2 Fuel Consumption Measurement System
Fuel consumption was measured with a commercially available highly accurate
Turbine activated transducers were installed in
the feed line and return line to each engine. This data was transmitted
electronically via cable to an onboard computer. The computer deduced the
actual consumption from feed minus return, and displayed the results in a
liquid diode readout. A memory chip stored the fuel consumption informa-
tion for each rpm level for print-out on demand. The print-out contains
date and time of print-out, time in hours and minutes at each rpm level.
total fuel consumed at each rpm level, total time, total consumption, aver-
age consumption in gallons per hour (gph), and fuel remaining onboard. The
microprocessor computes average fuel consumption in gph at each rpm level,
and prints this average. Comparison of the print-out tapes, tank soundings
and measurement of added fuel revealed the system to be accurate with I
0.5%. A copy of a standard tape print-out is shown in Figure 3.3.
3.6.3 Smoke Measurement
A probe was mounted via C-clamp midstream in the exhaust stack as illus-
trated in Figure 3.4. The probe was connected by steel mesh covered flexi-
ble tubing to a sampling pump that was compressed air controlled. This al-
lows a constant volume suction for each sample. When the sampling pump is
manually activated, a measured amount of exhaust gas is pulled via the
probe through a filter paper disk. The filter paper disk darkens during
this process and thus gives the measure of the soot content of the exhaust
gases.
The evaluating instrument is then used to take a reading off the
darkened disk photoelectrically. This reading is expressed as a Bosch num-
ber; which is one of the internationally accepted standards of measurement
of soot in exhaust gases. By volume, each sample contains the same amount
of exhaust gases, so a direct comparison of filter disks is possible. The
date, time, and engine operating details were recorded manually for later
specific comparison and analysis.
3.6.4 Exhaust Gas Temperature
Exhaust gas temperature probes were placed in the main propulsion engines
in the exhaust manifolds just forward of the drive side of the turbocharger.
The probes were connected by transmission cable to read-out units that dis-
played exhaust gas temperatures in degrees Fahrenheit. This data was manu-
ally recorded and incorporated into the data base.

Shortly after project initiation, it was found that the exhaust gas temper-

equipped with the CAPCS. Although it can be stated that there was no evi-
dence of increased exhaust gas temperature due to use of the CAPCS; con-

ly took place.

6.0 PROBLEMS ENCOUNTERED DURING TEST SERIES
6.1 Vessel Mission and Schedule
One of the dominant factors affecting both the time requirement and scope
of the onboard demonstration phase was the vessel schedule and hence avail-
ability to project personnel. One of the prime prerequisites of the proj-
ect had been that both the project personnel and the project itself be non-
intrusive into the vessel's primary mission. Despite outstanding coopera-
tion on behalf of the vessel's owner/operator, the test phases had to be
conducted in such a manner as to coincide with the vessel's mission and
scheduling.
On several instances, planned work had to either be postponed
or cancelled, due to non-availability of the vessel.
6.1.1 Operating Environment
Although such factors as wind, waves, tide and current were known, by proj-
ect personnel, to effect vessel operation, at least two additional major
aspects were found to effect acquiring accurate torque/horsepower data.
The dominant effect which is primarily experienced in canals, sounds, and
river operation is the "bottom" effect. Simply stated, the size, loading,
and water displacement of the test vessel plus barges required a certain
water depth for the propellers to function efficiently. In several situa-
tions, mainly in the canals and sounds, the depth was insufficient to allow
efficient engine power utilization. In those instances where the water was
too shallow, the torque/horsepower decreases while specific fuel consump-
tion increased. A similar problem occurred when the stern of the demonstra-
tion vessel came too close to a canal or river bank, and happened solely
A combination of water displacement, proximity
to the bank, plus bank erosion deposits reducing the water depth created a
strong "bank suction". Once involved, the towboat normally had to stop and
back out of the influence of the bank.
Both of these factors reduce the
operational efficiency of the vessel by prolonging transit time, raising
rue. consumption and occasionally involved rudder and/or propeller damage.
The variable operational factors such as wind, waves, tides, currents,
"bottom effect", and "bank suction" frequently create major problems in at-
tempting to compare one trip or set of conditions with another trip or set
of conditions. These nonstandard, nonpredictable factors compound the
problem of accurate comparative analysis of operational performance.
Knowledge of the specific operating environment is as essential in the over-
all comparative analysis process as is the accuracy and reliability of the
performance monitoring equipment used to record the data.

7.0 CONCLUSIONS AND RECOMMENDATIONS
7.1 CAPCS Effect on Overall Vessel Operations
The overall effect of the system has been documented herein. The maximum
potential of the CAPCS to increase shaft horsepower was not fully explored
during normal operations. A dockside or "dead bank" test was conducted just
off the Houston Ship Channel with the device installed on the port engine.
The relatively shallow water precluded testing at higher than 1550 rpm. The
maximum 646 shaft horsepower was achieved at 1550 rpm. This extremely high
shp reading at the 1550 rpm level can be compared to the 465 shp average with
the device under light load conditions @ 1550 rpm and the 503 shp average
with the CAPCS under heavy load conditions @ 1550 engine rpm.
The manufac-
turer's optimum propeller load curve @ 1550 rpm with the engine set at 775
hp shows approximately 500 shp. The full results of the shp readings
achieved between 700 - 1550 engine rpm during the "dead bank" tests are de-
picted in Figure 7.1. The changing schedule and nonavailability of test
vessel, during the remainder of the extended project period, precluded com-
pletion of a similar "dead bank" type test series without the CAPCS installed.
7.2 Recommendation For Future Investigations
the qualitative improvements indicated by past tests on high speed automo-
tive truck engines, the shoreside testing under this project, and the ship-
board testing indicates a need to perform shoreside controlled environmental
tests on larger engines to determine the exact quantitative value of any im-
provements.
