       Once upon a time the very idea of an Unidentified Flying Object under
the control of entities having their origins beyond the particular set of                                         �
space-time co-ordinates we habitually designate as "Earth" would have seemed
so strange as to be quite incredible. It is still strange but, thanks to the
reports     of   thousands        of    reputable       witnesses   world-wide,       it    is   no    longer
incredible. Indeed, if the findings of public opinion polls are to be trusted,
most people nowadays do accept flying saucers as a reality and there is even
an increasing credibility for the belief that some of them also abduct people.


       Strangeness is, however, a relative term and some can tolerate more of
it than can others, yet everyone seems to have a point beyond which what was
hitherto      merely     strange         moves     out    into   the     realm   of     the      personally
threatening, thus invoking the defence mechanisms of incredulity and derision.
High strangeness is feared because if it were true the consequences would be
found unthinkable by the individual concerned.                         This fear is conquered only
when, as in the case of J.Allen Hynek, the thinker consciously decides to
think the previously unthinkable - no more swamp gas or weather balloons.


       The location of the threshold of high strangeness, this demarcation line
between acceptable �nd unacceptable,                     is not however immutably fixed,              either
for the individual or for society,                      as Hynek showed.       When Dr.     Olavo Fontes
interviewed       Antonio     Villas       Boas    he    could   handle    the   strangeness          of    the
abduction     story     himself,        but   rightly     judged    it   too   highly      strange     to    be
acceptable       even   to   contemporary          ufologists,      yet   to-day      it    is   generally
accepted as the first reliably investigated UFO abduction.


       High      strangeness       in    France,    as    reported by Aime Michel             and     Jacques
Vallee in the case of Dr. "X" and the mysterious "Monsieur Bied", still relies
for   its   acceptance       on   the    international       repute of     its sponsors.            Even so,
Vallee has hinted darkly that the full story has yet to be told, and fears of
hidden extraterrestrial hands on the levers of political power trigger for
many defence mechanisms of incredulity.                    "If only", say others, "that were the
least of it!      What about the ongoing legacy of the Philadelphia Experiment?"


       That is, of course,              the kind of thing well calculated to produce hasty
invocation of the comforting dogmas of arch-sceptics Menzel and Klass,                                      for
there is of course no hard evidence surviving of   Project Rainbow, classified
in its time more highly than even the nuclear bomb. The Eldridge, rapidly sold
off to a Greek shipping line, is no more and only the lone voice of Alfred
Bielek still claims to tell the tale of this brainchild said to have been
begotten jointly by Einstein, Tesla and von Neumann, each a scientific genius
in his own right.
