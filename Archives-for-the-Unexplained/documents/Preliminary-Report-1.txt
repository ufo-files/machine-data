Preliminary Report # 1

Here follow investigations of the acquired remains of supposed rocket projectiles. These investigations were performed at the State Department Office of Chemical Research (hereafter referred to as FOA 1) during the period from 12/7 to 8/8 in the year of 1946.
The following report describes examinations that were performed on various retrieved objects, thought to be related to observed space projectiles. These objects were delivered to FOA 1 on the eighth of August, 1946.
Each obtained specimen was immediately examined for possible traces of radioactivity by means of a Geiger-Müller counter. Should unsound atomic nuclei emanate subatomic particles, the objects in question would indeed show signs of radioactivity. None of these objects, however, was found to be radioactive.
There are 15 finds in total. Each find has been separately described. These surveys were conducted by laboratory technician Skärblom.
In conclusion, regarding several of these finds it has become abundantly clear that they do not originate from any space projectile form or any meteorite, but other explanations have been considered. Since the remaining findings have yet to be proven to exceed the scope of human knowledge one can draw some conclusions with regard to rocket projectiles or explanations of a similar kind.

               Ulrikedal, on the 9th of August, 1946.

                          G. Ljunggren
                           Professor

Specimen # 1

Specimen was acquired in the community of Njurunda, on the date of 9/7 1946.
Added to FOA 1 on the date of 12/7 1946.
Specimen is accompanied by the following letter from Sundsvalls Journal:
"This specimen was discovered by Secretary-Editor Granberg. The impact which resulted from its fall had left a few feet deep and about one meter wide mark in the sand. This piece was found approximately ten meters away from the pit, where several of the other finds were made as well. Local residents have collected further pieces of scrap metal."
Weight: approximately 20 grams.
Visual inspection: 
Colour rust brown. Influences of a more light-yellow coloured substance. Visible concave cylindrical surfaces could suggest that the object in question has been in contact with a tubular article. The cylindrical surfaces are dark-coloured and the material is glassy and non-porous. It extend ½ -1 mm into the mass. In connection therewith, follow zones of large and small blisters. The larger cells are lined with more glassy material. Characterization of porosity is apparently at least somewhat consistent, as water spreads fairly rapidly when a part of the piece is soaked. On the other hand, it has proved difficult to desiccate the damp portion with laboratory filter paper as water rapidly penetrates the surface. The material is easily processed with a knife. The cylindrical surfaces are significantly harder. 
When viewed under microscope, the material appears glossy and slightly dissolved. The material was processed with a knife and proved to be composed of lighter elements embedded in a structure of harder amber-like components. Grains of sand were found in its cavities, which in turn appear vigorous. By breaking apart the material it proved to contain cavities, around which the material is composed of a geode-like structure resembling the structure of agate. In the centre of these geodes, the material is coloured light-yellow and appears quite soft. Outwardly, it hardens and shifts into a dark brown transparent layer, approximately ten millimeters thick. This layer appers tough.
Upon closer examination of the previously designated concave cylindrical surface, it appeared that a small portion of the porous mass had penetrated inside the cylindrical surface. The surface was in all likely hood pierced and has also shown to not be fully cylindrical. It cannot be excluded that the nature of this surface has resulted from contact with a tree branch or the like. By processing the material with a file, a balsamic odor reminiscent of benzaldehyde and nitrobenzene was noted.
Volume and weight determined by the displacement method:
0.8 to 0.9
How specimen reacts to heat:
Upon heating a small specimen of the material on a platinum electrode, said specimen emitted smoke whilst disintegrating and was thereafter ignited, leaving a fluffy, coke-like residue which combusted under stronger heating without leaving ash. A small specimen was introduced into a glass pipe. It gradually exuded gases or vapours, which distilled into the pipe, leaving behind a small amount of oil. The oil smell is reminiscent of kerosene and bitter almonds. The condensed oil amount appears to be small in relation to the specimen.
By heating the specimen, it quickly ignites and continues to burn even when removed from the flame. If the burning specimen is blown out, it continues to react by itself by developing a thick, yellowish and grey smoke.
The test is not fusible and decays when heated, as previously mentioned.
Solubility tests:
Solubility tests have been conducted on the lightest coloured soft parts of the specimen. Alcohol, acetone, turpentine oil, toluene and alo-oxime were used. Even when boiling in these substances, the specimen showed no signs of solubility, save for in acetone, in which a very small solubility was observed.
Chemical analysis:
Elemental analysis of Roehln-Weinbrenner device and determination of nitrogen according to Dumas [I feel unsure about this sentence – Translator’s note]:

C	H	O	N
%	%	%	%
72,6	7,1	17,7	2,6

The ash content: 0,29 %.
Microscopic examination:
The chemical analysis shows that the specimen cannot be a regular nitration product of an organic feedstock. Nitrate content is all too low. For the sake of comparison it must be mentioned that the nitrogen content of TNT is 18% and for nitrocellulose approximately 13%. However, this does not eliminate the possibility that the specimen could in fact consist of a mixture of oxygen-containing organic material with a nitro compound, or an ester linkage of nitric acid.
Origin of specimen:
Regarding the origin of the specimen it is, based of the studies conducted so far, clear that it cannot be explained. The structure of the specimen indicates that it does not consist of any material prepared for specific technical purposes. Rather, it provides the impression of being a natural product or an industrial waste product, which has changed in appearance to due to wear. Whilst visiting SGU (Sveriges Geologiska Undersökning) on the 13th of July, I found it necessary to show the specimen to state geologist Thorslund. Dr. Thorslund could not possibly categorize the specimen as a fossil. He suggested that it might be some waste product, related to the Cellulose Industry.
As I proceeded to visit the Institute of Technology on the 13th of July, Associate Professor E. Öman (expert cellulose researcher and paper technology specialist) could not compare the specimen to any waste product from the Cellulose Industry. Associate Professor Öman would state a slight similarity between the specimen and to a nitration product that he recalled seeing. Hence, an attempt to resolve the specimen in highly concentrated nitric acid was immediately made, which should have succedeed had the object indeed been a nitration product. Alas, the specimen did not dissolve in the acid and was merely stained black; otherwise remaining unchanged.
Associate Professor Öman suggested that the specimen possibly originated from wasted linseed oil, but had difficulty explaining its peculiar structure.
Engineer Klang (AB Wilh Becker) and I held a discussion regarding the subject on the date of the 15th of July, 1946. We dwelled upon the possibility that the product could stem from transformed resin or linseed oil. For comparison, specimens were taken from Becker's collections. It was obvious that the specimen simply could not be recognized as any form of known resin types. Engineering Klang had no idea whether the product could derive from any known device at all.
The examination is not yet complete. So far, no results allow a positive assessment.

Specimen # 2

Recovered and simultaneously submitted with specimen # 1. The test consists of four pieces, wrapped in the same package.
Visual inspection:

2:1 	Pumice-like texture, with pores of varying size ranging from microscopic up to 3 à 4 mm. Colour yellowish-white, has a tinge of grey. Easily processed with a knife. Size approximately 20 grams.

2:2 	Specimen is porous but of different structure than 2:1. Its grey-black colour does not resemble that of specimen 2:1. Several minor segments have shown to be non-porous and significantly tough. These segments appear bright with brick-coloured influences. Its surface is embellished with fine, parallel cracks. Size approximately 10 grams.

2:3 	Specimen weighs about 5 grams. Appears completely slag-like. One segment resembles the brick-colured tone of specimen 2:2. Non-porous, adorned with fine cracks.

2:4 	Small slag-like piece, weight about 2 grams. Coloured dark purple and brick red. It is porous.
Tests show abraded surfaces and include sand grains, indicating a longer subjection to abrasion in the beach sand.
All specimens float on water.
A qualitative spectral analysis of specimen 2:1 has revealed the primary components to be magnesium, aluminum and silicon. Specimen probably contains considerably larger amounts of magnesium than aluminum. Can certainly be classified as a naturally occurring magnesium aluminum silicate.
Other specimens belonging to this group have not been subjected to spectral analysis, since they provide great resemblance to some previously examined samples, connected with group 4.

Specimen 3:1

Recovered on the ninth of July 1946.
Added to FOA 1 on twelth of July 1946.
The specimen was enclosed in a glass tube.
The specimen consists of 3 pieces, each weighing about 1 gram.
This specimen was examined by Dr. Bäcklund at the Swedish Cellulose Company's Central Laboratory in Sundsvall, who therefrom prepared a piece of a "grid".
Visual inspection:
The material is very friable, easily penetrated with a needle. It is white to greyish-white in colour. The specimen includes linear and branched fibers, white and greyish-white crystals, black particles and small brown scales. The material shows a layered character and its original layer thickness was probably about 6 mm. Additionally, it has internal cavities whose walls are covered with black paste.
Solubility:
The specimen appeared soluble in acetone at 90.5%, insoluble in acetone to 9.2%. Ash content 1.3%. Soluble in acetone, it has a melting point of 122 degrees Fahrenheit. Saponification value 188, neutralization number 198. In saponification with alkoh. kali [unsure about this one – translator’s note] a clear solution was extracted.  No unsaponifiable contents would show to be present in the specimen. Detailed microscopic examination of the material shows a granular structure of the main fat component. When I visited Dr. Thorslund at the SGU, he wasn't able to provide any explanation regarding  the origin of the material.
As I visited the State Forest Research Institute on the 2nd of August 1946, assistant Hequist and I 
discussed the origin of this specimen. It was not out of the question, that the specimen could be explained to consist of remnants of small crustaceans, which have undergone a process of decomposition, primarily leaving behind the aforementioned fat substances. The separated fat particles would serve to explain the peculiar structure of the specimen.
While the specimen appears to contain mainly organic substances (probably fat acids), it is unlikely that this finding derives from a space projectile.

Visual inspection:
This "grid" is composed of an irregularly shaped sheet, which is about 10 mm2. The surface is characterized by transparent squares of slightly varying sizes. These squares are oriented after two orthogonal coordinates. The larger squares are surrounded by black squares of approximately the same size, while the less, transparent squares are surrounded by a black background. The "grid" appears to consist of pure transparent material.
Since the specimen could be defined as a reproduction of technical material, judging by the apperance of the "grid", a visit was made to Mr. Möller Esseltes reproduction department through the intermediary of Engineer Stuube Esselte. After having studied the specimen under microscope, Mr. Möller determined that the same consists of a piece of photographic film, probably collodion, prepared in half-tone negative with a "grid"; the coarseness of which serves to remind us of conventional equipment linked to the newspapers industry. Mr. Möller submitted samples of 
such membranes (ordinary photographic film and collodion film) for comparison. Both of these bear a striking resemblance to the specimen.
It is highly unlikely that this piece of photographic film was in fact found at the same site as specimen 3:1, even though Dr. Bäcklund processed the film from the specimen in question. Aye, the mere thought would result in a logical fallacy of gigantic proportions. Rather, it seems highly probable that the piece of film was included in specimen 3:1 after the discovery was made. It is easy to pin the root cause as human error and the culprit was undeniably associated to the Sundsvall newspaper editorial, as it was Editor-in-chief A. Morelius who came to discover 3:1 and subsequently forward the specimen to Dr. Bäcklund. Dry fragments of dustlike photographic film could naturally be anticipated to exist in the surroundings of any editorial staff.

There weren't any significant breaches in the ground.

Specimen # 8

Aquired on [date is missing - Translator's note].
Added to FOA 1 on the date of July 16th, 1946.
Accompanied by the following letter from Anti-aircraft warfare Captain Westlin:
	"In addition to previous reports, a newly acquired notification shows the the discovery of a slag-like mass in the city of Stöde. In the middle of May, the owner of Rasåsen No. 1 made the discovery on a cropland, around which the grass was burnt at one meter radius. The place of finding was located in the middle of an open field, 100 meters from the road. This glowing mass had probably been hurled through the air. Slag-like stains were found in several places in the vicinity. Portions of this mass were used separately by post."
The test consists of several pieces of grey mass, which were found to enclose wilting grass blades. These grass blades are tightly packed in the pulp. When splitting the mass open, the interior proved to be darker grey. The mass is non-porous but has a dense structure. It is brittle and can easily break. The mass is soluble in hydrochloric acid, with vigorous evolution of gas (carbon dioxide).
	The mass likely consists of lime-nitrogen, which for a long time ago came to the place of finding in the form of cohesive powder cake. It is known that if lime-nitrogen is not spread out, it destroys the vegetation in its vicinity. The observed blades of grass, which adhered to the sample, provide no evidence of incineration, but seems to have become wilted. It is therefore probable that the grass on the reportedly observed burned area was destroyed by the impact of lime-nitrogen.

	"At 10 am Thursday, July 18, stone worker Hugo Rydén, a resident of Löre, Glanshammar, reported by telephone that a couple of slag pieces had struck down on his farm during the night. The pieces were apparently hot, since the grass had been burnt. Immediately after receiving notification, I went to the finding place, where two smaller slag-like pieces were found in close proximity to each other. Grass had been burnt from an area of approximately 169 centimeters and several smaller slag pieces and grime were found there. Four wooden sticks, almost resembling 
matchsticks, only missing match heads, were found approximately 10 centimeters from the burnt area. All items were recovered."

The package contained the following note:
"Content was encountered on the date of 18/7 1946 in Löre, Glanshammar. It was acquired by constable K.G. Jernwall, Glanshammar."
The sample consisted of two irregularly shaped pieces of about 20 grams of weight and also a large number of smaller particles of approximately [these numbers are indecipherable - Translator's note] grams of weight. The larger pieces consisted of a brown-black [indecipherable - Translator's note]. Some parts of these pieces has a molten layer of a more metallic character.
	When processed with a file, the matrix appeared to be brittle and gave a grey-black non-metallic shiny surface. The molten material was tougher and gave a silver-like surface. The matrix has proved to be non-magnetic, but the molten metallic layers are, in truth, magnetic.
	When stored under water, the matrix showed no tendency to rust, whereas the molten material rusted quickly.

Chemical analysis:
	Qualitative analysis of the matrix shows an abundance of iron, alumina, and silica.
Spectral analytical survey:
	The qualitative spectrum analysis showed the main ingredients to be iron, aluminum and silicon.
	It is clear that the sample has been heated to a very high temperature (above the melting point of iron).
	It has since become apparent that the sample derives from a so-called moxbrikett [unsure about this word - Translator's note]. These consist of mixtures of iron oxide powder and aluminum powder and are used for the welding of iron parts. It is possible that the iron powder has been highly siliceous, whereby the reaction temperature is raised further.

Specimen # 14

Acquired on the date of 18/7 1946.
Added to FOA 1 on the date of 25/7 1946.
Accompained by the following letter:
	The State fiscal office in Sigtuna announced on the date of 18/7, that a "bombshell" had occurred in the neighbourhood of Brantshammar (12.5 km North of Sigtuna) on the date of 9/7. In view of this event, staff members of InfSS (Infantry Battle School) were sent to the site to conduct a survey.
	Upon arrival, the following information was noted.
a) 	Observer at the time in question was Mr. Karl Lundin, Brantshammar.
b) 	The location of the sighting was a field at the Southern end of the Army installation locations.
c) 	Time: 9/7 1946, at 14:45 o' clock. Mr. Lundin had not previously announced his observation, as he did not expect to be believed. He had found two fragments that he thought could originate from "the bomb" and would subsequently report this to the local sheriff after nine days had passed since the discovery was made. A third piece was discovered by InfSS personnel during their visit to the site.
d) 	The bearing was reported to be 4900 (seen from the place of the "bombshell" - 75 meters North-east of the abovementioned observation location) [since I am ignorant, this sentence might be factually incorrect - Translator's note].
e) 	The directional slope to the horizontal plane seems to have been around 15 à 20 degrees. No signs of curvature or zig-zag movement was observed. The phenomenon was continuous and did not appear intermittently.
f) 	Sound Effects: A hissing during movement and a somewhat stronger hissing at the "burst".
g) 	Lighting effects: An orb (approximately the size of a croquet ball), glowing in the colour pattern of a rainbow with a 2.5 - 3 meters shining tail.
h) 	The "burst" seemed to take place approximately 1.25 meters above the ground.
i) 	Ranging from the initial discovery to the "burst", length of time was 1 - 1.5 seconds. At the "burst", a shower of sparks in the aforementioned colours was seen.
j) 	Expect for the acquired parts, nothing of interest was found.

Since observations were made on the site (movement direction), coupled with the fact that the acquired remains do not belong in this area (Mr. Lundin has never been able to find this kind of material in the fields around Brantshammar) and that observations of many such phenomena were made about the same time in various diverse places in the country, it is easy to assume that it's all about meteorite impacts. The found details are separately sent in for examination.

Rosersberg, on the 15/7 1946.
				S. Ramström / B. Ahlström
	The sample consisted of three fist-sized pieces, one of which is heavier and probably composed mainly of iron. The remaining two are of slag-like appearance. By breaking the former, it was found to have pore characteristics. It has probably been at the place of its finding for a long time. Several centimeters of the pores had been penetrated by rust.
	The slag-like pieces had a similar appearance to additionally acquired slag-like material. They strongly resemble specimen 4:2.
	It is therefore likely that this sample does not derive from any space projectile.

Specimen # 15

Acquired on 9/7 1946.
Added to FOA 1 on 25/7 1946.
Not accompained by a letter.
The packaging bears the following message:
		"Contains:      A number of coke-like pieces, which were collected after a meteor-like projectile fell in the community of Lunde, Gudmundrå on date of July 9, 1946.
	Sent by the sheriff of Gudmundrå district, Kramfors".
The test consists, as the note on the package implies, of coke pieces of varying size. The pieces show clear signs of having remained on the ground for a longer time. Rootlets had grown into a piece of more slag-like appearance. When breaking a piece of coke, shells of insect eggs or pupae became visible.
