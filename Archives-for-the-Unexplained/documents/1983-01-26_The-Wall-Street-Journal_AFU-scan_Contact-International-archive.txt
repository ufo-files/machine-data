    Some newspaper readers might have
 trouble swa11owing stories headlined "Giant
 Humming UFO Smashes Windows and
 Scorches Earth" or "Simple 10-Point Plan
 Can End Agony of Arthritis." But more than
 five million people a week plunk down 65
 cents to buy the National Enquirer, which
 ran those two stories last June. Do the buy-
 ers believe what they read?
    An independent research company hired
 by the Enquirer's advertising department
 recently questioned 500 of the paper's read-
 ers on the credibility of 10 articles, including
 the UFO and arthritis pieces. On a scale of
one to five, with five being "excellent," the
 readers gave the Enquirer a credibility rat-
ing of 3.6.
    In a similar survey for the Enquirer, con-
ducted by another pollster, the paper
achieved a credibility. rating of only 1.3
 among advertising agency executives and
 1.8 among executives of other companies.
One respondent said, "It has no credibility
at all." Another added, "By association,
people might not believe our ads." Despite
its huge circulation, big-name advertisers
 have long shunned the paper.
    To the Enquirer, the surveys show that
Madison Avenue Is out of touch with Main
Street. In its promotional literature, the tab-
loid admonishes potential advertisers: "You
"can't judge readers of the National Enquirer
by asking non-readers their opinions."
