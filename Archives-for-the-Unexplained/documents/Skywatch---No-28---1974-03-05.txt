     On March 7th, 1973, an astronomer named Lubos Kohoutek,
working at the Hamburg Observatory, discovered a faint fuzzy
point of light on two photographic plates taken of a part of
the sky which he had been searching by telescope for minor
planets and asteroids. A few days later Kohoutek exposed
more plates showing that part of the sky, and then he knew
from its movement and appearance that this point of light
was a new comet.

     Soon astronomers in Tokyo, Harvard, and England, con­
firmed his finding and the world was agog at the news of the
discovery of Comet Kohoutek. Suddenly, certain religious
groups were claiming that the appearance of this comet he­
ralded the end of the world, and a "repent now" theme of fear
was spread amongst the gullible. So, once again, as it has
been throughout the ages, the fear and superstition surround­
ing the appearance of a comet has come to the fore.

     The poet Homer reflected the general view of comets when
in the Iliad he compares the helmet of the hero Achilles to
"the red star that from his flaming hair shakes down diseases,
pestilence and war. "

     The Australian Aborigine believed that a comet was the
firestick of some mighty shaman ( magician or priest ) , or per­
haps a powerful sky spirit on its way to harm Earth. Ha­
waiian mythology sees the comet as a powerful god. Certain
South American Indian tribes called a comet "the spirit of
the stars" and believed that it was a warning of disaster.
Ancient Egyptians associated comets with the storm- god Set,
the evil deity who was a forerunner of Satan. Aztec priests
predicted the downfall of the Aztec empire after the arrival
of a comet in 1517 and this proved correct with the landing
of Cartes and his conquistadors.

     Comet Kohoutek was proclaimed with a fanfare, but interest
in the event fizzled out somewhat once it was realized that
we would not be treated to a startling display of fiery lights.
                             ·- '13 -

It now remains to be seen whether it has been the forerunner
of disaster ( although, with disaster being so widespread at
the moment, one would always be able to �prdve• as much as
•disprove• the theory). But it seems in the light of other
predictions made, that Earth will still be around to witness
the arrival of Halley•s comet in 1986 - and here too, predic­
tions abound that gloom will surround this event.

     What is a comet? Briefly, it consists of three parts,
the nucleus, the coma and the tail. The nucleus is a fairly
dense, compacted area within the comet, but what it is made of
still remains unsolved. There may be a core of� hard· chunk
of matter like an asteroid, but some comets split up as though
they have no solid core at all. It is thought that the na­
ture of the particles of the nucleus are predominantly ice.

      Around the nucleus is the fuzzy luminous cloud called the
coma ( meaning long- haired ) ! This is composed of a gassy�·
cloud of very minute particles, immensely widely dispersed.
Some of these are dust specks, but some are far finer; they
are in fact molecules of many different substances in gaseous
fprm.

     When a comet is far away from the sun, it possesses no
tail and looks like a faint fuzzy smudge. However, as it
begins to come close in towards the sun, the ice and other
materials begin to melt and give off gasses which form the
coma which surrounds the nucleus. Pressure of radiation
from the sun drives the tiny particles from the comet out into
space, giving rise to the tail which, because of this pressure,
always points away from the sun.
