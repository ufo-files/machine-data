                                                                                          on mystery of the-North
        By AGNES KRUCHIO
   In reaching for the stars, man has
been to the moon, sent probes to the
planets and searched for alien life,
but an age-old space mystery close to
home still defies his full comprehen-
sion.
   The aurora borealis, or northern
lights, have been steeped in mystery
since the time the Roman Senator
Seneca wrote about the "heavens
which seemed to open and vomit
flames." They have evoked terror,
awe and inspired a myriad of mythol-
ogies, but only recently have scien-
tists begun to piece together the
immense puzzle of their origin.
   Now, the most mysterious of these
eerie lights may begin to yield some
of its secrets. During a two-month
project, a large group of Canadian
and Japanese space scientists are
probing the type of polar light known
as the pulsating aurora.
   Pulsating auroras, although not
very bright, appear fantastic even to
space scientists hardened to mys-
teries. They appear and disappear
with eerie regularity, turning on and
off at five-to-ten-second intervals,
sometimes for minutes or·hours at a
stretch.
   They occur at latitudes lower than
the bright auroras, and appear only
after midnight. Unlike the swiftly
undulating bright auroras, they al-
ways reappear in the same shape,
sometimes drifting slowly across the
sky as they switch themselves on and
off. There may be several of them in
the sky at the same time, and, blink-
ing on and off at varying frequencies
in different locations in the sky, they
treate what one scientist calls the
"Christmas-tree effect."
   In the process of learning more
about this. aurora, s.cientists are try-
ing to get answers to some puzzling
aspects of space near the Earth and
the Earth's complex relationship
with the sun .
