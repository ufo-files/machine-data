INVESTIGATION TIPS
THE SIGHTING REPORT
a. Taking the Report
Procedure
When taking the report, the investigator must:
Contact the witness by email or phone. Noting in the report
the time and date of call or email. .
Accept and handle anonymous reports in the same manner
as other reports, gather the same information from the
anonymous witness as from any other witness, and
encourage the witness to provide identifying information.
Always try to obtain a face-to-face meeting.

Methods Investigators Can Use When Interviewing A
Witness.
For all of these techniques, the investigator should consider
the use of voice, including pitch, tone and pace.

Questioning is the primary technique for leading the witness
through the information-giving process. Three types of
questions are helpful:

Open-ended questions can be used for the purpose of
stimulating the witness to talk. An example is “Can you
please describe what you saw, step by step?” The witness

most likely will expand on answers and give the investigator
the opportunity to probe into the subject under discussion.

Closed-ended questions restrict the witness response and
may be useful to get a specific answer when the screener
does not wish to stimulate further discussion. For example,
questions such as, “Did you see the object clearly?” will most
likely yield a “yes” or “no” response.

Probing questions should be used when a problem needs
clarification at progressively deeper levels. An example of a
probing question is, “You just said that you saw the object.
Tell me, what color was it?” Simple directive probes such as
“uh huh” and “please go on” are useful as encouragers, as
are requests for specific information such as, “What was the
objects shape?”

b. The Report and Witness

Repetition or rephrasing of what a witness has said will
help the investigator be sure the witness’s point is
understood.

Directness is used when the witness doesn’t know what
information is needed or is too emotional to know how to
proceed. Give directions, telling the witness what information
is needed, without being authoritarian or bureaucratic. For
instance: “We can best help you if we know some specific
information. I will ask you a series of questions. Please
answer them as best you can.”

Redirection is used to interrupt if the information being
given is unproductive or not relevant to the purpose of the
report. For instance: “Now I need you to give me some
specific information that will help us to follow through. Please

listen closely to my questions and give me clear, short
answers to each one.”

Supportiveness is used to encourage the witness to
continue to give the information needed, to support whatever
role they play in the sighting in addition to making the report,
and to enhance the witness’s view of your agency and
system. For instance: “Thank you for your concern and for
making this report.”

Summarization is used to briefly go over the important
information that has been gathered to see if the witness has
provided everything that is critical. Often, summarization will
be combined with a final probe question. For instance: “Let’s
see, you have given me information about your sighting,
you’ve told me the color, size and shape of the object, it’s
path of flight and it’s disappearing over that hill. Is there
anything else that has you concerned about the sighting?”

Dealing with Abusive Witness’s is sometimes necessary.
It is important for the investigator to stay calm and be clear
that the conversation will not continue if the witness is
abusive. For instance: “We will be better able to help if you
give me the information without yelling and cursing. If you
continue to use this language (tone of voice), I will end this
conversation.”

Regarding what will happen next. It is important to let
witness know the next steps, but not to give them
information that is unknown.

Conclusion
The investigator can conclude contact with the witness when
the following has been accomplished: The necessary
information has been obtained.
