                "ESSENTIALS     OF   ASTRONOMY"
                                                  Gregory Roberts.


          The planetoids, astenoids, or minor planets occupy a broad
belt between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter.    These were unknown
in early times and it has only been since the advent of the tele­
scope and more especially the camera that these objects have been
tracked.  Since the first discovery in January 1801 of the
a teroid Ceres by    Piazzi some 2, 000 have been located.    The
main difficulty nowadays is not in trying to find more but to keep
track of the ones discovered so that they will not be confused with
new discoveries.     The largest Ceres, has a diameter of 43 7 miles
followed by Pallas at 287 miles. Thereafter the size drops rapidly
until one ends with mere lumps of rock.


          Very few asteroids appear to be circular or spherical in
shape, one actually suspected of being cylindrical.    Their are not
confined to a particular kind, but range to different parts of the
Solar System.   Hidalgo goes beyond the orbit of Saturn, whilst
Hermes actually can go between the moon and earth and thus only
miss by a mere 1/4 millions miles.       Icarus, a typical asteroid
makes a close pass of 4 million miles from earth in June 1968.


          No one knows where these asteroids originated, the most
likely explanation being that it is the remnant of a planet that
burst asunder ages gone by.      Even if this was the case, the
original planet could not have been more than a few hundred miles
across.


          Of all the heavenly bodies visib_le to the naked eye, comets
are perhaps the most spectacular.     Although bright comets are a
not too frequent occurance, most people can see a comet or two
during their life time.  With a telescope it is possible to see
several each year.


          Comets move in a variety of orbits some moving in
elliptical orbits about the sun and hence reappearing every few
years, whilst others plunge into the Solar system from outer space,
speed past the sun and out again into space never to be seen again.
These invariably are the more spectacular ones.       Halleys comet
with a period of 75 years was last seen in 1910.      Few people see
this comet twice and remember it.


                                                  / The . ..........
          The nature of comets is not very well known at present.
Astronomers suspect them to be cold, mostly gaseous bodies with
the tail formed as a result of some reaction with the sun light.
Generally a comet appears as a large fuzzy object with a long
tail.  Their size can be remarkable - the smallest being about
10, 000 miles in diameter whilst the largest was that of 1811
with a diameter of 1, 225,000 miles.     The tails are even more
tremendous,   the longest being that of the comet in 1843 which was
200, 000, 000 miles long.


          American scientists plan to try and land a spacecraft on
a comet and use the comet to carry the satellite out into space.
This may be attempted with Halleys comet or even perhaps with
one of the rare visitors to our Solar system.


          The objects commonly known as meteors,     shooting stars,
fireballs or solides are familiar to everyone.     When they actually
impact the earth they become known as meteorites or aerolites.
Some 10, 000 enter earths atmosphere every day, though the naked
eye sees approximately 10 per hour, with the greater number
occuring after midnight.    A meteor is a lump of rock that floats
around in space.     When it comes under the gravitational pull of
the earth it speeds earthwards as it passes through the thin layers
of the upper atmosphere, the friction causes it to glow and invariably.
burn up before reaching the earth.  Burn up usually occurs at 10
to 60 miles up when the objects are moving at 5 - 6 miles per
second.


          Occasion:!lly the earth in its journey around the sun inter­
sects a stream of meteors in space, some of which follow a
definite path, and we have a meteor shower.      In rare cases up to
400 or so meteors per hour have been observed.       The best shower
of all is the Perseids but this is not well placed for southern
hemisphere observers, who have to tolerate the next best which is
the Geminids in early December.        The rate here is about 40- 50
per hour and they are very bright.


         The size of the meteors range from small specks to
giants - the largest recovered being that that fell in the Adrar
district of French West Africa which weighs about a million tons.
The average meteor however is the size of a pea.        We are
fortunate in having a protective mantle of air around the earth as
the greater majority of meteors burn up before hitting ground.

         Dear Members all, your Committee has a problem on its
hands which we feel you are in a position to enable us to s.olve
happily.   It concerns· the matter of the monthly meetings in
Durban.

         From the beginning of the year the attendance has been
increasing steadily, which we regard as an encouraging sign that
we are doing the right thing and giving you the kind of talks which
you want.   Lately, however, we have heard that some of our members
have not been very happy about the amount of U. F. 0. subject matter
brought up at our meetings.

          While we appreciate this point of view very well, we are
trying to vary the programme as much as we can, ( and after all,
is not variety the spice of life?) though the topic of U. F. 0' s is a
vast one embracing many fields, both physical and metaphysical.
The job is not as easy as it sounds, for it means finding speakers
who are both willing and able to speak with some authority, or from
personal experienGe, on one or more facets of Ufology.       We should
very much like to arrange a meeting where the speaker can say,
in effect, "I have seen and spoken to the Space people and here I am
to tell you all about it".  Where can one contact such a person?

         The facts to date are these.   We have put our problems
to you on a previous occasion, suggesting that meetings should be
held bi-monthly or even quarterly, due to the apparent lack of
suitable speakers.    The concensus of opinion was that we should
continue to meet once a month willy-nilly.    We then asked for
suggestions on what we should do or discuss at our get-togethers.

guided by the views of all you members.     So why don't you write
and tell us what you think?   We would very much like to publish
