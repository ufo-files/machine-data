                             REPORT on WHITE POWDER ANALYSIS


                                     G.   Hathaway,   P.Eng.

                                          July 4,   1989



    Introduction

        On June 20, 1989 the author received a sample of unidentified
   white material from Mr.     Harry Tokarz of Toronto for general
   chemical analysis. About 5 cc of an odourless part white powder,
   part  whitish  clumped   substance was    provided  enclosed  in  a
   conventional matchbox wrapped in a small decorated paper envelope.
   It did not appear to have been mixed and therefore was likely quite
   inhomogeneous. The substance was said to have been collected from
   a larger deposit of the same material located either in the sea
   water or on the shore at a location in Israel. Apparently the
   substance had been found in an area where a bright fiery object had
   fallen to the ground.


   Analysis Methodology and Results

             A.   Radioactivity

        Using a scintillometer as well as a Geiger counter, it was
   found that the sample was no more radioactive than background.



             B.   X-Ray Fluorescence

        On June 23 at the author's laboratory,       about   5 cc was
                                                                  .



   enclosed in a taped polyethylene bag and enclosed with an Americium
   241 gamma source for an initial qualitative X-Ray Fluorescence
   Analysis. The sample and source were not evacuated. The results of
   this analysis showed a quantity of Chlorine, lesser amounts of
   Calcium and trace amounts of Iron, Titanium, Copper and Nickel.
   Because the sample was not evacuated and for other reasons,
   elements below Chlorine, eg. Sulfur, Silicon, Aluminum, Magnesium,
   Sodium etc. were not able to be detected by this method.
        Due  to an apparent measurement      artifact,  several other
   elements were identified, including radioactive elements, but which
   were highly improbable. These spurious readings may be the result
   of the tape used and are currently being analysed again to
   determine their cause.
a.FCFlN 811IIETIN   ,   MAY-.ll'E,   1989                               6




         c.   Scanning Electron Microscope and Analyser

        On June 22, 1989 the author analysed about . 25 cc of the
   ?ubstance using an Hitachi Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) with
   an Energy Dispersive X-Ray (EDX) analyser attached, located at the
   University of Toronto. The sample was carbon coated for the
   analysis but the attached photo was taken of uncoated sample. This
   photo shows an area about 90 x 110 microns and unfortunately is not
   of high quality. It shows an amorphous powder with a circular
   object of the same material about 15 microns in diameter at the
   lower right. Subsequent images not photographed showed a few cubic
   crystals interspersed in the powder.
        The attached SEM EDX analysis represents an average analysis
   over approx. a 1 mm square area. It shows high amounts of Magnesium
   (Mg) and Chlorine (Cl) with a trace of Sulfur (S) and perhaps
   Potassium (K) and Calcium (Ca). This scan is up to 6.0 keV but no
   other elements were detected up to 30 keV. Elements lighter than
   Magnesium (eg. Sodium, Oxygen, Carbon etc.) could not be detected
   due to the wetness of the sample making the required high vacuum
   difficult to achieve.

        Quantitative analysis on the XRF and EDX systems were           not
   performed due to the time and cost involved to set these up.


         D. Neutron Activation Analysis

        On June 27, 1989 approx. 1 cc of material was analysed at the
   University of Toronto's Slowpoke Reactor using Instrumental Neutron
   Activation Analysis (INA.A). Due to the cost of the analysis, only
   the short-lived isotopes were analysed for. The sample was not
   dried but by this time it was in a sealed poly sample bag. The
   result of this analysis is shown below (recall 10,000 ppm ls 1%):

           In the above analysis, +/- means plus/minus error bound and
    <=   means less than or equal to.




         The quantity of Magnesium is consistent with either or both
    of Magnesium Oxide (the mineral Periclase - MgO) or Magnesium
    Peroxide (Mg02). In MgO the Magnesium is about 60% and in Mg02 it
    is about 43%. No analysis was possible for oxygen which,         in
    general,  requires   other  chemical  analytical   procedures.  The
    relative quantities of Sodium and Chlorine are close to that found
    in Sodium Chloride.
         The sum of all of these elements is a little over 55% with the
    remainder (ie. almost 45%) most likely oxygen.


            E. Solubility

            The sample is not.soluble in room temp (21 deg.                C) tap water.


    Discussion

         The analysis above is consistent with the burning of Magnesium
    in air. Depending on atmospheric conditions, either MgO or Mg02 can
    be formed, or both. Both compounds are �ffectively insoluble in
    water but the peroxide is a white .powder at STP          (Standard
    Temperature and Pressure) while the oxide consists of colourless
    cubic crystals. According to visual observation through the SEM,
    the vast bulk of the material, probably greater than 99%, is an
    amorphous white powder consistent with the peroxide.
         The Sodium and Chlorine may be from the seawater and possibly
    from the sweat from handling by human hands.
         The trace elements also found could come from the surrounding
    dirt or beach sand (the sample was by no means pure white) . They
    could also have come from the chemicals used to make the striker
    on the side of the matchbox. Most of this would be Phosphorous with
    Sulfur. Although the analytical procedures above were capable of
    detecting Phosphorous, only Sulfur shows up in trace amounts,
    consistent with its originating on the matchbox.
