     No reliable book has been written -- to this reviewer's knowledge -- by the witness him­
self about a single UFO incident with nary a mention of other UFO cases. Abductee Travis Wal­
ton has changed all that with The Walton Experience - - a significant, rather riveting achieve­
ment. Even more r·emarkable, indications are that Walton -- now only in his mid-20s -- had no
writing experience before authoring this slim but impressive volume.
     His incredible experience with six other woodcutters in the Apache-Sitgreaves National­
Forest of Arizona on November 5, 1975, and his subsequent five days aboard a UFO, has becomt
a classic contact/abduction case, but, as Walton himself says, "Not one, not a single one, of
the written accounts of my experience was entirely correct.11 Hence, this book.
     With the expertise of a journalistic professional, Walton takes us through the sighting
encounter that reduced 11Six hardened woodsmen11 to "mindless terror"; the suspicions of police
that Travis' eo-workers were "covering up for a bloody chainsaw murder"; the unprofessional
antics of William Spaulding, Ground Saucer Watch, and a certain bogus "Dr." Lester Steward,
as well as the unfounded assertions of Philip Klass (unnamed in the book); and his remem­
brances aboard the UFO with its occupants.
     The abductee has high praise for APRO's handling of his case. He even admits a brief
fling with drugs and a run-in with the law for "writing bad checks," but all that is behind
him. Walton is, after all -- unlike his abductors -- human. It's about time that a witness
in such a case not have to prove the impossible -- that he is "perfect11 -- before his claim
is accepted as possible evidence of extraterrestrial contact, particularly when all the other
evidence, including hypnosis and polygraph tests, substantiates his bizarre encounter.
