   Kam:
        Sure, and if they are born, we might say that they've
   gained something. But, if I get a parking ticket I've
   gained something. Consideration of loss and gain does
   not add up to moral consideration. What's important is
   whether or not the lost or gained thing is good.
   Sure, life is the prerequisite for good, happiness, and
   pleasure. It is also the prerequisite for evil,
   unhappiness, and displeasure. So, is life itself good
   or evil, pleasurable or painful? No, it is only things
   *in life* that are pleasurable or painful, good or evil.
        When one says that life is intrinsically valuable,
   one often means that the living deserve special treatment
   that the non-living don't deserve. This could be, as
   J.S. Mill argues, because the ability to suffer makes
   on a moral patient (but, then moral patienthood wouldn't
   extend over *all* life). It could be because (all) living
   things have interests, in a sense. Whatever the reason
   is, it isn't that life is itself the valuable thing,
   in the sense that happiness or well being is what's
   valuable. We might say, "maximize happiness," or,
   "maximize pleasure," but we need never say "maximize
   life." Life simply isn't valuable *in that sense.*
