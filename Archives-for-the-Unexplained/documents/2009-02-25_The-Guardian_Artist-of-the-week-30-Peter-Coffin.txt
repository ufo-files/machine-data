Artist of the week 30: Peter Coffin
This article is more than 15 years old
Jessica Lack continues her series on major contemporary artists with a sighting of Peter Coffin whose eclectic work is inspired by
UFOs, mythology, mysticism and Jung
Jessica Lack
Wed 25 Feb 2009 10.36 GMT
Share
Last July, the inhabitants of Gdansk were visited by a UFO; it spun across the sky over the Baltic Seabefore disappearing into the
dockyards where it came to rest like some glorious Spielbergian welcome wagon in the city's industrial heartland. Onlookers
documented the event by photographing the flying saucer as it circled the Gulf of Gdansk on their mobile phones and digital cameras.
But the resulting images were as grainy and blurred as those suspicious smears and black marks on photographs of UFO sightings
since the early 1900s.
The project, conceived by the conceptual artist Peter Coffin, was inspired by Carl Jung's theory that UFO sightings increase during
times of extreme social unrest. Coffin's interest in such phenomena is born out of his fascination for and scepticism over Jung's idea
that the UFO becomes a uniting symbol in the collective consciousness in these times.
The New York-based artist was born in 1972 and grew up in Berkeley, California, the centre of the hippy movement and a place of
radical left-wing politics during the McCarthy era. The city embraced New Age ideas and practices, particularly the writings of the
influential thinker and founder of analytical psychology, Jung. Coffin chooses to work with beliefs and practices he claims not to
understand, creating conceptual artwork inspired by dreams and different mythologies, religions and philosophies.
From pasting postcards of rainbows into a psychedelic spiral to making trousers for trees, Coffin creates witty artworks in response
to our knowledge and interpretation of the world.
However, it's his ongoing series, Aura Portraits, that perhaps best encapsulates his practice. Playing on telepathy and the idea that a
photograph can steal your soul, the artist used a specially adapted camera to photograph his subjects. The resulting images emerged
as bleached white ovals surrounded by a watery haze of colour, as ghostly as phosphorescence and as intangible as the subjects
Coffin sets out to uncover.
Why we like him? For Untitled (Greenhouse), 2002. Coffin invited musicians, DJs and sound artists to play in a greenhouse in
order to investigate the beneficial effects of music on plants. The resulting CD, Music for Plants, features 40 artists including Ara
Peterson, Christian Marclay, Dearraindrop and Mice Parade
