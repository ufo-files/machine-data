                          ESSENTIALS OF ASTROLOGY

                                                 Gregory Roberts.

               This article, the last in this series, we shall discuss
          star clusters. Star Clusters are assemblages of stars whose
          members are less widely scattered than the stars in the fields
          around the clusters. They are not temporary congestions in
          the celestial traffic. The stars of each group are moving
          together so that the clusters will retain their identity for
          a very long time.

               Star clusters can be divided into two classes; open
          clusters and globular clusters. As the name open clusters
          suggests, these are clusters which are not highly concentrated
          toward their centres. Some typical examples are the Pleiades
          (or Seven Sisters) the Hyades in Taurus, the double cluster
          in Perseus, the Praesepe cluster in Cancer and the cluster of
          Coma Berenices. These are all naked eye, the remainder being
          telescopic objectse  Over 400 open clusters are known.

               Astronomers have found that the open clusters seem to
          predominate in the plane of the Milky Way. The number of stars
          in a cluster range from a few to several thousand and giant
          stars are scarce in most and entirely absent in some clusters.
          On account of this and the heavy dust clouds in the directions
          of the Milky Way the present visibility of open clusters is
          limited to those within about a distance of 20,000 light years.

               Several star clusters are so near to us that the stars can
          be seen moving relative to one another. This requires some
          extremely small measurements on photographic plates as the
          movement is very small as seen from the earth. One good example
          is the Ursa Major group which has over 40 members. As the
          common motion suggests a common origin it has been pos8 ible

               Globular clusters are more compact and are spherical in
          form. They are much larger, more populous and more distant
          than the known open clusters.

               A hundred globular clusters are recognised in the Milky
          Way and can usually be distinguished from open clusters by
          their more regular form and their greater central concentra­
