                   COMMUNICATIONS
                               Charles Belensky   -   W9RVX




      One of the chief objectives of any contact group is to actually establish some
form of communication with our visitors from outer space.      There are many ways of
doing this, and I will try to describe the methods used by our group.     I myself, am
an amateur radio operator, and I would suggest to anyone starting a contact group
to try to get some radio operator, (HAM), who is interested in saucers.      Have him
write to A. David Middleton, WSCA, in Tijeras, New Mexico to secure addition­
al information regarding amateur radio nets etc.

   Our group has attempted contact by two methods. One is by radio-telegraph
"CW", the other, using light beam communications. We have not made too many
attempts as yet, but we will keep trying on a regular schedule.         One evening a
week at each method seemed to us sufficient.       Any group would have to have a
licensed "HAM" operator to attempt communications via radio, and even then there
are many restrictions.

      For more information on radio contacts I would suggest reading "The Saucers
Speak", a documentary report of interstellar communications by George H. William­
son and Alfred C. Bailey.      This book tells of actual radio-telegraph communications
by them and others with saucers in August of 1952. It is very interesting reading.
According to the book, the frequency which our "friends" in the saucers used to
send on was usually around 400 Kcs.       In one place they mentioned 40 meters as a
frequency for us to send on.    As far as I know, there is no certain frequency on
which to send and be assured of a reply.

     This talk of radio communications may discourage some, but that leads me
into our second method of communication, light beams. Light beams have many
distinct advantages over radio. You don't need any license whatsoever to operate
the equipment. This should be a relief to anyone interested in this work that doesn't
hold an FCC license. Another advantage is that all communications are strictly
two-way, whereas with radio, anyone having a short-wave receiver is able to mon­
itor your transmtsstons.   The unit is comparatively simple, therefore the price is not
prohibitive.   Depending on the type of unit built, including transmitter and recetver
with all parts new, I would say that the cost could be kept under $30.

     A light beam receiver is nothing more than a photo-electric tube (like those
THE W. C. G. RESEARCH BULLETIN                                                         9



used to automatically open doors at supermarkets etc.) connected with a two-tube
amplifier. This is all run off of batteries in order to make it completely portable.
Anyone with some electronic experience should be able to build one without too
much difficulty.

     There are quite a few circuits that could be used, as anyone doing a little re­
search on the subject will find out. Most radio and electronic magazines have had
articles on them for different purposes at various times. The quarterly publication
"Saucers" has also had a few plans.     The one I finally chose, though, was one ap­
pearing in "Popular Electronics" of June, 1955.      All items in this unit are easily
obtainable through any radio mail order house.           This unit takes advantage of
"printed circuit" components, which greatly simplifies building.   It also uses a con­
cave mirror principle which adds much to its sensitivity.   The only change I made
was to use a 923 photo-tube instead of a 930.       The 923 is sensitive also to infra­
red and ultra-violet.

     While still on the subject, I would suggest to anyone building any type of light
beam receiver to read the article in "Popular Electronics", and use their concave
mirror principle as it will multiply the unit"s sensitivity greatly. To give a short de­
scription, a concave mirror (a shaving mirror of the type that magnifies can be used)
is pointed at the sky, or the object from which the light is being emitted, and then
in turn focused on the photo-electric tube.    The principle is about the same as that
of a reflecting telescope.

     There is supposedly an accompanying penetrating beam along with infra-red,
and with this in mind, many interesting experiments could be conducted by interest­
ed individuals.

      More advantages of light beam communication are that the units are light, com­
pact, and need no antenna such as a radio transmitter would.

     The light beam transmitter is even simpler than the receiver.     All it consists of
is a portable, hand-held, sealed-beam, 6 volt spotlight, two 6 volt batteries, a 3ohm­
 l 0 watt resistor, and a carbon microphone. There are many good spot lights on
the market such as the "Radar Light" and ''Big Beam", to list a couple.         Usually
wherever you find an article on light beam receivers you will also find a description
of a transmitter. The article in "Popular Electronics" is no exception as it gives
plans for one that is even a little simpler than the one that I described. All they
do is connect two 6 volt batteries in series (or use a 12 volt battery) and then con­
nect the microphone and spotlight in series with that.

      For those of you who don't really understand how communication by light
beams would work, I will try to explain.    As you talk into the microphone, your
10                                             THEW. C. G. RESEARCH BULLETIN




voice will cause the spotlight to flicker.    These variations are then picked up on the
face of the photo-tube which is right in the path of light.     The photo-tube will then
change this light into electrical impulses.     These impulses are very weak, so they
are then fed to the two amplifier tubes and the voice reproduction circuitry.       The
end result is that you hear, through earphones, exactly what was spoken into the
microphone with no mechanical connection between the transmitter and the receiver.
It is the same principle as radio, except that your voice is carried by the light beam
instead of radio waves.

   This information should, I hope, stir some interest in this very interesting field.
As you probably have gathered, this type of communication has many possibilities.

   Record everything you hear, if you make contact, if it is humanly possible.      Re­
cordings are much better evidence than the spoken word.        Tape recorders seem to do
the job quite well.   You will probably run into some difficulties there though.   Most
tape recorders have to be run off of I I 0 volt� AC, and it can be difficult to obtain

which attaches to your car battery and ":'ill produce I I 0 volts AC (this is quite a
draw on the battery though), or 3. Obtain a small battery-operated tape or wire
recorder.   Use whichever method fits your situation.         The small battery-operated
wire recorder is ideal, but the one drawback is the price.         They average around
$300.00.

  When attempting contact it is also advisable to have along a portable radio,
and leave it tuned to one frequency.     In some cases light beam may work better
than radio and vice-versa.

  We will continue to use both radio and light in our attempts, and I will list our
progress in the next communications article in this publication.

Control will deal with the rejuvenation or reawakening of the "hid­

twice, it is certainly anything but conclusive of the fact that telep­

sive or satisfying orthodox explanation. However, if we were to
look at this incident with the possibility of telepathy in the fore­
ground, this and other equally baffling instances can easily be ac­

rushes madly about trying desperately to locate the stray item.

exhaustion. In a semi-coma the individual may perhaps meditate on
where the lost article may be, this is the process he should have
originally used. In a large percentage of the cases, the individual
will receive a mental picture of the whereabouts of the item which
is the obvious cause for his present state of immobility. After a fair­
ly acceptable recovery, the individual staggers forth to jostilly con­
firm his precognitive vision. Not really believing in the possibility
of finding the item by using such an unorthodox method but rather
to make jest of its utter failure he nears the place which was shown
in his vision and finds the article!

  The research field of Radiations is a big one, with many underly­
