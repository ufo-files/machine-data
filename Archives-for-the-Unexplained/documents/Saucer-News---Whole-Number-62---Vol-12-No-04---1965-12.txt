                                          ON OBSERVING FOR THE LAYMAN


                                                                      -by JAMES RAND!.


In order to provide more accurate and informative reports of UFO sightings,
the layman should certainly be informed of the standard system employed by
astronomers to designate the "brightness" of celestial objects.                                                                                          This short
article will attempt to provide a basis for this method of classifying objects
believed to be UFOs, and at the same time probably serve to enable the layman
to eliminate ordinary satellite sighting s as a cause for undue excitement.


First, let us understand that the astronomer refers to the "magnitude" of a
celestial object, not its ''brightness". The higher the number of the magnitude,
the dimmer the object. The faintest star visible to the average unaided eye is
of magnitude 6. The 200-inch diameter mirror of the famous Mount Palomar
telescope can only register stars of the 23rd magnitude.                                                                                      Here are some fair
examples of magnitude:

    VEGA(In the con stellation Lyra)                                                                      .   •   0   •   •   •       •               0.0

    VENUS {At maximum magnitude)                                                                          •   •   0   0   •   •       0             -4.3
    THE SUN   •   •   •   •   •   •   •   •   •   .   .   •   •   •   •   .   •       .       •   •   .   •   •   •   •   •   •       •           -26.7

NOTICE: negative magnitudes are used for objects brighter than zero, and are
designated by a minus sign(-) preceding the number.


The satellites ECHO I & II are approximately of zero magnitude.                                                                                         To estimate
the correct magnitude of any unknown object, simply compare it with some
star or planet it passes near. A little practice will lead to considerable facility
in assigning correct magnitudes to these objects.


It is a good idea to have at hand a copy of the New York Times for the night
when the observations are being made. This paper(and others) will carry, on
the "weather" page, a list of visible satellites for that date. Practise becoming
familiar with the appearance of these regular satellites, and you will avoid the
danger of identifying them to others(and particularly to the newspapers)                                                                                          as
UFOs! Besides, its an interesting hobby identifying & tracking the satellites!


Recently, in South Amboy,                                     N.J., the Evening News carried a UFO "sighting"
which turned out to be a passing of ECHO II, much to the embarassment of the
parties concerned. Lets not have that happen to a reader of SAUCER NEWS!!
