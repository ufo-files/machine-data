                                  FROM THE EDITOR
                                 by Allan       J.   Manak


     Benita "Bunny" Owens and I arrived at Alpine, Texas at 11:45 p.m.
on October 16, 1 9 93. It was difficult to find lodging because the
local college, Sul Ross University, was having a homecoming celebration.
     The next day, we drove to an area off Route 67 to check out a
parking place that would be in the middle of where most of the mysterious
Marfa lights are seen.           The is on      Route 16 9     just east of   Route 67.
      On the way back to         Alpine    I spotted a        Border   Patrol car,   so we
pulled along side his vehicle and I got out to tell him that we would
be on Route 16 9 the next night. The reason being is that I didn't want
the border patrol to think that we were someone sneaking into the
United States from Mexico.  He told me that he would inform the other
officers that we would be in the area.
       It was then that      I asked him if he had ever seen the mystery lights.
He said "Oh yes,        on several occasions."               I then asked him where would be
the best area for us to see them?  He said "right there" as he pointed
to the Mitchell Flat area.  The very same area that we had just driven
through,      Route 16 9.   I thanked him,           and we parted.
      Later in this same day we            drove to the official viewing area off
of   Route    90.    The area is located about 12             miles west of   Alpine and
approximately        9 miles east of Marfa.
      We arrived at the site about 8:30 p.m.                    The site is a semi -paved
drive,   south off the main road,           Route      90.    It has picnic tables,    trash
cans and always other vehicles.  This partic11lar night there were six
other vehicles parked when we arrived.  We were at this site for abo11t
four hours.         As always,   cars kept coming and going.              We were not lucky
this night, as we didn't see any unusual lights.
     The next night October 18th, we drove onto Route                      16 9 into the
Mitchell Flat area.  We drove about 6 to 7 miles w·hen we stopped and
turned the car around so as to face west.  About 45 minutes of viewing
the beautiful starry sky and the area around the car,                     we were shocked as
we saw this tremendous flash of light,                 as if someone had turned a huge
light on for j11st a split second. The light repeated itself a few
minutes later, and this time we heard rumbling in the distance.  Need­
less to say, we got into the car and drove out of that area back to
the official viewing area,         and sat in and out of the car for another
four hours with no luck again.
       The next day after seeing and visiting some historical areas,                        we
dined at a local resta11rant.             As soon as we were seated           I spotted two
Border Patrol officers just finishing their dinner.  I waited until
they were just about through with their last cup of coffee when I
pounced at them.
       Yes,    both officers were interested             in the mystery lights,      both
had sightings on a number of occasions,                  and both told me we were in the
proper area to view them.  That evening we spent five ho11rs listening to
the sounds of Yanni on the tape deck, and seeing nothing unusual.
     While visiting the Big Bend area, we had the occasion to visit
Judith M. Breeske, Ph.D., author of the book The Marfa Lights at her
place of business, the Ocotillo Enterprises, and much to our despair,
the good doctor was attending a seminar in Arizona.
     All in all, the Big Bend area is a very fine place to visit, even
if one does not see those pesky mystery lights, and have West Texas
lightning scare the hell out of them.

SOLAR FLARE MYTH?  "The time has come to lay the solar flare myth to
rest, " says Jack Gosling,                 a scientist at Los Alamos National                         Laboratory
in New Mexico.
        Many textbooks state that energetic outbursts from the sun's surface,
or solar flares,             cause magnetic storms such as aurora here on earth.                                        But
this view is wrong,               according to Gosling.               Although some flares occur at
the same time as geomagnetic storms, he points 011t that a one-to-one
association doesn't exist between the two sets of events.
        Instead,        says Gosling,        we should focus on coronal mass e jections,
gigantic blobs of hot gas,                  as the cause of geomagnetic disturbances.
Mass e jections do not result from flares but rather from the restructuring
of magnetic fields in the sun's outer atmosphere,  or corona.  ( Astronomy
Magazine, April 1 9 94. )
