Clouds

The common types of cloud are likely to restrict an observers vision and prevent the

observation of high altitude UFO activity.             Clouds may normally vary in height accord­

ing to the nature of such;             they may range from the low altitude nimbo-stratus rain

cloud to the cirrus at heights of up to 30,000 ft.             However   � certain cloud formations
are rather more unfamiliar and these have been enumerated below.


1.   LENTICULAR       CLOUDS    are,    as the name implies, symmetrical and circular in shape

     and are normally formed over mountains where the ascending air is formed into

     saucer shaped objects.

2.   NOCTILUCENT       CLOUDS    are of unknown composition and occur at altitudes of up to

     50 miles.     They resemble thin cirrus and are blue, silver or orange standing out

     against the night sky.            These clouds are visible when the sun is 5°-13° below

     the horizon.


3.   OPALESCENT       CLOUDS    are common cloud types which,    by a freak of light refraction

     are tinged with the colours of the spectrum.



Comets

A bright comet appears as a diffuse luminosity in the sky often associated with a

tail.    Although the true motion of comets through space may be very fast,            they are

generally so distant from earth that the apparent motion is small.               About two or

three new comets are observed by astronomers each year in addition to those which

return in their eccentrical elliptical orbits around the sun.               The nucleus.of a comet

is considered to consist of sand particles,            frozen carbon dioxide,   methane,   cyanogen,

ammonia and water.       On approaching the sun,       comet nuclei are affected by the increas­

ing radiation effects from the sun which vapourises a portion of the gases,                forming

a vapour trail.       The pressure of the solar wind,       emenating from the sun's_corona,

draws out a comets tail away from the sun,           often resulting in a tail,    whose length

fragile,   end with their high volatile content lead to fragmentation in the atmosphere.

Many of these objects,         which have drifted in from the outskirts of the Solar System

Coronae

These appear as rings of light which,      under certain atmospheric conditions,      surround

the sun or moon and are caused by the refraction of light by ice particles in cirrus

Coronae are generally white,      but often exhibit the colours of the spectrum with red

on the inside.     The phenomenon is often observed around the moon on cold nights and

when it is veiled by thin cloud.

field produces this phenomenon which can appear as a hovering ball of light.
