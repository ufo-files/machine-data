        Some Further Considerations Re: the "Venus" sightings of June 8/f30
                               by John Dowdell

      In the June/80 issue of this newsletter (CUFORG) there are two reports of
a bright object seen by separate witnesses in Cambridge and aterloo.
      Both witnesses agree with the ob"ect's elevation ( approx. J0°) and duration
( approx. from 8:30PM-9:30PM). Both witnesses also, fortunately had access
to binoculars,-and one witness, Dag Balzer , whose binoculars were somewhat more
powerful {7 x 50) was able with their help to sketch an interesting representation
of what he saw,- while the other witness, Bruce Park ( accomp. by wife & inlaws )
was fortunate enough to be able to photograph the object using a 400 mm. tele­
photo lens,-luckilJ capturing as reference, a passing planel
      The vaterloo/Wellington airport when contacted, reported "no weather
baloon launched that day." *However, it must be remembered that: ( a ) the W/W
airport has no radar facilities, ( b ) baloons equiy ped to measure atmospheric
pollutants;  ·  NOAA weather baloons;     NASA and/or industrial/military baloons,-
may be launched by any of literally hundreds of agencies across North America • • •
      Due to the fact that the planet Venus ( then an "evening" star) was nearing
'inferior conjunction' ( ie. between Earth and Sun ) , and therefore at its brightest,
the general consensus was that the object was in fact the planet Venus.
      At this point, I would like to say that both my wife and I also observed,
what must have been the same object, during a similar ( but earlier, 7:30-9:15 PM)
time-slot, at about the same elevation reported by Dag and Bruce (30°), from a
location in mid-Kitchener. I confess, at first I thought I must be viewing Venus,
but for the following reasons soon discounted that notiona
      1) The object was brilliant well before sun-set.*It is a fact that although
         Venus can be viewed during daylight, it is never bright enough to be
         seen without some practice and/or knowledge of its position. I have
         been able to do this on occasion, and I assure you, even at maximum
         brilliance (-4.3), Venus in the daylight sky is still a rather faint
         point of light. Also, the RASC Observer's Handbook (1980) gives a mag­
         nitude of -2.6 for Venus, near the time of inferior conjunction, which
         may suggest that due to its angular nearness to the sun at such times,
         its relative magnitude decreases drox earth-bormd observers due to
         atmospheric scattering of sunlight. ( In Astronomy, the larger the number
         after the minus sign, the brighter is the object ) . My personal estimate
         of whatever it was we viewed, was between mag.-5.5 and -6, several times
         brighter than Venus ever becomes.
     2) Both my wife and I agreed, that when references such as hydro poles or
         house roofs were used to determine direction and rate of movement,
         the observed movement was ( i) somewhat erratic ( ii ) away from the
         ecliptic ( iii ) considerably faster than were it a planet "moving" across
         the sky in accordance with Earth's rotation.
     The real Venus was � low in the WNW just after sun-set ( 8: 50 PM, E .D. T.),­
and although technically visible until about 9:30 PM,-the witnesses do not mention
if, when the object disappeared, it was approaching the horizon1 as wovltl '- tk ca� u�Jfl. �nuf.

clear sky } -for no apparent reason ( if it were Venus ) ,-slowly dimmed and dis­
appeared while still above 18 ° elevation!
