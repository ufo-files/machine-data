Re: Letter to the Editor: Keep creationism out of science class

   There is some interesting sleight of hand going on at the heart of this
   article. You start off with an under-the-table argument from adverse
   consequences, arguing that "[b]y the accounts of biologists and historians,
   Darwin . . . established evolution as a fact of science and effectively
   demolished the theory of 'special creation'" and proceed to:
        The above described situation unfortunately makes not
        only for an intellectual problem, but a moral problem.
        Specifically, at issue here is that a theory of science is
        being passed off as a fact -- a fact furthermore said to
        completely overturn the sincere religious beliefs of
        millions
   First of all, there is more than a bit of an equivocation going on here.
   You use "theory of special creation" in one sentence but switch to "sincere
   religious beliefs" shortly thereafter.   Of course, millions of theists do
   not see evolutionary theory as in any way impinging on their *religious*
   belief in special creation. I'll leave it to you to quibble with those
   people theologically. What you are doing here is taking a *specific*
   account of special creation, that makes *specific* historical claims as to
   *how* special creation proceeded, many of which are subject to empiric and
   scientific investigation, and trying to shield those claims from scientific
   investigation (and, more importantly, to prevent the education of children
   in public schools of the results of those investigations), based on an
   appeal to the ethical principles of "justice, beneficence, and autonomy".
   Furthermore, you are doing this despite the fact that many of the
   proponents of this version of special creation claim the mantle of
   "scientific creationism". In short, you seem to be making the argument
   that *within* science and science education, a belief can be clothed in
   terminology of and given the label of "science" but scientists should
   refrain from pointing out where that belief fails as science, in deference
   to the *religious* sensibilities of the proponents of that particular
   "theory of special creation". Needless to say, I do not accept that it is
   anymore unfair for science to describe its empirical findings in this case
   than it is for it to report on the causes of lightning, in contravention of
   the beliefs of the adherents of Zeus and Thor. *If* that is an adverse
   consequence, that may simply be "too bad".
   You also try to smudge the distinction between individual "evolutionary
   theorists [who] have a demonstrable penchant for debunking religion" and
   science education as it is actually proposed to be taught in public
   schools. The former are as entitled to their metaphysical, philosophical
   and theological views (and to express them) as any of the believers in a
   particular account of special creation. If you want to make a case that
   the teaching of science in public schools violates justice, beneficence,
   and autonomy, then you have to do so from the actual syllabus, not from the
   words of unnamed "theorists".
   Unfortunately, that is all the time I can give this for the moment.
   Perhaps I will be able to get to more later today.
