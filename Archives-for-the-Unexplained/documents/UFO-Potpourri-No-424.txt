                 LASER BEAM CAN POP OUT SINGLE CELLS

           A mystery, that some people believe to be related to the UFO mystery,
is animal mutifations. One of the reasons given is the apparent use of advanced
technologies in performing the mutilations.      Researchers have found evidence of
very accurate incisions, actually cutting between the cells in the removal of
organs.
           Some researchers have suggested that military units an-ned with field
laser packages could be performing the radical surgeries.          Others argue that the
accuracy needed to complete the operations is greater than what could be done
in the field.    Because a lot of the advanced laser technology is classified, it is
difficult to ascertain the facts.
           Some light may have been shed on the subject by an article in the
August 1 5, 1998 issue of Science News.         Scientists' Karin Schutze and Georgia
Lahr at the Academic Hospital in Munich, Germany have devised a laser that
cuts out a cell and flips it into a waiting test tube. This process offers researchers
a way to remove single cells from a tissue sample in less than 30 seconds.
           The process is a bit complex.        First, the researchers affix a slice of
tissue to a thin plastic sheet and mount it on a glass slide above a highly focused
ultraviolet laser.   Looking through a microscope, they identify a particular cell,
then burn around its edges with the laser, cutting out the cell like a cookie from a
sheet of dough.
          Then, by doubling the power of the laser and adjusting it to focus below
the target cell, they launch the individual cell vertically off the glass slide and
catch it with the cap of a test tube. The cell rides the strean1 of photons from the
laser like a surfer on top of a wave.      The cell can be beamed several millimeters
away, even against gravity.
          Other researchers have developed similar laser-based techniques to
isolate clusters of cells.   In one such method, a team at the National I nstitutes of
Health in Bethesda, MD., uses an infrared laser to heat a plastic film laid on top
of a slice of tissue.   The film melts above the target cells and sticks them to a
plastic cap held above the sample.         When the "'ap is lifted away. it carries the
cells with it.
