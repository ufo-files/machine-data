Thank you for your recent letter inviting me to
become a member of your committee. It came at a time when
I was about to write to you to offer any services that the
Center for UFO Studies and I could render to this committee.
I am happy, therefore, to accept your kind invitation, esp-
ecially since I am fully in accord with its stated objectives.
Anyone trained in astrophysics, especially at the University
of Chicago, would welcome any sincere and objective effort
to sweep away as much pseudo-science as possible.
No one could deny that the UFO phenomenon has foster-
ed much pseudo-science. Yet, I have often felt that the in-
vestigation of UFO's offers a good means of teaching the
scientific method, especially to young people whose interest
in the field is rather high. As a matter of fact, the Center
is now producing a thirty minute film for schools called
"Getting to Know the UFO" which is based on the above idea.
In the investigation of a UFO report principles of meteor-
ology, astronomy, and the earth sciences in general are essen-
tial for separating fact from fiction. It is pointed out in
this movie and in our other literature that the "U'' in "UFO'
simply means unidentified and emphatically does not automat-
ically equate to "little green men from outer space" or
to extraterrestrial spaceships.
In your own field I imagine that the subject of UFO's
poses somewhat of a challenge. When the Gallup Poll indicated
that fifteen million Americans say that they have seen UFO's
and that 51% of the American public believes that UFO's "are
for real", these are certainly numbers having sociological
implications. Perhaps there are a number of Ph.D. thesis
topics lurking in such statistics.
My interest in UFO's lies solely in the interdisci-
plinary challenge that the phenomenon presents. I am not

The National Archives has recently come into posses-
sion of the Air Force's files for Project Blue Book, the two-
decade-long investigation conducted to determine whether
unidentified flying objects were for real. The project was
closed in 1969 after the government decided that none of
the 12,618 cases in the file indicated the existence of extra-
terrestrial vehicles.
The collection has been available for perusal by scholars
willing to make the trip to Maxwell Field in Alabama. Now,
at the Archives, anyone will be able to walk in and see for
himself the tangible residue of all the excitement that began
with the "first" UFO sighting, over Mt. Rainier, in 1947-
newspaper clippings, scrawled letters, technical papers,
drawings, blurred photographs, and bits of junk collected
from purported landing sites.
The material consists of about 42 cubic feet of written
materials, photographs, a few dozen artifacts, a number of
sound recordings, and 39 films. Everything but the tapes,
films, and artifacts has been put on microfilm. The Air
Force has blacked out all the names of the people who
contributed materials; otherwise, the files have been trans-
ferred unabridged
It is an assortment, judging from the brief sampling
Science took, that only a hardened UFO buff could love.
The artifacts, only a few dozen in all, have all been
identified by scientists as being quite earthly: some tiny
filaments identified as radar chaff, a couple of small
strange-shaped lumps of nylon residue, a strange large
bullet-shaped mass that turned out to be a pipe cleaning
anode, a piece of volcanic rock, a few grains of charred
dirt, an arrowhead said by the sender to have been bent
when he let fly an arrow at an extraterrestrial visitor, a
mysterious cloudy little ball identified as a part from a roll-
on deodorant.
