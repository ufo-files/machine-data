POSSIBLE METEORITE CRATER UNDER CHESAPEAKE BAY:      About 35 million years
ago, a large meteorite blasted a crater at least 85 km. across at the
mouth of what is now Chesapeake Bay, says C. Wylie Poag of the U. S.
Geological Survey in Woods Hole, Massachusetts.      Poag and fellow USGS
scientists identified what they believe to be a buried impact crater using
seismic refection techniques to image rock layers under Chesapeake Bay.
They also studied jumbled sediments found on land in Virginia and the
Delmarva Peninsula.   Their report was published in the August 1994 issue
of Geology.   The seismic profiles show disturbed rock layers below the
present bay floor that resemble a cross section of an impact crater.       The
land outcrops contain broken rocks and jumbled sediments that the team
interprets as coming from tsunamis (commonly referred to as tidal waves).
At the time of impact, the site was about 100 km. offshore.      The geologists
think the crater could also be the source for a field of tektites that
stretches from Barbados to New Jersey to Texas.      Tektites are glassy rocks
formed when impact- melted rock is flung high into space, chills quickly,
and falls again to earth.   Poag and his colleagues are now searching
through the deposits looking for the telltale signs of high- velocity impact
shocks.   These are considered by geologists to be diagnostic of meteorite
impacts.   Source: Astronomy Magazine, January 1995.

THE 1908 TUNGUSKA EXPLOSION : In 1908 , a mysterious blast exploded in the
remote wilderness of Tunguska, Russia.       The common explanation for the
blast is that an iron meteor crashed into the ground.        If the newest
theory proves true, however, is offers a chilling preview for what could
be the greatest catastrophe to strike the earth since the great flood.
A pair of NASA scientists, Christopher Chyba and Kevin Zahnle, along with
Paul Thomas, as astronomer at the University of Wisconsin, have reached
the conclusion that the massive d estruction at Tunguska was the result of
a shock wave generated when a small stone meteor or asteroid exploded about
five miles above our planet's surface.       The group calculates that the
meteor was probably about 100 feet across when it plunged into the atmos­
phere, the stress of its decent was greatest on its front.         This stress
ripped the meteor apart until it exploded.       Had the meteor been composed
of iron, its fall to earth would not have slowed and its impact would have
left a crater.   Not one expedition to Tunguska has ever found evidence of
an impact crater.    If true, then this scenario of an aerially exploded
meteor presents terrifying possibilities.       The blast destroyed more than
850 square miles of forest.      Experts have concluded that the force of the
blast may have been as powerful as 20 million tons of high explosives.         If
all this destruction was the result of a lone meteor only 100 feet across,
what would happen if one twice that size came our way?        Or ten times that
size?   Or larger?   The answer to these questions might come at any time.
The earth is surround ed by billions of comets and meteors, some of which
come dangerously close each decade.       Keep watching the skies.

CROP CIRCLE IN NEW YORK STATE
     A mysterious crop circle appeared last August in a Central New York
corn field, leaving UFO investigators abuzz about the second formation in
the region since 1993.
     The latest discovery is on the north shore of Oneida Lake, in the
town of Vienna in Oneida County.
     "The folks had farmed there for about 25 years and they've never seen
anything like it," said Ron Taylor, a Baldwinsville geologist who investi­
gated the formation for the Central New York Questors, a group that studies
strange happenings.  Taylor said they are awaiting test results, but haven't
ruled out the possibility weather conditions caused the phenomenon.
     The circle of flattened corn stalks is about 19 feet wide, and covers
most of an 11-row corn field, Taylor said.
     "It wasn't perfectly round," he said.  "It was sort of elliptical,
and looked almost like a comma."
     The family that owns the land has no explanation, Taylor said.  The
family does not want to make its experience public and harvested most of
the flattened corn to keep away curiosity seekers.
     "They just wanted it kept kind of quiet," said Suzanne Ezra, Central
New York coordinator for the Center for the Study of Extraterrestrial
Intelligence.
     "They weren't real open to the possibilities," Ezra added.  "The
father doesn't believe in UFOs, and he just wanted it done and over with."
     Taylor said the family permitted him to take samples of the flattened


                                   - 7 -
corn on Saturday.    He said he was told the damage occurred Aug. 14.
     "You could see a counterclockwise swirl of corn, with corn near the
middle inter - braided and wrapped around each other.  Most of it they had
picked.  But we did find enough corn on the stalks for samples."
     The samples were sent for analysis to w.c. Levengood, a plant bio­
physicist who studied crop circles discovered last summer off Route 28.
     The formations found in Helen Pyc's oat field in July 1993 reminded
many of crop circles that have been found in Britain.    Some of the British
circles have been blamed on rare weather conditions or pranks.    Others
are unexplained.
     State police suggested the Route 28 circles were the result of mischief,
but never solved the case.    UFO investigators had the soil and grain sam­
ples analyzed by Levengood at Pinelandia Biophysical Laboratory in Michigan.
Levengood found similar characteristics to the British circles.
     Results in the Oneida Lake case are expected in about a month.    Neigh­
bors reported a thunderstorm moved through the area during the day.
     The landowners say the circle appeared between 9: 30 a.m. and 7 p.m.,
when they were away from home, Taylor said.
     The National Weather Service confirmed thunderstorms were in the area
during the day, but they had no reports of tornadoes or other severe winds.
They had not received reports about the crop circle.
     "This is the first I heard about it," said Kirk Lombardy, a meteoro­
logist at the National Weather Service radar station in Binghamton, which
covers Central New York.
     "If it was hot that day it might have been a dust devil," Lombardy
said. "On a real hot day the warm air from the ground rises very rapidly
and begins to spin.    It looks like a tornado.  They usually go as far as
a field maybe, and then they dissipate."
     Lombardy ruled out that possibility after he found the day's tempera­
tures were in the 70s.
     "I would say there is the possibility this could be from some sort of
whirlwind vortex," Taylor said.    "It's hard to think somebody would want
to leave a message in an 11- row wide corn field that engulfs all but
two rows."
     He added, "If Dr. Levengood doesn't see anything there, I guess we'll
dismiss it as a freak of nature."    cr: Syracuse Herald Journal Aug. 24, 1994 .
