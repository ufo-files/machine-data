    The immediate need is for, first, data collection and second, analysis of observed
phenomena: these would include identified categories such as earthquake lights,
mountain lights, St Elmo's fire, feux toilets, plasma-vortices &c as well as ball lightning
itself ; and unidentified individual observations such as those at Hessdalen, Yakima,
Piedmont &c.
    These operations should be conducted without preconceived constraints as to what
constitute appropriate parameters. For example : While BL is reasonably supposed to be a
purely physical phenomenon, there are indications that some luminous phenomena
(including some cases provisionally listed as BL) display puzzling indications of
interaction with their human observers. Eventually, a decision will have to be made
whether to disqualify any luminous phenomenon displaying such features as a candidate
for BL, or to confront the possibility that BL, too, may present such features. For the time
being, however, we should postpone evaluation and concentrate on building up our
collection of data on a non-restrictive basis.

The broadly-held consensus is that
       ' Ball lightning is a luminous, globular mass whose diameter is usually in the range
       10-40cm with a duration of between one and 10 seconds.' (1)
The use of 'Ball Lightning' as a label identifying a category of anomalous phenomena is
justifiable on grounds of convenience, but only so long as we bear in mind it is simply a
label, not a definition. Even as it is, many accounts of BL display inconsistent and
paradoxical features, so that we may reasonably wonder if we are including a diversity of
phenomena under the same label.
       No one can concern himself with BL for a moment without realising that the primary
problem is to identify what constitutes BL and what does not. Even someone whose
interest is solely in BL as a meteorological phenomenon must be concerned to locate the
boundaries which set it apart from other phenomena. However, I suspect that most of us
are interested in the BOL phenomenon as a whole ; and if we have defined our research
field specifically as BL, that is partly because it is the only type of BOL to have been
awarded an official label and a measure of scientific status. lt is academically respectable
- or at least relatively so - to admit to an interest in BL, whereas if we admit to an interest in
the ball-of-light phenomenon in its entirety we run the risk of exposing ourselves to
suspicions of naive amateurism. Nevertheless, as I hope to show, there are good grounds
for considering the possibility that the BOL phenomenon, taken as a whole, presents a
serious challenge to our understanding of the physical universe, and offers a way in which
we may hope to increase that understanding.
       Whatever approach we take to the BL phenomenon, we discover in a very short
space of time that a great many observations cannot be covered even by the generous
umbrella-description which opens this paper. Many BOLs are more than 40cm in
diameter, many more have a life-span which far exceeds 10 seconds. The following case,
for example, is conventional in most respects, in that it seems to relate to a typical
thunderstorm-related BL, but the object's life-span far exceeds what we have been taught
to expect:
       A rural policeman in France, about 6.30 in the evening, was suddenly aware of a
       bright light which he saw emanated from a ball of light which was dropping at great
       speed from a cloud to the earth. Curious, he started to walk towards it, when he saw
       another light rising from the summit of a tree, emitting bright flashes of light. lt
       shone for about a quarter of an /Jour, then gradually faded and vanished.
       Inspecting the tree, later, it was found that the lightning had pierced the interior of
       the tree for about 3m, then descended by the exterior as far as the ground, then
       travelled round to the far side of the tree and climbed it again. At the foot of the tree
       was a small hole, about 4cm in diameter, which retained some noticeable heat for an
       hour and a half. (2)


Such observations of prolonged duration are exceptional, but by no means rare ;
consequently we must either exclude such observations from our field of study - an
exclusion for which there is no scientific justification - or we must stretch our parameters
of duration to accommodate them.

     And so it is with each and every parameter generally attributed to the BL
phenomenon. lt is no exaggeration to say that every feature which is conventionally
ascribed to BL must be extended or revised, and in some cases quite contradicted, by
observations which have been made involving BOLs which in other respects appear to
conform to the consensus model. A proportion, no doubt, may be discounted on
grounds of witness error, but many are supported by a body of testimony which cannot
easily be dismissed.
     Examination of some of these paradoxical features reveals apparent similarities
with other luminous phenomena not normally supposed to be BL. This in turn suggests
that study of this wider range of phenomena may help us, on the one hand, to decide
what should and what should not be defined as BL; and on the other, to move toward a
taxonomy of the whole spectrum of anomalous luminous phenomena, of which BL is
probably a sub-category.


Let us start by making a rapid survey of the most prominent categories of BOLs. This is
not intended as a comprehensive catalogue, simply as a sampling to indicate the range
and complexity of the material which challenges us. In the same spirit, I shall pick out,
in each category, one or two examples which give us some idea of how such an
observation stretches any conventional idea of BL.
     For convenience' sake I have categorised these as to whether they seem (1) to be
atmospheric in nature (2) or to occur in an aerial context (3) or in a marine context (4) or
in a terrestrial context (5) or in association with humans. But these distinctions are only
for convenience, and there is no reason to suppose that each constitutes a separate
phenomenon or group of phenomena. In our present state of ignorance of the BOL
phenomenon. we cannot with finality define any specific manifestation as, say,
atmospheric or terrestrial. Not only may it be the result of an interaction between
atmospheric and terrestrial forces, there is also the possibility that both atmospheric and
terrestrial forces are themselves triggered by some underlying force or over-riding factor
- for example, to take one seemingly far-fetched explanation which has nevertheless
been seriously put forward, the sun-spot cycle.
     A further caveat. First. although most of these observations were made by
competent people in conditions which allowed for reasonably accurate observation, and
though many of them were corroborated by, say, a ship or an aircraft's crew, the fact
remains that they are ultimately no more than anecdotes resting on human testimony;
and as such, should be received with the usual caution. On the other hand, the
examples presented here are generally representative of a very substantial bulk of
similar material, so that to cast doubt on any one long-duration observation, for
example, would nevertheless leave us with many others to be accounted for. One has
only to look at the catalogues of William Corliss's Sourcebook Project (3) to be
reminded what a rich diversity of extraordinary observations continue to puzzle the
student of nature.

1    Observations associated with atmospheric factors
Our starting-point must be BL observations associated with thunderstorms : it seems
that the same forces which generate lightning in its various 'conventional' forms also
generate, occasionally and for unknown reasons. the phenomenon which has become
known, though possibly inaccurately, and certainly with a dismaying lack of precision, as
ball lightning. BL is at one and the same time the paradigm for any discussion of BOLs,
and also its central enigma.
     However, there are other types of BOL which also seem to originate in the
atmosphere. Perhaps the most interesting are those which Or Meaden supposes to be
generated by air vortices (4). Apart from their apparent association with the crop circle
phenomenon, and the probability that they are responsible for a proportion of so-called
UFO observations, these vortex-BOLs are some of the few species of BOL which can
boast some scientific support for the fact of their existence. They therefore represent a
welcome point of access to the phenomenon as a whole. They are not BL ; but they may
be distant cousins.
     What appears to present during this type of of event is exemplified by this case :
     Chapelle-Taillefer, around 02.30, 19.03.1967 : Three witnesses saw a� orange/red
     light, estimated diameter 10m, descending from the sky at about 45°, terminating its
     path in a field a few metres away from them. lt was egg-shaped, with the pointed
     end upward, and was surrounded by a halo of white sparks 5m long which were
     brighter than the ball itself. There was sound, compared by witnesses to a car's
     starter or to an old windmill. The ball seemed to sink into the ground in a burst of
     sparks, about 15 seconds after the observation commenced. A fourth witness saw
     the ball pass overhead. Next day a circular patch was found in the field. (5)
Although alternative explanations for this case were offered, Meaden invites us to agree
'how well the details of the story fit the requirements I have spelled out for the new
plasma-vortex'.
     One of the problems with the UFO phenomenon has always been how to reconcile
the apparent trustworthiness of an observer with the implausibility of what he tells us.
Meaden's theory enables us to see that witnesses can have made good, reliable
observations of objects which were indeed flying and unidentified, but which can be
attributed with some confidence to a hitherto unclassified natural phenomenon. His
suggestion has therefore been warmly welcomed by many serious ufologists (eg 6) who
accept Meaden's suggestion that
     the plasma vortices may be relevant to the UFO problem. By this I mean, first and
     foremost, those observations of luminous phenomena which hover in the sky or
     descend towards or away from the ground but which, for lack of scientific
     recognition, have been given the sobriquet 'UFO'. I also include those further
     reports which involve electrical failures to cars. unexplained effects on animals and
     humans, etc. in connection with proximal light forms of unknown origin. (5)

2    Observations associated with aerial factors


A great many intriguing accounts exist of aerial phenomena which might be classified as
BOLS :a problem here is that there are so many strange things moving about in Earth's
atmosphere - hence, of course, the extraordinary rise and sustained hold on public
interest manifsted by the so-called UFO phenomenon. To the range of orbiting heavenly
bodies, and occasional celestial visitors such as comets, meteors and meteorites, man
has added a proliferation of artificial satellites, and we cannot deny that it is possible -
though perhaps we may not think it very probable - that extraterrestrial visitors have
made their contribution.
     Just what proportion of UFO observations we should take under the BOL umbrella
is a wide and complicated question. Here and now, Jet us confine ourselves to one
specific manifestation, which has the especial merit that it occurred before the 'age of the
flying saucer' ( generally taken to have been ushered in by the Kenneth Arnold sighting
in July 1947). The so-called Foo Fighters of World War Two were paradoxical both in
their behavior and in the circumstances of their manifestation : for example :-


     On 22.12.1944, an American bomber crew over Germany saw two very bright lights
     climb towards them from the ground. On reaching the plane they levelled off and
     stayed on the plane's tail for about 2 minutes. They were huge, bright and orange­
     coloured ; they seemed to be under perfect control. (7)


     Winter, 1944 : During a mission over Austria and Yugoslavia, USAF bomber pilot
     William Leet reported a BOL which suddenly appeared alongside his plane 'like a
     light switch being turned on'. Estimated distance from the wing-tip, 75 to 1OOm
     laterally and about 5m to the rear of it. lt seemed two- rather than three­
     dimensional, like the amber light of a traffic signal, but not like any earthly light :
     estimated diameter 3m. lt stayed with the plane for about 45-50 minutes, then
     suddenly was gone. lt was seen by all the crew : 'Our gunners wanted to shoot it
     down, but I ordered them not to. I told them if it was hostile, it would already have
     shot us down. Let's try to figure out what it is, I told them. ' ( 8b)
A duration of 45+ minutes far exceeds the life-span of any scientifically accepted BOL
phenomenon. Leet himself, in his 1979 book ( 8a ), is sufficiently influenced by current
UFO thinking to ask 'Could it possibly have been a space craft piloted by advanced
beings from another world?' and speculates that the BOL could have somehow
protected his plane from enemy attack. However, he does not propose this suggestion
with any conviction, and really it does no more than express his bafflement at the
experience.
     The explanation which comes immediately to our minds will no doubt be a plasma
created by the aircraft's progress through the atmosphere :but if so, it is curious that
only one wing-tip created such a plasma, and it is hard to account both for its extended
duration and for its intense luminosity in the night sky ( there was nothing for it to reflect
from) - it was bright enough for Leet and his crew to suppose their plane had been
picked up by German searchlights. But they saw no beams coming from the ground, one
crew member reported 'a blinding glare that seemed to come from above' and more than
one spoke of intense heat which may or may not have been associated with the BOL.

     This is just one instance of several such reports, mainly from the European theatre
of war but also from the Japanese. But the most puzzling feature of the Foo-fighter
phenomenon is that it was for the most part limited to a very brief historical period - two
or three years of WW2 - and even then occurred only occasionally, in the course of
operations in the German and Pacific airspaces. Because the historical context is so
clearly a determining parameter, we are constrained to look to the historical
circumstances for an explanation. I think it is legitimate to speculate that some kind of
artifact, generated by military activities or the associated hardware, was interacting with
natural phenomena to produce the phenomenon.
     Or Richard Haines, in his important study of reports made by pilots during the
Korean war (9), includes many BOL-type observations. For example :
     10.02.52 :Lieut. Perez, flying a 8-29 bomber, reported a globe-shaped object,
     estimated size 1m across ... color resembled the sun, a light orange, occasionally
     changing to bluish ...the outer edge appeared to be fuzzy and it seemed to have an
     internal churning movement like flames or fiery gases... it came in on the same level
     as the B-29, remained in the same relative position for approx one minute, then
     receded on the same path, fading in the distance...    '




If this is a naturally occurring phenomenon related to mechanical flight, why does it not
occur more frequently? There are occasional references to similar phenomena in a non­
military context, such as the following:
       April 23, 1964. Crew of an aircraft flying over Bedford, England, reported 'a loud
       bang and a whitish-blue flash of light. A ball of blue light the size of a football
       appeared on the starboard wing tip. lt vanished in two seconds (11)
but the great majority of accounts - particularly those in which a long duration is cited -
relate to military flying : we have to ask, are there any special features of military flying
which distinguish it from civilian flying? The 2-second duration of the incident just cited
falls a long way short of Leet's 45+ minutes : can we be sure that we are confronted by the
same phenomenon in both cases? Then again, while military aircraft are different in many
respects from civil aircraft, they are surely not so very different? The wartime aircrews are
liable to be in a state of unnatural tension - but there are many circumstances in civilian
flying which would also lead to unusual stress and provoke the same psycho-physiological
responses. Yet they do not seem to trigger the same BOL reports.
       lt seems beyond doubt that the too-fighter phenomenon is a real one, in the sense
that it comprises a number of observations which are more-or-less similar to one another
and more-or-less distinct in some respects from other comparable phenomena. As such it
vividly demonstrates one dimension of the BOL challenge. This is that, in the case of the
too-fighter as in that of many other categories of BOL, even if we recognise its similarities
to other BOL phenomena, even 11 we successfully ascribe it to a natural cause, there remain
contextual circumstances associated with it which require elucidation: these must either
be shown to be illusory, or be taken into account in the eventual explanation.

3      Observations associated with marine factors


The best-known BOL phenomena associated with the sea are reports of the manifestation
popularly known as St Elmo's fire, which is sufficiently well-understood for us simply to
note its possible relevance to other phenomena.
       But there are also a number of observations of BOLs apparently originating from
beneath the surface of the sea : for example :
       12.11.1887 : off the Newfoundland coast, the crew of the steamer Siberian saw a
       huge ball of fire rise from the sea and soar into the air. lt floated against the wind
       and headed towards the ship. Just when a collision seemed certain, it changed
       course, shot into the sky, and disappeared within a few minutes. (11)
Though sometimes listed as an Unidentified Submarine Object, there is no reason to
suppose that this was anything other than a natural phenomenon :but what kind of natural
phenomenon? To hypothesise a submarine volcano is by no means to explain what
exactly the fireball was made of, and how it acquired the momentum to float against the
wind. (Clearly, it is more strange that something should float slowly against the wind than
that it should be propelled violently against it.)
       Andres Alfaya, in the course of his duties as a Cuban security officer, investigated a
number of anomalous submarine incidents which defy conventional explanation. For
example :
       On 07.06. 1965, the fishing-boat of Archibaldo Centeno was for 15 minutes
       accompanied, under the water, by a luminous object, larger than his boat, and which
       he could not escape by manoeuvring his vessel. (12)
Though the Cuban authorities ascribed this and other such incidents to U.S. naval activity,
some of them seem to exceed the limits of what is conceivable, even allowing a generous
margin for secret American naval technology. On the other hand, there are problems also
with natural explanations : Corliss has outlined many of these difficulties (3).



4      Observations associated with terrestrial factors


BOLs of terrestrial origin have been the most extensively studied for the simple reason that
they are the most easily studied. Yet extensive study has not succeeded in satisfactorily
explaining any of them.
       Even the type of BOL which probably has the longest historical record of all - the
feu folie/- continues to elude us, though from time to time a scientist is kind enough to
provide us with what he claims to be a 'definitive' explanation. Folklore provides us with a
wealth of anecdotal material : here is one, taken virtually at random, which illustrates the
enigmatic quality of the phenomenon :
       A very intelligent and well-balanced girl I know told me how, one night as she was
       walking home only an hour or so after dark, she saw a Will-o'-the-wisp [the popular
       name in the British Isles] moving slowly along the bog parallel with the road and not
       50m away. As soon as she stopped to look at it, it suddenly changed course and
       began to come straight towards her, increasing its speed. This rather shook her
       determination to examine it with scientific coolness, so she hastily moved down the
       road a bit. To her consternation it changed direction too and still came straight for
       her, now crossing the soft bog as fast as a man could run on a road. This proved to
       be too great a strain on her morale and she promptly turned and made for home with

       all the speed she could. To this day she is convinced that it was directed by a real
       intelligence. (13)


This anecdote, one of thousands which tell a more-or-less similar story, includes the
characteristic observation by the witness that the feu toilet seemed to be behaving
intelligently. However, even if we discount this as a subjective impression, there remains
the fact that the duration of the phenomenon defies most of the scientific explanations that
have been proposed. When there was an outbreak of 'fireballs' near Saratoga, Texas, in
1961, Or Edwin Hays, head of the biology department of the nearby Lamar State College of
Technology, offered the 'conventional' explanation of marsh lights - 'swamp gas' ignited
by spontaneous combustion. Curtis Fuller, the editor of Fate magazine, responded:
       We would like to challenge Or Hays and every other proponent of this old chestnut
       of a theory to prove it. A common scientific criticism of psychic phenomena is that it
       is not repeatable under laboratory conditions. Well, we challenge the swamp gas
       theorists to produce a little swamp gas under laboratory conditions, ignite it
       spontaneously, and produce their own little fireballs that go bouncing merrily along
       for minutes at a time. (14)


There is more than one difficulty with the marsh-gas explanation, but the most serious
objection is that of duration. Feux toilets have been observed for periods up to one hour,
sometimes even more. If they had remained in the same location, we could hypothesise
that they were sustained by a steady supply of fuel from that particular portion of the terrain
- a natural source of methane gas or some such. But this is not the case : on occasion,
they have been followed, moving in a seemingly purposeful manner, over a distance of a
kilometre or so, as in the following account :
       In October 1938 I had developed an interest in the folklore of the Ozarks. I had been
       told that if I went to a certain old sawmill pond after dark I would stand a chance of
       seeing one of the local spooks - a ball of blue light rising from the ground and
       drifting into the woods. With the fellow who had told us about it my dad and I went
       to the millpond one evening carrying an old lighted barn lantern. We had been
       there tor maybe 20 minutes when around the end of the pond about 10m away, a
       small blue light began to form near the ground. lt was about the size of a softball
       and its colour was a clear cornflower blue. Dad picked up the lantern and we started
       toward the strange light. By the time we were 3m from it, it was about 1m off the
       ground and had expanded to the size of a basketball. Its colour was still clear and
       bright but now more a robin's egg blue. We were less than 2m from the light when it
       started to move away. Our companion picked up a piece of a tree limb and shoved it
       into the ground to mark where the light had first appeared.
          We started following it down the road. lt had now taken up a swinging motion :
       the high point of the arc was about 1.3m above the ground and the low maybe 0.2m.
       At its lowest point it gave enough light for us to see the white sand of the road. The
       light went south about 200m to a one-room country schoolhouse. There it left the
       road, moved over to the building and passed from front to rear along the north side.
       We followed it along the back side of the school and back toward the road where it
       started south again. This time it went to a fork in the road, took the lefthand road
       and went about 600m to the gate on a lane leading to an abandoned two-room log
       house.

          The light went over the gate and by the time we opened it and passed through,
       the light had reached the house. We watched it make a complete trip around the
       house and then it went inside through the only door. We could see it passing the
       windows and through the cracks between the Jogs. lt came out after a few minutes
       and started back toward the road. At this time the light passed within 0.3m of me,
       moving smoothly about 1m above the ground. lt emitted a low soft hum like the 60-
       cycle hum of an electrical transformer.
          The light took pretty much the same route back to the pond without the detour
       around the schoolhouse. We were 6-lm from it when it reached the tree limb
       sticking out of the ground. Hovering there for perhaps half a minute, it became
       smaller and darker in size. When it had reduced to the size it was when we first saw
       it, it settled into the sand and was gone.
          I don't think this was ball lightning nor was it the classic will-o' -the-wisp. To
       this day I have no idea what it was - but three of us saw it and followed it for most of
       an hour. And it did return to its point of origin. (15)


Nothing we know of has the capability to sustain itself over so long a period.. let alone travel
over so long a distance. The return to its place of origin is a feature which the narrator is
right to emphasise, for this is particularly hard to explain in terms of any natural
phenomenon. Even if we set aside the behavioral characteristics of the object, its physical
properties are sufficiently baffling. Moreover, though some details suggest that this was a
feu follet of some kind, no convincing explanation has ever been given, as Fuller justly
points out, as to why marsh gas should spontaneously ignite in the first instance.


A very interesting category of BOLs of terrestrial origin are the mountain l ights and so­
called spook lights, which are seen recurrently in specific locations, often intermittently
over a period of centuries which rules out such simplistic explanations as poorly observed
automobiles or railway trains. An additional curious feature of such BOLs is that rarely has
any observer succeeded in approaching them. Other types of BOL - feux toilets and BL, for
instance - have been observed at very close quarters - often within touching distance :the
question, why mountain lights should be so exceptionally shy, constitutes an anomaly
within an anomaly.


From the investigation point of view, the most interesting category of all BOLs must be that
which emerges from the recent recurrent near-terrestrial observations at three locations in
particular - Piedmont, in Missouri (see 16) ; Yakima, in Washington State (17) ; and
Hessdal en in Norway (18).    All three have in common, not only the fact that they involved
low-level BOLs over an extended period of time within a relatively limited region, but that -
because of these facts - they were able to be studied not in retrospect only, as
spontaneous one-of-a-kind observations by casual witnesses who chanced to see them,
but over a period of here-and-now time. Moreover, the observers were not random
members of the public, but persons with some degree of knowledge and expertise, and in
some cases scientific qualifications :who were prepared to see them rather than being
taken by surprise : and who were frequently armed with cameras and other appropriate
scientific equipment. Consequently, the phenomena have been registered on instrumental
records, and there exist many hundreds of photographs which constitute our best objective
evidence for the existence of a BOL phenomenon unrecognised by current science.

       Unfortunately, though we know more about these three sets of lights than we do
about any other kind of BOL except BL itself, that is not saying very much. In the 1984
series of observations at Hessdalen, for example, though 188 observations were recorded,
only two of them were made in conditions which left no reasonable room for doubt that an
anomalous event had occurred.
       Nevertheless, the fact that these observations are of higher- than-average quality
does at least broaden our data-base. At Yakima, for example, the observations were made
in a largely forested area requiring continuous surveillance, which meant that there were
observation posts manned 24 hrs of the day and night, in particularly strategic locations -
few observers can ever have been so well-situated for their task! Experienced observers
were able to discount the usual natural phenomena, so when they tell us of something they
considered to be anomalous, they have every right to be given a serious hearing.
       Some reports say structured objects were seen, but most sightings - and all the
available photos - show only BOLs. Nevertheless, one 1972 witness described an object
the size of a two-floor house ; one reported that the object responded to her flashlight ; a
farmer who saw one of the same lights reported that it affected the instruments on his
tractor when it passed overhead. And so on....


Another category of terrestrial BOLs is earthquake l ights. a category of particular interest
because several researchers have taken it as the starting-point for their experimental work.
The first recorded sighting of earthquake lights dates from 373 BC in Greece, and their
existence is generally accepted, if not yet fully understood. Derr & Persinger have
proposed 'a number of potential mechanisms, including tectonic strain, exoelectron
emission, streaming potential, EM excitation of water droplets, and the fault zone as an EM
waveguide' (19). The fact that these experts feel the need to list such a catalogue of
possible causes, most of which are probably less than solidly established in their own
right, is an indication of the fragile state of earthquake light research. Moreover, it is in
respect of earthquake lights that the question of ultimate origin is seen to be most baffling.
For example :
       In May 1951, the Rock Solano and Contra Costa districts of California were shaken
       over a two-week period by 'blasts, explosions, shocks and jars' which commenced
       around 9 am and occurred periodically throughout the day. The authorities ascribed
       them to old explosives being destroyed by the military, but there are many problems
       with this explanation, notably the fact that a few weeks later the explosions began
       again. This time a naval base was affected, so the authorities began to take the
       incidents more seriously. Later in the year, in the same area, fireballs were
       reported : in one incident, a falling luminous object ignited a 200m row of trees, 7m
       above the ground, though no wreckage or ground trace was found. Some months
       later, there was a series of electrical storms. in the course of which many instances
       of BL were reported.
Such a succession of events, some of them seemingly terrestrial in origin, others

5      Observations seemingly associated with human factors

Once again, though, we are denied the chance to establish a positive across-the-board

Perhaps the most remarkable, because the best attested, instance of BOLs appearing in

such as the Society for Psychical Research : and it is difficult to discount the many reports,
many of them made by skeptics who came reluctantly to accept the testimony of their own
eyes where they had previously rejected what they had been told by others.
       Low-altitude aerial lights were seen on several occasions by numerous observers
hovering over chapels where a prominent preacher, Mrs Mary Jones, was scheduled to
appear, or where future converts lived. The conclusion, that the lights were physically real,
and that they were associated with Mary Jones, can be avoided only by denying the validity
of the testimony ; and that is not easy to do, considering the large number of independent
witnesses.
       But if we accept the observations as real and substantially accurate, it is hard to see
how we can avoid the logical consequence - that the lights were somehow generated by
Mary Jones herself. This is so contrary to received experience that most of us would prefer
to reject the testimony, however well confirmed. We could derive some comfort from the
fact that there existed a long-standing tradition of anomalous lights observed in the area,
dating back over a century or so - long before Mary Jones' lifetime. Kevin & Sue McCiure,

along a prominent geological fault line, which according to his hypothesis is capable of
generating short-lived luminous effects.

they be reconciled with the Mary Jones phenomenon as described by witnesses? lt is
possible that naturally-occurring lights were mistakenly attributed to her, by those who

the fact is. the phenomena challenge us to account for them somehow..

human aspect to the BOL phenomenon, it is not one we have the right to ignore.


The question arises : What can we do with this mass of variegated data? The logical
approach is to catalogue the apparent attributes, and see which ( if any ) are constants,
which are periodically recurrent, and which, though only occasional, are reported
sufficiently frequently to suggest that they are more than one-of-a-kind accidents, drawing
our data from the entire spectrum of BOL categories. Such a catalogue should enable us to
establish patterns which would provide a working basis on which hypothetical models can
be constructed.

of the form it might take.

We would start with such measurable - or at any rate, estimatable - parameters as siz�

Some behaviors are reported sufficiently often that they can be classed as recurrent ; for

can we conclude that there is a clear line of demarcation between t hose which don't melt

themselves accept that 'a number of facts do not fit into this simple explanation', the most

other cases, the glass is simply broken.

3 : intangible qualities


The testimony asserting interaction with humans, often of a seemingly intelligent nature, is
only one of the more enigmatic aspects of the phenomenon. Seemingly purposeful
changes of direction are also reported very frequently, giving rise to suggestions that the
BOL 'knows what it is doing' or is under intelligent direction. This kind of behavior has
given rise to a specific sub-section of the extraterrestrial theory for UFOs, which suggests
that many small lights are in fact remotely-controlled robot scouts sent out by alien
spacecraft to report, just as we send our space-probes out into space to collect data for us.

way of explaining a case such as the following :
       Queensland, Australia ; date unknown. A Mr Pike, walking in marshy terrain, saw a

human imagination, how much is objective fact, before we reject such testimony. We have
noted two cases in which BOLs seemed to act benevolently, guiding lost travellers to
