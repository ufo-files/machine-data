                                    Welcome




Late again. Oh well, best thing to do is to abandon deadlines and you’ll see us when
you see us. Ironically, issue 11 might not be far behind.

As is the way with these things, an underlying and continuing progression of thought
has brought me to a point where I have become convinced about the process that will
be used for introducing the Human race to the reality of alien life. Yes, I’ve cracked it
and I’m serious. It will be through the eventual announcement of life existing on Mars.
It’s really very obvious actually when you think about it, because that announcement
will come at some point, but it won’t be about Martians. It will be about microbes; life
that can only be seen under a microscope.

That isn’t the way of course that most of us would like it to be done. What we want is
drama, perhaps an unplanned event like the Phoenix Lights (assuming that was
unplanned) and the President of the United States being forced into making a
statement. That though we are unlikely to see. First we need to get comfortable with
the fact that something else with a pulse lives elsewhere other than here on earth and
small creepy crawlies are not too threatening to the general public. Then, gradually,
the debates start up and the process unwinds. It won’t be quick. And a small part of
that process will be the next Spielberg film War of the Worlds which is dripping in
irony. For many consider that Orson Welles’ radio adaptation in 1938 was partially
responsible for the mess we’re in right now with this subject.

Many disagree with what I’ve written above but in my bones I feel this is the way this
story of ours will unfurl.

For the last three or four years I have been setting myself a target of getting over to
the States to attend a conference ­ I have one or two in mind ­ and to see some old
friends. I set myself the goal of the latter part of the year and every time it comes
round I seem to find excuses for not going. It’s very depressing. I am determined to
get over this year.

As is the cyclical nature of these things, the debate about the Alien Autopsy footage
has re­surfaced again in one or two places, and indeed, we carry an article in this
edition that draws heavily on the topic. I’m glad that things come round again because
it gives me the chance to re­focus on something that I hadn’t looked at properly the
first time. I was easily swayed by the uproar the film generated when it first came out
and had long put it in a box in my mind labelled “probably fake”, but I had done that
on the backs of other people’s opinions. Now I feel differently about it. There’s no point
in pointing to various bits of analysis or things that people did or did not say at this
juncture; all the evidence that was ever likely to surface has done so and we need “the
next step” to take this any further. My point is that experts have contradicted each
other and countered each others points and Santilli has done his bit in answering
various questions and owning up to his misdemeanours. I don’t feel, looking at the
evidence out there that one can genuinely come to an objective decision about it. Most
people have arrived at their conclusions subjectively but aren’t honest or bright enough
to either recognise that or admit it. In fact, it would be an interesting study just to look
at the Ufological community’s reaction to the whole farrago purely because it not only
touched upon so many vested interests but also reached into the very souls of
                                                                                           4


ufologists in terms of what they wanted and what they expected when that great
moment arrived and they saw their first ever non terrestrial being. My own thoughts
have changed very rapidly over the last few weeks with this and I am now of the
opinion that the film itself is genuine. At this moment though, I’m only “so­so” about
whether what we’re actually looking at really are aliens or something else. But this
subject isn’t going to go away.

As usual, this edition carries a wide range of subject matter and in particular, we step
into the topic of crop circles for the first time in a serious manner. Jenny Randles
appears to have got all this sorted out, or so she keeps telling everyone, ages ago
actually. I’m very pleased for her. I’ve not bothered to read what she’s written and
probably won’t. Difficult to take statements like that seriously and hence the folk
behind them.

        Interview with Andy Thomas




Mention crop circles to any “serious” ufologist and if you get a reaction at all, it will be
the standard, “They’re all man made”. Or, they shift uncomfortably at the thought of
them because it brings to mind an image of mature baby boomers reliving their hippy
days with shades of the dreaded “New Ageism” thrown in. Serious ufologists care about
which thread screw fell of a particular alleged craft and in the background, there is
some vague awareness that crop circles equals “spirituality”, or, god forbid, thinking.

If you do read this, which of course you should, you will laugh your head off at the
similarities between the two sciences. Yes, sciences. The parallels are striking. Almost
the same problems, the same frustrations, the same difficulties.

So, methinks, if we are going to talk about crop circles in a semi­serious ufological
publication, we might as well speak to someone who doesn’t talk bollocks and can
speak to us at a level we appreciate and interact with.

He may not agree but I regard Andy Thomas as the leading voice of Cereology,
certainly in the UK and probably within the top 5 in the world. How do I come to that
conclusion? I’ve read his books, or some of them, and was delighted to be confronted
by a sane, rational, readable, and very wry and amusing individual.

Andy has certainly had his moments. Now an integral part of the Glastonbury
Symposium, his lectures in the past have been anything but conventional, as you will
discover. He probably redefined the concept of the word as his audiences were verbally
and emotionally hit hard over the head with a figurative hammer, as he went about
making his various points.

SM: I know that you were, and for all I know may still be a performer. Are your
performances and your interest in crop circles in any way linked?

AT: I’ve always been interested in creative things and I was in the music business on
one level for many years and wrote music and songs. I’ve been a gigging musician as a
keyboard player for 20 years plus now and I would say it was that artistic/creative
spark which the crop circles stimulated; I found the circles very inspiring when I
became involved with them. I used to write a lot of song lyrics and writing generally
was something I was very interested in, so when I became involved with crop circle
people and we started up our own organisation, which eventually became known as
Southern Circular Research, it made sense that I would write the newsletters. That’s
how it began. Then it turned into our journal, which was known as the Sussex Circular,
a few stapled bits of paper that then wound up selling 500 copies every month, which
was pretty good. (Now we have transferred our work to the website
www.swirlednews.com.)

Because of the journal, that was when I began to write a lot about crop circles. And
then eventually my performing side did become a part of it because I was asked to
give talks about them, which I had never done in my life. I’d never given a lecture,
although I was used to appearing on stage and already had a knowledge of stage
presence and making my voice heard through acting and singing and whatever. So
those skills then came in useful.

SM: I’ve never seen you perform but the impression I’ve got is that it’s quite ‘in your
face’. Would that be right?

AT: I give lectures about crop circles all over the world now. I’m off to Peru in a few
months time, for instance, which is very nice, but a lot of the time you’re talking to
smaller local groups where basically you’ve just got to give a straight talk. You can’t be
too flamboyant. My infamous reputation for big theatrical stuff really comes from my
connection with the Glastonbury Symposium, which is the world’s longest running
annual crop circle conference, held each July.

Originally I was just a speaker at the Symposium and now I’m one of its organisers,
but when I first arrived there in 1994, I found that other speakers often used to do a
little bit more than they would do normally. There was a slight theatrical bent to the
whole thing already. So I was inspired by that and took it on somewhat further. You’d
be up on stage and people quite liked you to do something that was a little bit
different. It started as small things, with the odd funny little prop or costume, and then
it expanded into full scale performances. We had the Queen, who turned out to be an
alien, onstage; we had people take their clothes off; CIA agents roaming around; and I
once came on in robes surrounded by worshipful followers! All this sort of thing. But it
was always to make a point.

Partly the reason for this was that I needed to wake people up as I’m normally on first
thing in the morning on a Saturday. The other need was to do something that’s just a
bit unexpected, to hopefully shake an audience out of their complacency about what a
lecture is. It seems to work, but the only thing you’re stuck with is that people tend to
remember the silly bits, but can’t always remember three years on what you actually
said!

SM: Have you ever had a negative response to any of your stage performances?
                                                                                           7


AT: Very few. Most people appreciate it. I think there’s one or two, particularly older
people, who like a lecture to be a lecture and that’s that, and who perhaps don’t
appreciate the more flamboyant side of it. But even when a man and woman took their
clothes off on stage, I think only one person said they thought it was a bit over the top.
Everybody else took the point and seemed absolutely fine about it, which was great
because I was expecting more complaints.

The theatrics are always to make a point, and obviously it’s not the sort of thing you’d
do in your local Women’s Institute hall. Normally I keep it straight. It’s the Glastonbury
lectures, and just occasionally elsewhere on request, where the stage gimmicks take
place. On the whole though, I do it straight.

SM: Was there a single defining moment in which you took an interest in crop circles?

AT: Yes, there was, definitely. I had been vaguely aware of crop circles in the mid
80’s, because there’d been one that had appeared near Dennis Healey’s home (ed ­
Healey, curiously, is a former Minister of Defence in the UK government), which is not
very far away from where I live, in East Sussex. I remember that being in the local
newspapers and then the nationals, and recall being curious, but for some reason it
didn’t particularly grab me at that point. There was a strangeness to it which appealed
to me, but my mind was involved in other things at the time.

It wasn’t until 1991 when it really clicked. I was on my honeymoon, of all things, with
my then wife (sadly, we’re now separated), and we went down to the West Country. I
was aware that crop circles appeared in the Wiltshire area and I remember just
casually thinking, “Oh it would be nice to see some of them while we’re down here.”
The year before, in 1990, there had been loads of stuff in the papers about it.

So I remember being aware of crop circles, but even then it was just a kind of
afterthought on our trip. I remember we were staying in a bed and breakfast place and
we just said to the landlady, “Are there any crop circles around here?” She went and
got some maps and showed us the places to go. It was the last day of the honeymoon
and we got out onto the road on a misty morning and saw a crop formation in the
distance. When the mist rose from it, and it became clearer, that was the defining
moment. There was just something which immediately grabbed me inside on a strange
feeling level that is really hard to define. I’ve described this before and the nearest I’ve
been able to get to it is to say it was like something I had been waiting for all my life ­
but I didn’t know it until it was there in front of my eyes. And it was like suddenly,
“Yes! There’s something about this which is going to change a lot of things.” And it did.

So I just walked around in a daze, visiting these crop circles just completely blown
away by the scale of them and the questions they raised, having absolutely no idea
what could have put them there, going through all the questions that people do. Could
it be a joke? Was it real? Where was it from? So that was the defining moment –
seeing that crop circle coming out of the mist. It was quite some distance away, but it
did it for me in that moment.

And then later that day, I actually walked inside some pictograms. It sent me on an
obsessive search. I just knew I wanted to be close to this mystery; I wanted to get
nearer to what it was about. And from there I joined the Centre for Crop Circle Studies;
I found their address in the back of a book which I’d bought and hungrily read through,
obsessively. I tried to find any other book I could about the whole phenomenon.

It was through the Centre for Crop Circle Studies that I met other interested people
down in the Sussex area where I live, and we decided to form our own group. So that’s
where things started. We’re still meeting in the same room, holding public meetings,
to this day.

SM: Colin Wilson, in a recent interview, reckoned that the number of man­made
circles is about 80% and the other 20% being genuine enigmas. Would you agree with
that percentage?

AT: I’ve always raised objections to putting percentages on this, because by and large
when you actually track down what people are basing their percentages on, it’s just
plucked out of thin air. It’s vague. When you actually try to clarify the criteria, and I’ve
had issues with other crop circle researchers about this before, you realise it’s not
based on evidence. It’s often just based on their vague feeling. And giving a
percentage based on a vague feeling is a contradiction in terms. It’s a waste of time.

When you look at the actual real percentage of known man­made formations ­ that
doesn’t mean there aren’t more ­ but if you just look at the known ones, it’s something
pathetic like 8% to 10% a year which can be provably shown to be man­made. Beyond
that, it’s speculation. So when people say 80% are man­made, I have to wonder where
that leap of faith comes, because that’s not based on evidence. It’s usually based on
rumours they’ve heard or it’s because they don’t quite like the look of that formation,
because it’s got a bit that doesn’t look quite right to their eye, blah, blah, blah.

But then you find everyone’s opinion is subjective. Everyone has a different view, and
more often than not it’s not based on evidence, nor on any proper analysis at all ­ it’s
just opinion and nothing more, and you cannot make up a percentage based on that. I
think comedian Vic Reeves said it all when he said that “88.5% of all statistics are
made up on the spot”, and I think that’s true. So, I would agree that there probably
are more man­made formations then there have been in the past, but to say that 80%
are man­made is an unprovable statistic and I just don’t think anybody can put their
finger on exactly what the percentage is.

My feeling on the whole is that it would be less than 80%, and there are not as many­
man made formations as that – but that’s my opinion. If I then make up my own
percentage, I’m talking bullshit too unless I’ve made a serious analysis of the evidence,
which people often don’t do these days. People often base an opinion on just seeing an
aerial photograph, and I’m sorry, but you cannot tell anything definitively from that.
You’ve got to go inside, you’ve got to take samples, you’ve got to measure it, you’ve
got to look at the ground evidence, look at its geometry, and there’s only a small body
of people that do that.

Researchers that sift through a load of aerial shots and then say, “I like that one, like
that one, hate that one, hate that one, that’s a hoax” etc., based on nothing but their
opinion and whatever mood they’re in that day, make for a very poor system. And
unfortunately, I think a lot of researchers do it like that. But it’s useless, and to base
these percentages, as people do, on that kind of analysis, if you want to call it that, I
think is wrong.

SM: It’s been well known for some considerable time as a result of research that there
are biological changes that occur within a field where a crop circle has appeared. Am I
right in thinking that that those same biological changes have also been noticed in
fields where there are circles that are known to be hoaxed or am I wrong there?

AT: I have never heard yet of any man­made formation, done as a demonstration or a
test which has ever produced these biological anomalies. To my knowledge, based on
the work of Nancy Talbot and W.C. Levengood etc., no man­made circle has ever
demonstrated these same affects.

About two years ago, there was a programme on the Discovery Channel where they
got these students from MIT to try to replicate the biological anomalies in crop circles
by making their own, and they couldn’t. Even there, the programme misrepresented
                                                                                           9


itself. It tries to make out that the students did better than they did, but if you read
Nancy Talbot’s account of what they did and didn’t manage, it proved that the effects
she and her team were detecting were not reproducible by any manual flattening
technique.

SM: How long does it take you to walk into a circle and come to the conclusion that it’s
a hoax?

AT: Well first of all, I never do that. I think it’s a massive arrogance for anybody to
stand in a field and quickly put their nose in the air and say, “Well in my opinion this
is…” I think when you go down that road, you’re in dangerous territory because you’ve
got to do a number of things before pronouncements can be made. If you want the full
criteria, if you like, on which you can base a proper opinion about a crop formation,
you’ve got to take samples, you have to measure it, you have to look at its geometry,
you have to find out under what circumstances it occurred, talk to local people; were
there any witnesses to strange lights or were there any witnesses to people seen in the
area that night? You have to look at the ground evidence, at the state of the crop, etc.
Now that takes a long time to do and just standing in a formation for about 15 minutes
as some people do, unfortunately, and then say, “Well this is a hoax”, is not viable. I
don’t think you can do that.

SM: You possibly misunderstood my meaning. When I said, “From when you first walk
into a circle” I wasn’t expecting a timescale of a few minutes. Possibly months after,
but you’ve really answered the question anyway – some considerable time after.

AT: Yes, and even then, to be honest with you, unless you were there when it was
made by either people or non­people, you can’t be certain, I don’t think, unless you
have absolute first­hand evidence, which most people never do. You cannot declare
with certainty the reality or otherwise of that formation unless you have that. All you
can do is express an opinion and I think it’s very important when people say, “I think
that one’s good, or that one’s bad” that it is made clear that it is an opinion, unless it is
based on absolutely scientifically collected data, which most times it isn’t.
                                                                                        10


SM: So it sounds like an incredibly muddled field, no pun intended, where the
statistics are a waste of time……

AT: Well no, the statistics are not a waste of time; it’s the people who make them
based on opinion and not evidence that create the problem. The point that I am
making here is that you can reach a more accurate level of understanding if you use
the actual data, but often people can’t be bothered to collect the data and therefore
just express opinions.

With enough time and enough scrutiny, I think you can make some very clear and
interesting observations on which maybe then better percentages can be based. But
what I’m saying is a lot of people do seem to reach this level where they can’t put the
necessary time and energy into ground research but are still expressing opinions. I
have been outspoken about this in the past and have upset people because of it, but
my take has always been that it is not a valid way to do research.

SM: Do you think there is pressure on researchers to take a sceptical point of view?

AT: I think what’s happened over more recent years is that some researchers have
been very keen to be taken seriously. If you’re not seen to take the man­made
component of it seriously, then you are open to attack.

What then happens, though, is that they can then become over­accommodating
towards the human circle makers in an effort not to be seen to be ignoring that part of
it, and wind up being very, very scared not to sound cranky or too New Agey. As a
result, certain researchers tend to play more towards the sceptical side because they’re
afraid of being made fools of.

That’s OK, but as soon as anyone goes down that road, often you then find they don’t
express their true beliefs. Instead they express opinions they think will sound good to
the media or to the so­called scientific establishment, as if they’re trying to have one
foot in that camp and another in the whole surreal, weird and wonderful camp at the
same time. I find that is not a position you can really be in for too long. Sooner or later
you’re going to fall into either the sceptical camp or the ‘believer’ camp ­ or you will be
placed into one of the two boxes by other people.

Now because I have always refused to go down this road of pandering to the sceptics
just to keep them happy, I therefore have been placed by a lot of people in the
‘believer’ box, as if I’m completely undiscerning and have no sense of reality at all,
because that’s the only way they can cope with it. They can’t cope with me still saying
“I believe there’s a real phenomenon here, based on the evidence”, especially if you’re
another researcher who no longer even bothers to spend time looking at the evidence
properly. It’s easier for them to paint me as some crazy person, while they continue to
try to keep the establishment happy by saying things which sound good, but which
actually aren’t based on real research at all.

SM: Would you care to name those researchers that you have respect for?

AT: I don’t think that would be fair either way because I wouldn’t want, in an
interview, to name researchers that I didn’t have respect for. So therefore I don’t think
it would be right to pick out any of them.

But I think there are researchers out there doing fantastic work and there are also
researchers doing not such good work. I think it’s the same in all areas. Suffice to say,
there are a lot of people who don’t necessarily hog the limelight, but who are doing
absolutely astonishing research, purely through their personal obsession with it. Maybe
this work will reach the light of day eventually; one hopes it will. I’m in contact with
                                                                                            11


various people like that. Often, the ones that hog the limelight are the ones no longer
doing the actual hard work, but that just seems to be the way of it. There is some
successful crossover, of course, happily, but there is still a sort of divide between some
of the personalities in the crop circle world, and those out there at the coal face, really
doing the research.

What I’d like to see is more of a meeting of the two approaches. It would be nice to get
more of a blend. You probably gather here, even as a crop circle researcher myself,
that I have criticisms of the way the arena has become. But I would say I think it is the
same in all areas. From what I know of UFO research, you get a similar phenomenon
occurring there – it’s just human nature. But I think with crop circles in particular,
because researchers are often put on the spot to give their reasons for why they
believe this is real, I think it creates more of an opportunity for this kind of polarity to
be brought to the surface more obviously.

SM: When I’ve read your comments about hoaxers, they’ve been restrained, at least
from what I’ve read. What is your opinion of hoaxers?

AT: It depends what you’ve read. You say “restrained”; I think I’ve been fairly
outspoken about them in the past. What I’ve never done is attack them as people, for
the most part, because I just don’t see the point. I think you have to accept there are
human circle­makers, that there is a certain level of the phenomenon that’s made by
people.

But I’m not particularly interested in that side of it. I’m more interested in looking at
the evidence which suggests that they were not involved, and I try to concentrate
more on that, without blinding myself to the human circle­making side of it. But that’s
not an aspect I’m that personally bothered about being involved with. Other people
investigate that. They talk to them at great length, and that’s fine. Other people can do
that, and I read their reports and views. Therefore, I’m not going to give my time and
energy to continually slagging the human circle­makers off. What’s the point? It gives
them more attention than they deserve. I have been restrained compared to some.
Some researchers have gone on record saying all the hoaxers should be rounded up
and shot, but I don’t see how that helps anybody. All that does is make us sound like
complete extremists.

SM: OK, perhaps that was an emotive over­reaction, but I can certainly understand
where they are coming from with that. I’m surprised that you say that you’re not
bothered by them because surely what they’re doing is getting in the way. They bring
ridicule on the subject and they hinder serious research.

AT: But they’re there. It doesn’t matter what you or I think about them, they are not
just going to go away. They’re there, and they do hinder the research on one level, but
on the other hand, they also keep us on our toes. They make us have to look very
clearly at what we are calling good evidence, and this is where you do have to take the
human circle­making into account. It helps bring it into balance at one level because
you are forced to scrutinise what you are claiming are your results for a genuine
phenomenon all the more accurately, and that’s not entirely a bad thing.

So, yes, they’re a nuisance at one level, but it’s just a fact of life. It’s just the way of it
and I think in any area of study you’re going to get a dark side and a light side, and
you have to find the balance in­between. I just can’t be bothered to get angry about
them anymore. There are certain researchers that I’ve seen waste a third of a lecture
criticising them, carping about how upsetting it is that hoaxers are there. Well, what’s
the point? It’s not going to make them go away. If anything, it feeds them. They think,
“Hey great, we’re having an effect. We’ll keep this up.”
                                                                                       12


SM: Do you think they’re pathetic?

AT: I don’t know. I think it depends what the motivations of the individual hoaxers
are. If they are just there for mischief and to have a sniggering tee­hee behind the
hand at what they see as all these pompous pseudo­scientists, if that’s their attitude,
then yes, that’s pathetic. If they are out there trying to see what really can be done, as
some research thing, okay, it’s still morally dubious because they’re not doing it with
permission, but perhaps that’s a bit better.

If they are creating art as some have claimed, as some kind of higher inspirational
thing and they just can’t help themselves with it, well, that comes down to whether
you believe them or not­ if they are, then okay, there’s another motivation to consider.
But I think that’s the problem – how does one discover the real motivation? These
people play games and don’t always tell the truth. For me to say they’re all evil wicked
people means that I therefore have got some higher knowledge of where they stand ­
and I don’t.

The ones I have spoken to over the years, and I have, inevitably, been on TV
programmes with some of them and chatted to them, most of them, with a few

2. Principles of Quantum Theory Relevant to Physio­ and Bio­chemical
Processes (15, 16, 17)


The failure of classical electro­dynamics to account for the spectral distribution of black
body radiation on the basis of a “continuous emission of radiation” concept led Max
Planck in 1901 to discard this concept and forward instead the hypothesis that black
bodies radiate energy not continuously but discontinuously in energy packets, called
quanta, given by the relation:
                      E = hv­1,                                               (1)
where E is the energy in ergs of the quantum absorbed or emitted (by a resonator
electron of an atom or molecule of the black body radiator), hv­1 is the frequency of
the resonator electron and of the resonant quantum absorbed or emitted, and h
(Planck’s constant, empirically determined), is equal to 6.62517 x 10–27 erg­sec.
Equation (1) is the fundamental relation of the quantum theory of radiation. Planck
further postulated that the resonator electron of an atom or a molecule could either
absorb or emit energy in the unit quantum:


                                   E1 = hv1­1                             (2)
                          or in whole number multiples, n, thereof:
                                         En = nhv1­1.                   (3)


Planck’s success with the quantization of black body radiation led Albert Einstein to
generalize the quantum theory on the basis of evidence existing in the newly
discovered photoelectric effect. In the photoelectric effect, Einstein pointed out, one
quantum of ultraviolet light brings about the ejection of one electron from a radiated
                                                                                                    93


surface (due to the phenomenon of resonance absorption by an electron), the
exchange of energy taking place in terms of Planck’s hv.            Einstein then generalized
Planck’s quantum theory by asserting that all electromagnetic energy of the whole
electromagnetic spectrum is quantized, and not only that of black body (infrared)
radiation. Thus, electromagnetic energy of all bands of the electromagnetic spectrum
is absorbed, emitted, or exchanged by atoms and molecules in terms of Planck’s
discrete quanta (5).


Another extension of Planck’s quantum theory, developed by Bohr and others, was that
an atom or molecule has a set of discrete energy states in which an electron possesses
a definite and invariable energy content independent of thermal energy. The set of
permitted energy states of an electron is usually referred to as “energy levels”. The
energy levels in turn are designated by “n”, which may take on any series of value 1,
2, 3, . . ., known as principal quantum numbers. There are three sub­energy levels
(which are designated by quantum numbers 1, m and s), due to the fact that the
energy of an electron may be spread over a major orbital, sub­orbital, sub­sub­orbital
and taken up in the spin of the electron.        The principal quantum number, n,
therefore, represents the major energy level to which an electron of an atom or
molecule belongs. An electron thus can be at one or another major energy level within
an atom or a molecule, but it may not acquire energy values intermediate to those of
the major energy levels.


  The means by which an atom or molecule acquires or loses (electronic) energy is by an electron
absorbing or emitting a photon. The absorption of a photon forces the electron (and hence the atom
 or molecule) into one of its higher major energy levels; thus the atom or molecule gains energy. If
   the electron emits a photon by “relaxing” to one of its lower major energy levels, the atom or
molecule loses energy. The energy lost or gained by an atom or molecule thus results from a loss or
 gain of a photon, which has an energy value represented by the energy difference of the two major
 energy levels the electron has spanned. Thus, if ∆E represents the gain of energy by an atom or a
molecule due to the absorption of a photon which forces one of their electrons from energy level n =
               2 to energy level n = 1, the gain in energy may be expressed as follows:


                ∆E = n1 – n2 = hv­1.                                      (4)

content is known for the highest major electronic energy level of an atom or molecule,
