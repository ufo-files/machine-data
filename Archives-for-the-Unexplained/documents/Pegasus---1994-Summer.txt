TONY WEDD- PHILOSOPHER, ARTIST AND FLYING SAUCEROLOGIST
                         by Jimmy Goddard

Tony Wedd was born inLangport, Somerset in 1919, and had a number of different activities. During
the war he was a pilot operating training aircraft in Canada; he also worked at a school for maladjusted
children and as a freelance artist. He was a very unconventional character, very conscious of the
failings of society and also open to new ideas.

It is the period of his life after the war that most interests ufologists and ley hunters. During this time
he was living in the beautiful Tudor village of Chiddingstone in Kent - first in a flat in Chiddingstone
Castle, and then in a house which he built himself in a walled garden which was part of the Castle
grounds. (He started with a firm of builders, but found they were lazy so he sacked them and finished
the job himself1)

He discovered Ieys in 194 7 when he read The Old Straight Track by Alfred Watkins, although he had
been interested in such subjects as the Somerset Zodiac previously and had been fortunate enough to
meet its discoverer Mrs. Maltwood when in Canada during the war. He had in fact been born at the
portals of the Zodiac and had gone for walks round the "Girt Dog ofLangport" as a child. It was
imn1ediately after reading Watkins' book in 194 7, however, that he walked across Parliament Hill and
noticed a pine tree and a tumulus. On returning home he found they aligned with Westminster Abbey
and he was immediately hooked on leys. He was to find a number of very interesting alignments after
moving to Kent in 195 5 and, through his linking of them to the flying saucer enigma, brought leys once
again into public attention. Watkins' Straight Track Club had ceased operation some time before and
the alignments had gone into obscurity.

In 195 5 he attended a lecure by DesmondLeslie at the Tunbridge Wells Flying Saucer Club, in which
the experiences of contactee George Adamski were described. He became fascinated with this subject
and was soon the local representative of the International UFO Observer Corps, set up by Flying
Saucer Review. He hated the term UFO, however, and always referred to himself as a "Flying
Saucerologist''. It was during his investigations for the Observer Corps that he noticed that a number
of local sightings had occurred where there were points on his alignments(mainly pine clumps) and
two of them had the word Mark in the place name(Mark Beech and Keston Mark). He began to think
that the saucer crews knew about the leys and this was further corroborated when he read in contactee
Buck Nelson's account that "the places where the magnetic currents cross is comparable to a cross­
roads sign''. The sentence would have been complete without the last word; the addition of the word
''sign'' must mean that visible signs on Earth were involved. Also ''magnetic currents'' indicated that
there was some kind of energy in the leys utilised by the saucers - and indeed later there was found
to be an unknown energy there, which we can pick up by dowsing. He produced the now famous
booklet Skyways and Landmarks, which led eventually to the founding of the earth mysteries
movement as we know it today.

Also a little earler Aime Michel had brought out his book Flying Saucers and the Straight-Line
Mystery, in which he showed that sightings during a particular flap in France in 1952 seemed to fall
on alignments when the ones for each particular day were taken separately. These alignments, like
leys, converged on nodal points or centres where sometimes a cigar-shaped object would be seen, and
at other times discs would descend with a falling-leaf motion. This was further evidence to Tony of
the connection between leys and UFOs, and he believed them to be the same lines. In August 1963
he went on a trip to France and was interested to find pine clumps at some of the points where UFOs
had executed the falling-leaf(which Tony interpreted as going from one line to another at a different
level). There was even a small shrine with a Madonna at one, indicating to Tony that the spot was
recognised as holy ground.

He believed that contact was the way ahead in the flying saucer field, and set himself to reading all he
could of contact accounts. He was worried, however, that the movement was taking an anti-contact
stance. He put an article "Diffusion1ist Theory and the STAR Fellowship" in Flying Saucer Review
to try to redress the balance. The STAR Fellowship was to be an organisation of those who wished
to welcome our visitors fron1 space, and whose members would be identified by the wearing of a badge
containing a white seven-point star on a midnight blue background. It was also to have an educational
role. STAR stood for Space Travel and Research. From 1960, he held a Rally of members of the
Fellowship each year, culminating in one at which contactee Arthur Bryant ofScorriton in Devon gave

He promoted thought communication as a means of contact with the space people, and indeed received
the seven-point star telepathically. He also got a very thought-provoking dissertation on medicine and
music by this method, and his friend Mary Long brought through that the spiral sycamore tree near
his property in Chiddingstone marked a vortex from which twelve lines would radiate linking healing
centres. With his friend Philip Rodgers, a blind musician from Grindleford near Sheffield, he obtained
plans for several free energy machines, one of them based on a picture in contactee Howard Menger' s
book. Some ofPhilip's were said to have worked - a model flying saucer produced a vortex of energy

He made several other discoveries during this period, including some leys crossing the Somerset
Zodiac which he called ''Mother Carey's Chickens'' - the River Carey outlined the Virgo figure, and
crossing it were two leys, one with "cock" names and the other with "hen" names. (Coxley and
Henley are two nearby villages). He also found that Stonehenge seemed to be based on a seven-point
star arrangement; the diagram of this is still in existence.

In 1970 he emigrated to Australia, becoming warden of a youth hostel in a remote valley. This period
of his life can be seen as a consolidation of his former discoveries, for, while he edited a magazine called
The Crow in which he produced articles on the various things he had found out in England, there is
no record that he did any ley or flying saucer work during that time. His articles in The Crow, however,
form a very good archive of his findings. He lived in Australia for ten years until he died in 1980.
