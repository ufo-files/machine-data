                                   THE PHENOMENON


      To the best of my knowledge and belief, no one knows precisely what UFOs
are, where they originate or why they come here. That they do indeed come I take
as axiomatic, bearing in mind the testimony of so many thousands of reliable
witnesses from all over the world. If it is claimed that all these people are
either lying or mistaken, then we must indeed have a psychological problem of
incredible and hitherto unsuspected      dimensions, even less susceptible of
solution than the high strangeness dilemmas posed by the UFOs themselves.

      I am aware that there are people whose emotional and intellectual balance
is so insecure that such reassessment of their cherished and comforting world
picture as an impartial     study of the UFO problem may demand is seen as
unacceptably threatening. Since they already know that UFOs do not exist, they
see no point in considering the evidence impartially and seize         upon any
alternative explanation, however improbable, which leaves intact the familiar
basis of their world. This attitude        they usually like to describe as
"scientific", yet they are not objective scientists but subjective believers,
men of such faith as those who refused to look through Galileo's glass because
they already knew from Aristotle that our earth was the centre of the universe
and that it did not move. These words are not for them; they are for any who are
willing to consider carefully and without prejudice such evidence as we have.

      It must be admitted that this evidence is very far from being the kind of
evidence we would like, but it is all we have and we must do with it the best
we can. All our information is undeniably anecdotal or hearsay, yet in everyday
life we do not invariably disvalue or discount such knowledge. Some time ago my
wife acquired at an exhibition in V ancouver two enamelled vases which purported
to come from China. Indeed, there was a whole pavilion filled with goods and
salespeople who were all said to have come from China. Thousands of people at
that exhibition accepted this claim without demur, purely on hearsay evidence
none of them had ever tested empirically. There is nothing either stupid or
unscientific about this, for one understands that what is being accepted is not
proven truth, but simply a hypothesis that China exists and is the source of
such goods and people.

      Hypotheses are, by definition, merely provisional explanatory paradigms
which can be· ranked in order of probability      largely on the strength df
supporting evidence. The empirical test of a journey to China is unavailable to
most of us, but we   accept the testimony of those who claim to have made it
because    the weight of supporting evidence increases the value of its
probability rating to virtual certainty. The probability that landed UFOs and
entities associated with them have interacted with humans is certainly less, but
must still be estimated as well above zero by any who have assessed the evidence
impartially.

            Meaningful research into the nature of UFO phenomena can best be
conducted on this basis, namely the evaluation of probabilities in such a way
that, while no hypothesis is excluded     from consideration, neither is any
regarded as having achieved validation. This is the essence of the scientific
method and hence the basis for the claim that this book represents a genuinely
comparative scientific evaluation of UFO phenomena.

      Through careful comparative study of the effects on people exposed to the
proximity of UFOs, James McCampbell (1987) was     able to infer that many and
possibly all UFOs are surrounded by a     complex electromagnetic field and to
specify in considerable   detail the dimensions of its components. In similar
fashion, the    present writer hopes, by consideration of a selection of
significant and well-reported cases, to attempt an elucidation of various more
general questions confronting anyone. �ryi;ng to obtain a coherent view of the
available material. Though there., can be no prospect of finding definitive
answers, there is always    hope of progress through trying to ask the right
questions and    assessing the probability of such tentative answers as may
suggest themselves. In the end each makes his own individual analysis according
to his interpretation of the evidence available.

      We should first, perhaps, consider the epistemological question of how far
we can rely on the validity of the sense data witnesses receive from UFOs and
their occupants; whether the fields they project may be not only physical but
also psychic, insofar as there is evidence that UFO entities are often able to
control the perceptions of humans they encounter. McCampbell (ibid.) has offered
a convincing explanation of the physical        paralysis they have frequently
inflicted, but a comparable and      often simultaneous form of mental control
remains still unexplained. Entities are sometimes perceived as normally human,
at other times as bug-eyed little monsters, robots or blond, blue-eyed demigods.
Are these all varied projectio"ns of a single underlying persona or simply the
four differentiated types     they   appear to be? At the macro level we can
similarly ask whether the multitude of UFO events are merely the epiphenomena
of a single underlying entity, often known as 'The Phenomenon', or as they seem
to be, products of autonomous individuals or groups.

      Ufonauts have often claimed extraterrestrial or1g1ns but         there is
certainly no need to accept uncritically a claim which        may be merely an
avoidance of the question, either because the     true answer might be thought
beyond comprehension by the witness or simply from a desire to mislead. Now that
discoveries in sub-atomic physics have supported the possibility of alternative
universes spatially coexistent with our own planet, the         extraterrestrial
hypothesis (ETH) of UFO origins has certainly     come to seem less plausible,
especially in Europe, though still popular in the USA, where many ufologists
still cherish the hope of grabbing a landed UFO and kicking its tyres to assure
themselves of its objective reality!

      Though the UFO problem in its present form is generally thought of as
dating only from 1947, there are also suggestions that, perhaps in a form more
believable by contemporaries, the Phenomenon has existed throughout rec�Qed
history and even beyond. Some would like to locate its origins in the fabled

in the beginning may have seeded the Earth with mankind and continue to observe

My interest in the Roswell case stems in part from the fact that my father, a retired colonel and former air force

          All things considered, UFO research has become pretty much of a
circus to-day, and the most intriguing and controversial sideshow
skirting the edge is the question of the "silencers", the mysterious
"Men in Black." There is a strong subliminal appeal in these accounts of
visits by mysterious dark-suited figures - (I have been visited myself,

typical situation would be that a witness has a UFO sighting or UFO -

beings who obviously must be directly connected in some way with the

ALIENS, DINOSAURS AND ALLERGIES


Paul Whitehead reviews a book by Albert Budden, a member of the Environmental
Medicine Foundation and a school teacher and lecturer, whose ''Allergies & Aliens "
(Discovery Times Press, ISBN 1 -899071 -00-8) will cause controversy, some of it
fam iliar.


The year was 1 990, and I was on holiday in France, listening to Radio 4 (I believe),
when an interview with Jenny Randles took place. In it, she claimed that close
encounter and alien interaction / abduction expenences were explainable by means of
the Terence Meaden plasma vortex theory. Randles argued that the electromagnetic
field of the vortex influenced our minds in such a way that we imagined meeting with
aliens; ergo, the vortex became a spaceship. Randles added that most if not all past,
present (and presumably future) UFO reports could be attributed to the vortex theory.

"Time to hang up my boots", was my response, until I recalled a BUFORA meeting
Meaden had attended in London a few years earlier, in which he tried to explain away
the "�orn circles" as effects of the vortex. "But what about the more complex circle
patterns," some one asked, in response: to his research which showed that a vortex
might be responsible for a single circle: · To paraphrase Mea den, his response was, "I
am sure my future research will show that the vortex can form the different patterns. "

This, I realised, was not the scientific approach which BUFORA had been looking to
Meaden to provide. Reductionism might nave its benefits (in medicine, for example),
but when it is combined with a leap of faith (or wishful thinking?) it implodes and
drags down its perpetrators with it. Today, Meaden has disappeared from public view
- refusing even to answer phone calls from circles photographer Busty Taylor, and
Jenny Randles has modified her views.

What has this to do with Bud den's book? Read on. One of the books mentioned in his
bibliograph y is a book about electromagnetic pollution co-authored by Simon Best and
another wnter. Best argues that the fields generated by pylons, radar, computer and
household electric equipment, can have adverse affects on us. I am sure he might be
right. But when one of the writers, whom I know, appeared in a photo on the front
page of a magazine, standing beneath electricity cables and holding a lighted light tube
he, like Meaden, was involved in wishful thinking. He told me later tfiat although he
had heard that light tubes would automatically "come on" when held under
pylons / cables, he could not get his to. So, he rigged it up to a battery. Nobody was to
know, were they . . .

I n his own book, Albert Budden argues that allergies are caused b y the same

             r
electromagnetic pollution as mentioned by Best and others. Especially vulnerable are
sensitive peo le living near radio transmitters, electricity pylons, etc. The effects are
heightened i they live on natural energy lines (some might call these 'ley lines') .
According to Budden, the effects are intensified still further if the person hves in a
damp basement flat and has a history of emotional problems or 'child abuse' .

These problems can record themselves into the structure of the room, via repeated
thoughts about them and via the stress they cause the recipient. Budden gives some
fascinating case studies; of a woman who saw a menacing-looking girl sitting on her
bed (which Budden theories is an imprint of her young stressecf-out self); and of a
couple who see a flying goldfish and a dinosaur - these he explains as symbols created
by fhe couple in an attempt to resolve their inner conflicts.

His "most acute" case, as he calls it, is of a 1 3-year-old girl who is visited at night by a
being of light. She hears a voice in her head asking 'What is the power, what is the
power?' . Budden points out that the Alexandra Palace radio transmitter is "outside the
window". Other strange phenomena occur to the girl and to her as an adult, and these
are all attributed to allergies and electromagnetic pollution.

                                           J-i
Budden moves on to cases which are similar to alien abduction and alien physical
examination cases. He believes that modern man, beset by neuroses caused b y various
forms of pollution and our stressful way of life, is visualising alien visitations -
perhaps as a means of mental and emotional escape, or as a way of resolving inner
emotional conflict.

This is an area which seems to demand more research, which would of course benefit
people who write these types of books. It is a fact that we humans are assailed by
pollution (50,000 man-made chemicals in the air, water and food, plus varied amounts
of electromagnetic radiation and also nuclear radiation - and hot air from our political
leaders) .

Yet, it has been stated time and time again that a high proportion of UFO events of all
types take place in remote areas, away from settlements - i.e., away from electromag­
netic radiation; away from much of the pollution from other human activities; and
away from the "space culture" which, Budden theorises, causes his cases to visualise
aliens instead of fairies or pink elephants.

He confronts us with some compelling research findings and theories, and within his
own field of speciality he may be "right". His cases often appear to be genuinely
disturbed people; but are they disturbed because of electromagnetic pollution? Did that
pollution cause their allergies, or did their mental anguish and / or emotional distur­
bances cause their allergies? Do pollution and allergies form a crutch upon which they
stand?

{Interestingly, psychiatric / sociological research has shown that close encounter "vic­
tims" are as sane as the rest of us; they do not seem to share the emotional amd
mental disturbances which are a feature of many of Albert Budden's cases} .

Budden opens up a Pandor a's box o f questions, not least of which concern religious,
mystical and creative experiences. Can these be explained merely by force fields acting
on the brain, or is Budden another single-subject enthusiast who believes his special
subject can be applied to almost everything? Despite some reservations, I recommend
(but not to hypochondriacs) this book (Allergies & Aliens), which is available from
Discovery Times Press, Kew Studio, 270 Sandycombe Rd, Kew, Surrey, TW9 3NP. The
£5 price includes P+P.

Summ ary:- The issue of alien encounters in country and remote areas largely
neutralises the author's theory. The release over recent years of many patients from
(closed) mental hospitals may invalidate other parts of his theory. Finally - I hope
Budden is not "doing a Meaden" and embarking on a leap in the dark as a way
closing the loop in a theory. More research is needed, but by whom?

                              ALillN ACKNOWLEDGEMffiNT

             A recently inalJourated movement holds that there is now sufficient evidence to
    justify an unequivocal statement that aliens not only exist but are in contact with humanity.
    It has therefore inaugurated a campaign to bring this alleged fact to public notice.
    Unfortunately, it is necessary to ask what standards of evidence are being accepted here,
    and on closer examination it is clear that the Alien Acknowledgement Campaign (AA C) is
    rather too readily convinced of what it wishes to believe. Since its campaign is directed at
    comparatively sceptical members of the public who will certainly challenge the evidence
    presented, the ultimate outcome of their efforts is likely to be the precise opposite of what
    they presumably desire.

            The problem really hinges on the definition of 'belief.' At an AAC meeting held in
    Oxford last January, it soon became apparent that what was being demanded of potential
    adherents was a binary either/or attitude to belief. One was required to have an absolute
    belief in the proposition concerning alien presence, or be counted an unbeliever. Now a
    rational approach to the problem of alien presence could only warrant absolute belief in the
    presence of complete 1 00% certainty, of the kind that would prevail if an alien vehicle were
    to land as traditionally expected on the White House lawn, disgorging its occupants, who
    would inevitably demand to be taken to our leader.

             While the kind of evidence we possess with regard to the possibility of aliens
    interacting with our own species is often highly suggestive, none of it has so far reached the
    level of certainty which would justify absolute belief. What is, however, justified is a level
    of provisional or partial belief, conveniently expressible as a percentage. The mistake AAC
    are making is to demand the absolutism of binary logic when the fuzzy logic of relativistn
    would be much more appropriate. This would allow us to distinguish the relative credibility
·   that might reasonably be afforded to a CE3 witnessed by several observers simultaneously
    as against a CE4 with a single subjective witness account. Neither need necessarily be
    totally disbelieved, but equally neither may warrant anything approaching the 1 00% which
    is the AAC's sole alternative to utter disbelief.


              The demand for alien acknowledgment is in fact an unwarranted eaU'" for the
    acceptance of an as yet unvalidated hypothesis. Its supporters would be more profitably
    occupied in joining less credulous ufologists in their continuing search for evidence which
    will either confirm or refute their claim. The scientific method can accomodate even the
    paranormal, for normality is merely a statistical concept, but belief unsupported by tested
    evidence is religious rather than scientific. Ufology should not become a matter of faith.
