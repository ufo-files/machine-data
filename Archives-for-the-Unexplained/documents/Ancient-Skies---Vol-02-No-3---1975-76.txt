SOLVING THE NAZCA MYSTERIES
By ROBERT EARLE*
      Of all the evidence reviewed by Erich von
Daniken in his many books on the subject of ancient
astronauts none impressed me more that did the
seemingly preposterous claim that the hundreds of
miles of "aircraft runway" markings on the Plain of
Nazca in Peru were made of steel (now converted to
iron oxide) . This is the type of evidence, I
thought, which would be verifiable. Indeed, it
would be nothing less than I would expect to find
as the lost vestige of the technology of visitors
from outer space . If they could get here in a
spacecraft, then we should expect far more than
just rock drawings made by Earthmen of visitors they
may or may not have seen. Considering the runways
though, it's pretty stupid to deny the existence of
something that's possible and which cannot be dis-
proved .

     While I could see the logic of the steel run-
ways, I realized that it was only a theory, until
I could find some proof. I had a good idea of what
I was looking for, but I was astounded to find a
full-scale drawing (Figure 2) alongside the type of
runway I've described, but without the 60-yard by
600-yard triangular concourse which can be seen in
the distance in Figure 1. The drawing is of a
spacecraft, 180 feet long in its entirety. It has
two 100-foot long "runners" (to land on the steel
rails); four lift engines (which also apply side-
thrust); and an 80-foot nose, perhaps to provide
distance shielding by separating a nuclear power-
plant from the crew. Surprising (to me) was the
22-foot distance between the runners! This was
fascinating, but I kept the search going for some-
thing else - something really tangible as evidence.    YUGOSLAVIA TO HOST THIRD WORLD CONFERENCE
